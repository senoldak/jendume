Cabinet leadership urged transforming the administration to move beyond a reactive stance, outlining plans for a strategic shift of powers, resources, and accountability to regional and local governments. Pointing out that a significant portion of public servants already operate outside the capital, officials argued that public service effectiveness should no longer be bound by human limits, thereby making the adoption of artificial intelligence essential.

However, this perspective faces critical scrutiny from observers who emphasize that while technology can help flag individuals facing housing risks, it cannot physically provide a home. Maintaining fair outcomes and public trust requires robust administration alongside proper resources. A 2025 study conducted by researchers from Oxford University and Nottingham University indicates that access to elite civil service positions continues to favor individuals from privileged backgrounds, with historical progress in widening access having reversed over recent decades.

The academic paper analyzed the biographical data of 476 public service leaders serving across multiple decades. The findings reveal that many officials advancing to top tiers originated from privileged classes and frequently lacked educational backgrounds in quantitative disciplines, science, technology, engineering, or mathematics. Experts suggest that this cultural divide has historically contributed to significant policy missteps.

Former advisory figures have similarly cautioned that the recruitment pipeline fails to bring in enough scientific expertise to reshape the traditional skillsets found in government administration. As modernization discussions proceed, the integration of computational tools and the social background of leadership remain vital topics for public governance.
