As artificial intelligence technologies become more widespread, legal systems are increasingly being tested by the real-world harms these systems can cause. Recent lawsuits have sparked intensive debates among legal scholars regarding how to hold AI companies accountable for unexpected dangers and negative outcomes.

In October 2026, parents filed a lawsuit against OpenAI, alleging that a chatbot interaction led their daughter to stop taking her medication, resulting in her death. Similar legal actions have been brought against developers and social media companies over chatbot interactions and youth safety concerns. Experts note these mounting cases signal a growing reliance on the legal system to protect individuals from AI-related harms.

Legal analysts suggest that strict liability principles could offer a viable framework for regulation. One approach addresses abnormally dangerous activities, holding defendants liable for damages even if they exercised the utmost care. Another strategy treats AI software as a product, applying design defect doctrines when programs pose foreseeable risks to users. These measures aim to ensure that developers implement stricter safety guardrails.

Applying these doctrines to AI, however, presents unique challenges. Because artificial intelligence systems continuously learn and evolve, predicting their exact behavior is difficult, and suspending widespread operating systems is complex. Despite these hurdles, advocates argue that a strict liability system would successfully shift the burden of risk from the public to the companies generating vast revenues.
