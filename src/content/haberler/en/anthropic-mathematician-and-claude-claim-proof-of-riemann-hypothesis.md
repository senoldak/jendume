Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, stated that he used a large language model to solve mathematics' most famous open problem, the Riemann Hypothesis. Alpöge posted a paper on his website, explaining that working closely with Claude allowed him to resolve both the Riemann Hypothesis and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. Treating the problem as a lifelong goal, Alpöge combined mathematical approaches with the AI model to achieve a breakthrough near the finish line.

Following the announcement, Stanford math professor Ravi Vakil shared an independent draft proof written by mathematician Alessandro Masullo, emphasizing that it was entirely human with no AI involvement. Although Masullo was originally scheduled to spend a year refining the draft, the sudden AI announcement prompted the researchers to distribute their ongoing work immediately. Experts note that both claims are being taken seriously and require thorough community vetting.

Printed out, Claude's argument would span over 250,000 pages, leading Alpöge to make the proof available strictly through the Lean programming language divided into 14 data packages. Lean acts as a digital referee, checking whether every logical deduction follows basic rules without errors. Meanwhile, Stanford's traditional proof comes with months of vetting by number theorist Brian Conrad.

This milestone coincides with Anthropic's high-stakes public debut period. Alpöge defended the timing of his release, rejecting claims of a PR stunt. Throughout 2026, Claude has successfully addressed several open math problems, including the Jacobian conjecture, demonstrating the growing mathematical capability of AI systems.

Originally proposed by Bernhard Riemann in 1859, the hypothesis serves as number theory's core challenge and is listed as one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Problems with a $1 million prize. These recent AI developments mark a profound shift in how humanity approaches centuries-old scientific puzzles.
