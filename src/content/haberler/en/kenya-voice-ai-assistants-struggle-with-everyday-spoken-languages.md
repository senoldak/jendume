Voice artificial intelligence technologies perform well on prepared text, yet they face significant barriers in everyday conversational speech. A preprint report published in March 2026 highlights a 3.24 percent word-error rate on volunteer-recorded speech datasets. However, people in Kenya rarely speak in formal scripts, instead shifting between languages, using neighborhood slang, and dealing with background noise. This spontaneous audio exposes a clear generalization gap in current scaling laws built primarily on the English language. As voice becomes a primary interface in a country ranking first globally in AI usage, the inability to parse everyday speech excludes everyday users.

Experts emphasize the need to collect unscripted conversational data to capture natural nuances and code-switching. Although ongoing projects supported by external grants have gathered thousands of hours of audio, mere hours do not guarantee true coverage of regional variations and accents. Complex grammar, inconsistent spellings of borrowed words, and rapidly evolving slang further complicate model training. For developers building voice tools in Kenya, the priorities remain centered on gathering conversational speech and adapting to linguistic diversity.

Various research teams continue to record unscripted dialogue and build pronunciation dictionaries to bridge the gap between written scripts and spoken variations. Addressing background noise and overlapping languages without stripping out crucial phonetic details remains a central engineering challenge. Ultimately, researchers argue that voice systems must be evaluated against real-world conversational conditions rather than relying solely on high-level benchmark scores.
