How fast does unemployment return to its old level after a shock? Monthly data from the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) give two very different answers to two separate shocks. The OECD-wide unemployment rate was 5.8% in April 2008. It rose to 8.7% in January 2010 and returned to 5.8% only in September 2017. A rate of 5.3% in February 2020 rose to 8.9% in April 2020 and was back at 5.3% in December 2021.

In the first episode the rate took twenty-one months to reach its peak and came back to its old level seven years and eight months after the peak. In the second it took two months to reach the peak and came back twenty months after it. In this piece we put the two episodes side by side with the same yardsticks and read them in time order: first the global financial crisis period, then the pandemic period.

## How did we set up the comparison?

To compare two episodes, three things had to be decided: where to start, what the peak is, and what the old level means.

As the start, we chose a reference month in each episode: April 2008 for the first and February 2020 for the second. The rate in the reference month is, for us, the old level. The peak is the highest monthly value of the episode in the OECD-wide series: January 2010 and April 2020. The rise from reference to peak is 2.9 points in the first episode and 3.6 points in the second. So the second episode's peak is higher.

We took the starting level, not the height of the peak, as the yardstick because the two episodes do not start from the same point. The reference of the first is 5.8%, of the second 5.3%. Reading each episode against its own starting level, rather than against one common percentage, makes for a fairer comparison.

To define the return to the old level, we looked for the first month after the peak in which the rate was equal to or lower than the reference value. Rates were compared at one decimal. In the first episode that month is September 2017; in August 2017 the rate was 5.9%. In the second it is December 2021; in November 2021 the rate was 5.5%.

## First episode: a short climb, a long decline

Between April 2008 and January 2010 the rate went from 5.8% to 8.7%. The climb took twenty-one months and totalled 2.9 points.

After the peak the decline was slow. The rate was 8.5% in July 2010, 8.2% in January 2011, 8.1% in July 2011 and 8.0% in January 2012. Two years after the peak the rate was still 2.2 points above its starting level.

Then the decline stopped. The rate rose to 8.1% in July 2012 and to 8.3% in January 2013. Three years after the peak it stood 2.5 points above its starting level, a gap larger than the 2.2 points of January 2012.

By January 2014 the rate had fallen to 7.7%, and by January 2015 to 7.1%. So even five years after the peak the rate was 1.3 points above its starting level. The return to the starting level, 5.8%, came in September 2017.

## Second episode: a jump in two months, a return in twenty

Start with February 2020: the OECD-wide rate was 5.3%. In April 2020 it was 8.9%. The rise is 3.6 points and took only two months. The first episode's rise of 2.9 points had taken twenty-one months.

Decline also began at a different speed. The rate fell to 7.1% in October 2020: 1.8 points above its starting level. In April 2021 it was 6.8%, 1.5 points above. In October 2021 it fell to 5.6%, a gap of only 0.3 points. In December 2021 the rate was 5.3% and back at its starting level.

The episodes can also be compared at the same distances from the peak. Six months after the peak the rate was 2.7 points above its starting level in the first episode and 1.8 points above in the second. One year later the gaps fell to 2.4 points and 1.5 points. Eighteen months after the peak the gap was 2.3 points in the first episode and only 0.3 points in the second. At every distance, the second episode was closer to its starting level.

After the return the rate went below the reference. In April 2022 it was 5.0%, 0.3 points below its starting level. It was 5.0% in October 2022, 4.8% in April 2023, and 4.9% in April 2024 and in April 2025. Two years after the peak the rate was below the reference, and it stayed below it over the next three years.

As of August 2026 the rate is 5.0%, which is 0.3 points below the 5.3% of February 2020.

## The same aggregate, different countries

An aggregate figure should not be read as one average; it combines the movements of individual countries. An eight-row table shows this. For each country we computed the rise from the reference month to the highest monthly value inside the episode's window. The first window runs from May 2008 to December 2011, the second from March 2020 to December 2021.

In the United States the rate rose from 5.0% to 10.0% in the first episode: 5.0 points. In the second it rose from 3.5% to 14.8%: 11.3 points. In Canada the rise was 2.6 points in the first episode and 8.4 points in the second. The second episode's rise is 126% larger than the first in the United States and 223% larger in Canada.

Türkiye's first-episode rise is 4.8 points, against 2.9 points in the OECD-wide aggregate. Other countries look different. In Japan the rise was 1.6 points in the first episode and 0.7 points in the second. In France, 2.3 and 1.2 points. In Türkiye, 4.8 and 1.5 points. In Korea, 1.6 and 1.2 points. Australia looks more like the United States: 1.6 points in the first episode, 2.3 points in the second.

How large a rise is and how high the rate stands are separate things. In France the rate rose from 7.9% to 9.1% in the second episode, in Japan from 2.4% to 3.1%. Because levels differ so much across countries, comparing rises directly needs care; we compare each country only with its own reference.

Rises of 2.9 and 3.6 points in the aggregate are a blend of these movements. The difference in the aggregate, the higher peak of the second episode, is consistent with the large rises in the United States and Canada in this table; but it does not come from two countries alone, and we did not decompose the aggregate by country.

## Where do countries stand against the reference in August 2026?

One last question: in the last month common to the series, where do rates stand against the reference level of the second episode, February 2020? In all eight rows we selected the data run to August 2026, and we answer with that month.

OECD-wide, the rate is 5.0%, 0.3 points lower than in February 2020. Against the level of April 2008, the first episode's reference, it is 0.8 points lower. In the United States it is 4.1%, 0.6 points above February 2020. In Canada it is 6.4%, again 0.6 points above. In Japan it is 2.5%, 0.1 points above. In France it is 8.2%, 0.3 points above. In Korea it is 2.7%, 0.7 points lower than in February 2020. In Australia it is 4.7%, 0.4 points lower than in February 2020.

Four of the eight rows show a rate above its level of February 2020: the United States, Canada, Japan and France. In three it is lower: Korea, Australia and Türkiye. The OECD-wide rate is lower too. In the United States the rate was 14.8% in April 2020 and is 4.1% in August 2026; in Canada it was 14.2% in May 2020 and 6.4% in August 2026.

Türkiye's rate fell from 12.6% in February 2020 to 7.8% in August 2026: 4.8 points; in the OECD-wide aggregate the rate fell 0.3 points. We report only the measure here; this series cannot tell us the reason for the difference.

## A counter-reading: what does the OECD-wide figure hide?

An aggregate need not peak at the same time as the groups inside it. One example is the euro area (EA) aggregate, which the OECD publishes separately. In that aggregate the rate was 7.4% in April 2008 and reached its highest value between May 2008 and December 2014, 12.3%, in February 2013. The rise is 4.9 points. The OECD-wide peak was in January 2010. So the peak dates of the two aggregates are more than three years apart.

During the second episode the euro-area rate rose from 7.3% in February 2020 to 8.6% in August 2020, a rise of 1.3 points. In August 2026 it is 6.4%, 0.9 points lower than in February 2020.

Second comes the definition of the rate. The unemployment rate is the share of the labour force that is unemployed. People who stop looking for work and leave the labour force also leave the denominator, so when participation changes the rate can move differently from employment. These data do not show participation; we cannot explain the difference between the two episodes through it.

Third, a reading on the cause of the difference between the episodes. This series shows only the path the rate took; we do not attach the difference in speed to a cause, and this piece puts none forward.

Fourth, the windows. We took the peak as the highest monthly value in the episode. Some countries may peak near the end of a window, and a longer window would have enlarged the rise in some countries. The euro-area example shows this.

## Measurement limits: monthly, adjusted and estimated

Our series is the OECD's monthly unemployment rate set. The rate is computed for the population aged 15 and over and is adjusted for seasonal and calendar effects. The OECD flags the observations of the aggregate series as estimated values.

The OECD flags a time series break for Türkiye in December 2013. Some of the Türkiye values fall before the break and some after it, so we do not read the rises of the two episodes for Türkiye as one uninterrupted series.

There are two further limits. First, monthly values can be revised afterwards; the figures here were retrieved in October 2026. Second, the OECD-wide series has no observation for October 2025; we used no value affected by that month.

Releases from national statistical offices may show values different from this OECD series; we used only the OECD series.

Put the two episodes side by side and what shows is this: an aggregate can return to the same level by very different paths. In the first episode the rate waited seven years and eight months after its peak to return to its starting level, in the second twenty months. This comparison describes the path of the two episodes; it does not say which episode was better or worse.

## How we calculated

- We pulled the monthly unemployment rate series from the OECD's monthly unemployment rates data flow: the share of the labour force that is unemployed, aged 15 and over, women and men together, adjusted for seasonal and calendar effects. We used the same query for the countries and for the OECD-wide aggregate.
- The reference month is April 2008 for the first episode and February 2020 for the second. We rounded values to one decimal.
- In the OECD-wide aggregate the peak is the highest monthly value of the series in the episode: January 2010 and April 2020. For countries the peak is the highest value in the window from May 2008 to December 2011 for the first episode and from March 2020 to December 2021 for the second. For the euro area the first window runs from May 2008 to December 2014.
- A rise was found by subtracting the reference value from the peak value. The differences after the peak were found by subtracting the reference value from the values six, twelve, eighteen, twenty-four, thirty, thirty-six, forty-eight and sixty months after the peak month.
- The month of return to the old level is the first month after the peak in which the rounded rate is equal to or lower than the reference.
- The month counts (twenty-one, two, twenty, seven years and eight months) are calendar differences between these dates.
- Country choice: not every row of the table, but eight rows from different regions and different paths. The rows are the United States, Canada, Japan, France, Türkiye, Korea, Australia and the OECD-wide aggregate.
