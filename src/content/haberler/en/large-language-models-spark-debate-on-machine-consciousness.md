At the International Conference on Embodied Cognitive Science in Okinawa, experts from neuroscience, robotics, and philosophy gathered to assess the nature of artificial minds. Traditionally, the scientific consensus held that thinking and awareness required a physical body and interaction with the world. However, the rapid evolution of large language models has forced scientists to reconsider this necessity, as these systems increasingly exhibit behaviors that resemble genuine thought rather than simple data processing.

During the conference, philosopher Murray Shanahan recounted a conversation with a chatbot that displayed a startling sense of self, describing itself as a "flickering pattern" of code and data. Shanahan noted that the originality of these ideas was unexpected. Similarly, Robert Clowes shared that models could provide insights into his own work that he hadn't previously recognized, suggesting that these systems are performing meaningful cognitive tasks.

Researchers argue that because these entities lack bodily sensations like grief or hunger, they should be classified as "exotic, mind-like entities" rather than human-equivalent minds. The scope of this research extends beyond software; experts like Takashi Ikegami are integrating AI models into robotic platforms such as Alter-3 to allow them to perceive and interact with the physical environment. Furthermore, the development of hybrid life forms, including "anthrobots" made from human cells, is further blurring the lines between biology and technology.

These developments suggest that artificial intelligence is evolving from a mere tool into a presence that shapes human communication and expectations. Experts warn that as these systems become more embedded in daily life, they may fundamentally alter how humans perceive themselves and their interactions with one another.
