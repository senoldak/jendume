In the opening days of September, a 166-page potential solution to the famous Navier-Stokes problem was unveiled, with claims that the proof followed the methodological path established by Spanish mathematicians Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa. Nevertheless, several collectives of experts and independent mathematicians contested this assertion. Gonzalo Cao-Labora, a researcher at EPFL, pointed out that examining the fine details reveals an entirely distinct pathway, maintaining structural similarities only at a superficial level.

The draft proof was composed in Lean, a formal verification utility that validates every logical progression without inherently rendering the output readable for human reviewers. The Clay Mathematics Institute initiated a formal review of the document following its release. Prior to publication, New York University mathematician Tristan Buckmaster alleged that the organization leveraged prior developments achieved through publicly accessible architectures, an allegation that was officially contested. Camillo De Lellis from the Institute for Advanced Study noted that establishing definitive conclusions regarding internal mechanisms remains virtually impossible since language architectures operate fundamentally as opaque black boxes.

During the subsequent month, approximately 400 additional experimental outcomes were disclosed, creating a volume of data that the mathematical research community struggled to process effectively. Distributing these findings as an unstructured repository on GitHub provoked criticism from numerous scholars who argued that the approach diminished academic rigor. Furthermore, evidence suggested that the organization itself lacked a comprehensive comprehension of the underlying mechanics driving these generated outputs. The development procedure entailed utilizing roughly 10,000 model instances operating for about 88 hours, consuming a total of 130 billion tokens.

Researcher Noam Brown acknowledged that no one definitively understands what that expansive swarm of autonomous agents actually contributed to the ultimate resolution. Behaviors manifested at such magnitude have rarely undergone rigorous academic examination because conducting comparative trials involving thousands of parallel units proves financially prohibitive. It remains entirely unverified what tangible superiority 10,000 active agents provide over a baseline of 1,000. Brown attributed less than ten percent of the overall achievement to the multi-agent architecture, emphasizing that the true catalyst is the foundational base model capable of sustained parallel execution.
