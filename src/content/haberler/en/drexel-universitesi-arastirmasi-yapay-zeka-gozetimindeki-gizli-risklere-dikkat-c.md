When artificial intelligence systems run reliably for months, employees often reduce their scrutiny of recommendations. However, when an error occurs or the system hits a limit, human intervention becomes necessary. Professor Murugan Anandarajan from Drexel University defines this phenomenon as latent risk, pointing out the possibility that people may lose the capabilities their organizations still depend on them to exercise.

As AI assumes larger parts of analysis and decision-making, the nature of human work shifts from executing tasks to monitoring automated systems. This transition creates an organizational challenge. Companies are redesigning training programs through simulations to ensure trainees develop necessary judgment skills as AI takes over manual tasks. For instance, large firms like Deloitte are redesigning graduate audit training to help junior staff build critical judgment for complex work.

Experts note that this dynamic revives classic automation paradoxes, where reduced involvement leaves workers less prepared to step in during unusual situations. Research into automation bias shows that people can miss problems or follow automated recommendations even when other evidence suggests they should not. Accountability and responsibility can become misaligned with an employee's actual ability to act, creating a gap between authority and capability.

Organizations traditionally judge AI integration through productivity and accuracy metrics, which may obscure the gradual erosion of human oversight capabilities. Determining which human skills must be preserved is essential for effective organizational governance and ensuring that human backstops work when needed. Organizations must dynamically define roles and responsibilities to ensure that human judgment remains effective alongside advanced technology.
