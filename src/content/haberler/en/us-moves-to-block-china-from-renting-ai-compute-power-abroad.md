The US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) has introduced an interim rule designed to block Chinese entities from accessing advanced AI chips via foreign cloud providers. Under the new directive, data center operators in Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, and Brazil must obtain a US government license before purchasing high-end chips from manufacturers such as Nvidia. Applications for these licenses will be evaluated on a case-by-case basis.

The regulatory shift aims to close a legal workaround that permitted Chinese companies to bypass physical export controls by renting compute capacity remotely from foreign data centers. Subsidiaries of US-headquartered corporations and data centers dedicated solely to commercial recommendation systems for retail or social media platforms are exempt from the licensing requirement. Following the announcement, Nvidia shares dropped as much as 2.9 percent, while the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell up to 5.5 percent.

The policy move comes amid heightened political scrutiny sparked by the sudden resignation of Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, following disclosures of an affair with a former manager at Singapore-based Megaspeed International. US authorities are investigating Megaspeed for potential export control breaches after restricted Nvidia chips were traced to a linked data center in China.

Simultaneously, an investigation based on more than 300 Chinese papers and documents revealed China's extensive reliance on model distillation to mitigate hardware shortages. Chinese developers systematically query advanced US teacher models to extract responses and code, using the data to train smaller, cost-effective local student models. Institutions including Shanghai Jiao Tong University and Alibaba have reportedly utilized outputs from OpenAI's models to power domestic AI development, drawing warnings of potential intellectual property sanctions from US officials.
