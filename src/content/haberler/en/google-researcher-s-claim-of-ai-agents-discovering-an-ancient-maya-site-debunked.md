Google AI researcher Pietro Miotti recently claimed that his autonomous AI agents discovered a previously unrecorded Maya site buried deep beneath the Mexican jungle canopy by analyzing NASA lidar data. Miotti announced the finding in a blog post, claiming the agents went from a random thought to receiving an official reply from the Mexican government in under 72 hours, touting it as a major breakthrough for scientific research.

While the claim went viral on social media and picked up coverage from technology news outlets, the scientific community quickly disputed the assertion. Archaeologist Sarah Parcak noted that the location had already been thoroughly studied and published by Dr. Francisco Estrada-Belli of Tulane University. Estrada-Belli's team had analyzed NASA Goddard Space Flight Center lidar data covering 95,000 square kilometers in a 2025 study, which provided key insights into the ancient Maya population.

Following the pushback from experts, Miotti issued an update conceding that the site had indeed already been mapped. He explained that his AI agents failed to find the relevant academic literature during their search, effectively leading to a "rediscovery" rather than a new find. Geoscience experts highlighted that machine learning and computer vision tools have been standard components of remote sensing for decades, criticizing hyperbolic claims that diminish expert labor.

This occurrence reflects a broader pattern within the AI industry, where automated systems generate unverified or plagiarized claims across academic fields like mathematics and archaeology. The controversy demonstrates that AI outputs cannot replace rigorous peer review and academic validation, serving as a reminder of the limitations inherent in current autonomous research agents.
