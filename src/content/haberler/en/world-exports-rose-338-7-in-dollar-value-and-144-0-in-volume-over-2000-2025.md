World exports of goods and services were worth 7,953 billion dollars in current dollars in 2000. In 2025, it had reached 34,891 billion dollars, a rise of 338.7%. The series that measures the same exports at 2015 prices went from 11,613 billion dollars to 28,331 billion dollars, a rise of 144.0%. The two figures look at the same world and the same period, and the distance between them is large.

Both measures are right. One is a total written at the prices of each year; the other is a total with the change in prices stripped out. The first tells us how much money the goods and services crossing borders were worth in dollars, the second how many goods and services crossed. So the distance between them is less about the size of exports than about how the ratio of the current and constant-price series changed.

We set the two series side by side year by year: in which years they fell together, in which they parted, how much of the dollar rise corresponds to volume, and how the same split looks across countries. We do not go into the causes of the divergence; we only show what the two measures did.

## Two measures, one question

Our data come from the World Bank's goods-and-services export series in the World Development Indicators (WDI) database. The sources are official statistics and national accounts of the countries, and the world total is compiled by filling in missing country data. The series covers exports of services as well as goods; exports of services are services provided by residents of one economy to non-residents. The current-dollar series shows each year at the prices of that year, in US dollars. The constant-price series adjusts the same exports for price changes and expresses the values at prices of the year 2015.

Both series give the same value in 2015, which is a definition and not a coincidence: in that year both are 21,273 billion dollars. When we divide the current-dollar value by the constant-price value we get a ratio, which we call the price ratio. All the ratios and changes in this piece are our own calculations; the source does not publish them ready-made. The price ratio is 1.00 in 2015. It is not an official price index; it is simply one series divided by the other. When the ratio rises, the current-dollar value has grown by more than the constant-price value.

The first chart shows how the position of the two curves relative to each other changes over the years. In 2006, the current value was 14,867 billion dollars and the constant-price value 16,019 billion dollars; in 2007, 17,312 billion dollars and 17,085 billion dollars. So the current curve moved above the constant-price curve that year. In 2016, it went below again (20,878 billion dollars and 21,816 billion dollars), and in 2017, it moved above once more (22,995 billion dollars and 22,944 billion dollars). The years in which the curves cross are the years in which the price ratio passes through 1.00.

## 2000–2008: value outran volume

At 0.68 in 2000, the price ratio meant the current-dollar value was only 68% of the value of the same exports at 2015 prices. The current value reached 12,927 billion dollars in 2005, and 19,760 billion dollars in 2008, while the constant-price value in the same years was 14,765 billion dollars and 17,592 billion dollars. The price ratio was 0.88 in 2005, 1.01 in 2007 and 1.12 in 2008.

Even the start of this period saw the two measures move in opposite directions. In 2001, the current-dollar value fell by 3.1%; volume rose by 0.7%. In 2004, the current value grew by 21.4% and volume by 10.2%. In 2008, the current value rose by 14.1% and volume by only 3.0%. Except in 2001, in every year up to the year 2008, the rise in the current value was larger than the rise in volume.

## 2009–2019: a fall, a rebound and a retreating price ratio

Falls came first in 2009, when the current-dollar value fell by 19.2% and volume by 9.6%. The fall in the current value was twice that in volume. The price ratio went from 1.12 to 1.00; so in that year the current value fell by more than the constant-price value.

Recovery came in 2010: the current value rose by 18.9%, volume by 11.85%, and the price ratio was 1.07. In 2011 the current value grew by 18.4% while volume grew by only 5.5%; the price ratio climbed to 1.20. The ratio was 1.18 in 2012 and in 2013, and 1.16 in 2014. In 2014, the current value was 23,860 billion dollars and the constant-price value 20,583 billion dollars.

Then the price ratio retreated. In 2015 the current-dollar value fell by 10.8%; volume rose by 3.4%. The ratio fell to 1.00 by definition. In 2016 the current value fell by 1.9%; volume rose by 2.6%, and the ratio was 0.96. In 2017 the current value rose by 10.1% and volume by 5.2%; the ratio was 1.00 again. In 2018, rises of 9.6% and 4.1% came with a ratio of 1.06. In 2019 the current value fell by 1.6%; volume rose by 1.3%, and the ratio was 1.03.

Over 2001–2025 there are five years in which the current-dollar value fell while volume rose: in 2001, in 2015, in 2016, in 2019 and in 2023. Three of the five fall in 2009–2019. Exports read in dollars seem to shrink in these years, while the volume measure shows growth.

## 2020–2025: a second fall and a renewed rise in the price ratio

In 2020 the two measures fell very close together: the current value fell by 9.4% while volume fell by 8.51%. The price ratio was 1.03 in 2019 and 1.02 in 2020, almost unchanged. The chart below shows the annual change in volume over 2018–2025; in this period volume fell only in 2020. Before that it had fallen only in 2009.

After falling to 22,486 billion dollars in 2020, the current value reached 28,117 billion dollars in 2021, and 31,601 billion dollars in 2022. In 2021 the current value rose by 25.0% and volume by 9.98%; in 2022 by 12.4% and 7.04%. The price ratio was 1.15 in 2021 and 1.21 in 2022.

Then, in 2023, the current value fell by 1.2%; volume rose by 0.76%. The current value rose by 4.0% in 2024 and volume by 2.87%; in 2025 by 7.5% and 4.88%. The price ratio was 1.19, 1.20 and 1.23 over 2023–2025. In 2025, the current value is 34,891 billion dollars and volume 28,331 billion dollars.

## How much of the dollar rise is volume?

Over 2000–2025 the current-dollar value of world exports rose by 26,938 billion dollars. Over the same period the value measured at 2015 prices rose by 16,718 billion dollars. The 10,220 billion dollars between the two is what remains when the constant-price rise is subtracted from the current-dollar rise; this piece does not break the remainder into components. As shares: 62.1% of the rise is volume and 37.9% is the remainder.

This is a decomposition calculation done only on the 2015 base; we did not repeat it with another base year, so we say nothing about how sensitive the shares are to the base year. The price ratio was 0.68 in 2000 and 1.23 in 2025. On this base, 62.1% of the rise corresponds to volume and 37.9% to what is left outside volume.

## Two rises by country

For some countries the value in 2025 is not yet available, so we compare countries over 2000–2024. For the world total over this period the current-dollar value rose by 308% and volume by 133%; that is, the current value rose to 4.1 times and volume to 2.3 times.

Exports in Türkiye rose from 55 billion dollars to 375 billion dollars in current dollars, a rise of 582%, and from 81 billion dollars to 312 billion dollars at 2015 prices, a rise of 285%. As multiples that is 6.8 and 3.9. Among the seven countries we selected, the largest rise in current dollars is in India (1,261%) and the smallest in Japan (77%); the largest rise in volume is again in India (702%) and the smallest in the United States (99%). In Germany the figures are 232% and 115%, in India 1,261% and 702%, in Brazil 485% and 169%, in the United States 193% and 99%. In South Korea, by contrast, the rise in volume (347%) is above the rise in current dollars (324%). Japan is the same: 116% in volume and 77% in current dollars.

This difference relates to the ratio of the current and constant-price series, which differs from country to country. Türkiye's current value in 2000 was below its constant-price value (55 billion dollars and 81 billion dollars); in 2024 it was above (375 billion dollars and 312 billion dollars). South Korea's constant-price value was above its current value in 2024, at 858 billion dollars against 832 billion dollars, and so was Japan's, at 929 billion dollars against 921 billion dollars. Here we only report the two measures; we do not explain the course of prices or exchange rates in these countries.

## Constant-price dollars are a calculation too

Now the counter-reading: the volume series is not an observed quantity but the result of a calculation. It is obtained by removing the change in prices from the current value, and this removal relies on the national accounts of the countries. Methods may differ from country to country. In other words, the constant-price series is the output of a calculation, not a direct measurement. Second, choosing 2015 as the base year is what makes the price ratio exactly 1.00 in 2015; had another base year been chosen, the constant-price series would sit at a different level and the ratio would be 1.00 in another year.

Third, any series read in US dollars can also be affected by the value of national currencies against the dollar. This piece does not separate out exchange rates; it uses the constant-price series as published.

## The limits of measurement

According to the World Bank, export data are compiled from customs records and the balance of payments. Although balance of payments data are relatively reliable for cross-border transactions, they may not fully follow the definitions of valuation and timing. So the levels in the two series should be read as the values the compilation gives, not as exact values. Because we rounded values to whole billions of dollars, percentage changes calculated from the source's raw values could differ slightly in the last digit.

Because the world total is calculated by filling in missing country data, an update to a single country's value can move the total slightly. All values in this piece come from the version of the World Bank database published in October 2026; they may change in later versions. As the value in 2025 is the newest observation, it is more likely to be updated later, and we cannot measure that.

## How we calculated

- We downloaded the NE.EXP.GNFS.CD (current US dollars) and NE.EXP.GNFS.KD (constant 2015 US dollars) series from the World Bank WDI for the world total every year over 2000–2025; for countries we took the values in 2000 and in 2024. We converted values to billion dollars and rounded them to whole numbers.
- Annual change: the percentage change of a year's value against the previous year, for both the current and the constant-price series.
- Price ratio: for the same year we divided the current value by the constant-price value, written to two decimal places.
- Decomposition of the rise: we split the current-value rise over 2000–2025 into the rise of the constant-price value over the same period and the remainder; the remainder was found by subtracting the constant-price rise from the current rise. We expressed the shares as a proportion of the current rise.
- Country comparison: for each country we calculated the percentage change between its values in 2000 and in 2024, separately for the two series. We left out countries whose constant-price series is incomplete.
- All calculations were made on record values.
