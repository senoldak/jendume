The Allen Institute for Artificial Intelligence released OlmoEarth v1.3 in October 2026, introducing a satellite map embedding model and a browser-based map-making tool. The architecture condenses twelve months of orbital observations into numerical representations for every ten-by-ten-meter ground section. The system processes radar and optical data originating from missions like Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, and Landsat to build comprehensive planetary datasets. This unified framework enables researchers to analyze large volumes of geospatial information without manual alignment.

These numerical identifiers capture distinctive patterns that assist applications in identifying crops, forests, and land surfaces. Through the newly deployed explorer application, operators can examine temporal shifts and geographic data directly inside web browsers without authentication barriers. Teams can evaluate ideas rapidly without dedicating extensive graphics processing unit infrastructure to initial experiments. Initial passes across regional data require mere seconds, significantly accelerating preliminary research phases.

Regarding computational efficiency, version 1.3 represents the most economical variant developed by the organization thus far. Training required 53% of the computational duration consumed by its immediate predecessor and 18% of the initial edition, while generating 50% and 22% of the respective carbon footprints. This balance preserves fine-tuning capabilities for advanced academic and partner laboratories while keeping resource demands exceptionally low compared to earlier iterations.

Practical deployment scenarios include tracking unauthorized gold mining operations and identifying agricultural expansion patterns. By analyzing historical pixel variations across consecutive years without requiring specialized model training, analysts can directly quantify environmental shifts and measure conservation outcomes. These capabilities provide vital insights into how human activities impact sensitive ecosystems across the globe on a continuous basis.
