According to the modeled estimates of the International Labor Organization (ILO), 25.8% of the world's workers were employed in agriculture in 2025. In 1991, the share was 42.8%. So over thirty-four years the share of agriculture fell by 17.1 points.

On the same estimates, the share of workers in services rose from 35.4% to 50.6%, a gain of 15.2 points. The share of industry went from 21.8% to 23.7%, a rise of 1.9 points. At the world level, the shares moved less toward the factory, as the textbooks have it, than toward services.

Here a “share” means how many of every hundred workers are employed in a given sector. It does not say how many people work or who changed jobs; it shows only the distribution. The data come from the World Bank's World Development Indicators (WDI) database, which compiles these series from ILO modeled estimates. Every value in this article is such an estimate, and the latest years may include projections. We look at how the shares sped up and slowed down over time, where the change was concentrated, and what the data do not allow us to say.

## In 1991, agriculture was the largest source of jobs

Start with the baseline. In 1991, 42.8% of the world's workers were in agriculture, 35.4% in services and 21.8% in industry. The three shares add up to one hundred percent every year: when one rises, the other two must fall by the same amount in total.

This is how the World Bank draws the three sectors. Agriculture covers crop and livestock production as well as hunting, forestry and fishing. Industry covers mining and quarrying, manufacturing, construction and public utilities (electricity, gas and water). Services cover everything else: trade, restaurants and hotels, transport, storage and communications, finance, insurance, real estate and business services, and community, social and personal services. A “worker” is a person of working age engaged in an activity to produce goods or provide services for pay or profit.

A single world figure averages very different regions. In the same year, 63.15% of workers in South Asia and 60.23% in Sub-Saharan Africa were in agriculture, while in North America the share was 2.99%. By income group, the share was 66.74% in low-income countries and 8.19% in high-income countries. The world average sits between these extremes and does not describe daily life in any single region.

## Services have been ahead of agriculture since 2001

Through the nineties the share of services rose gradually: it reached 38.0% in 1995 and 39.3% in 2000. Over the same years the share of agriculture went from 40.57% to 39.35%. In 1999, services were ahead, 39.58% against 39.50%; in 2000, agriculture was 0.06 points ahead of services, so the two shares were almost equal. In 2001, services moved ahead for good: 40.2% against 39.0%, a gap of 1.2 points, and since 2001, services have been ahead in every year.

After that the gap widened. In 2010, services stood at 44.6% and agriculture at 32.6%. In 2019, services reached 50.2% and agriculture 26.3%. Industry's share changed more slowly: from 21.8% in 1991 it was 21.4% in 2000, 22.9% in 2010, 23.3% in 2020 and 23.7% in 2025. Industry gained 1.9 points over 1991–2025; services gained 15.2 points.

## Five-year windows show an uneven pace

A single figure for the whole fall can mislead, because the shift did not follow a straight line. Splitting the fall in agriculture into five-year windows shows three periods.

| Period | Fall in agriculture's share, points |
|---|---|
| 1991–1995 | 2.25 |
| 1995–2000 | 1.22 |
| 2000–2005 | 3.08 |
| 2005–2010 | 3.71 |
| 2010–2015 | 4.02 |
| 2015–2020 | 1.35 |
| 2020–2025 | 1.43 |

*World; difference between the start and end of each window in agriculture's share of total employment, percentage points. Source: World Bank, ILO modeled estimates; our calculation.*

During the first half of the nineties the fall was 2.25 points, and in the second half 1.22 points. Across 2000–2015, each five-year window saw a fall of between 3 and 4 points: 3.08, 3.71 and 4.02 points. Then the pace slowed markedly: 1.35 points in 2015–2020 and 1.43 points in 2020–2025. Each of the last two windows is less than half of the 2010–2015 window.

One detail in 2019–2020 sits inside this slowdown. In 2019, agriculture's share was 26.3%; in 2020, it rose to 27.2%, an increase of 0.9 points. In the same year, the share of services fell by 0.7 points. This coincides with the pandemic period (2020–2021); we cannot say from this series why it happened, only that two things occurred in the same year. In 2015–2019 the fall was 2.26 points, and in 2019–2025 only 0.52 points.

## Where did the shift concentrate?

Regions and income groups are mixed inside the world average. Below we look at six World Bank regional aggregates; the results cover only these six regions. In all six regions the agricultural share fell over 1991–2025, but at very different speeds.

East Asia and Pacific saw the share fall from 52.79% to 21.65%. In South Asia it went from 63.15% to 41.5%. In Sub-Saharan Africa it went from 60.23% to 49.34%, in Latin America and the Caribbean from 21.14% to 12.72%, in Europe and Central Asia from 15.49% to 6.67%, and in North America from 2.99% to 1.48%. In East Asia and Pacific, the fall is smaller in each window than in the one before. In South Asia, the share rose by 0.2 points in 2015–2020 and then fell by 2.44 points. In Latin America and the Caribbean the share also rose in 2015–2020, by 0.78 points. In Europe and Central Asia the fall is slightly larger in each window: 0.78, 1.02 and 1.2 points. In North America the share is so low that the movements stay small. In Sub-Saharan Africa the 2020–2025 fall (2.79 points) is close to the 2010–2015 fall (2.75 points). In East Asia and Pacific and in South Asia, the 2020–2025 fall is less than half of the 2010–2015 fall. These series do not give the weight of each region in world employment, so we cannot separate how much of the slowdown in the world average comes from slower change inside regions and how much from changes in the weights of the regions.

## How were the shares lost and gained spread across income groups?

By income group there are three different stories. Over 1991–2025, agriculture's share fell from 49.82% to 19.84% in upper-middle-income countries, a fall of 29.98 points. In lower-middle-income countries it fell from 58.66% to 39.77%, a fall of 18.89 points. In low-income countries it fell from 66.74% to 59.37%, only 7.37 points. In high-income countries the starting point was already low: from 8.19% to 2.89%, a fall of 5.3 points. The fall is largest in the middle-income groups and smallest in the low-income and high-income groups.

Across the two middle-income groups, both industry and services gained share: industry 7.3 and 6.6 points, services 11.6 and 23.4 points. In the high-income group agriculture was already small; there, industry's share fell by 9.6 points and services gained 14.9 points. In the low-income group, agriculture's share fell by 7.4 points and industry's by 1.7 points, while the share of services rose by 9.0 points. Behind the small 1.9-point rise in industry's world share lie movements in opposite directions across the groups. We do not offer reasons from these data; we only see that the pattern is not the same across groups.

## Türkiye's value on this measure

Türkiye's series comes from the same source. On this measure, Türkiye's value was 46.06% in 1991 and 14.22% in 2025, a fall of 31.84 points. The world value in 2025 was 25.76%. Over the same period the share of services in Türkiye rose from 31.13% to 58.49%, by 27.4 points, and the share of industry from 22.81% to 27.29%, by 4.5 points. In 2025, the share of services in Türkiye is 7.9 points above the world value and the share of industry 3.6 points above it; the share of agriculture is 11.5 points below.

These numbers are not used here to rank countries, so Türkiye is not in the income-group chart either. The database's own notes say that missing observations are completed by a model, and that such completed observations should not be used for country comparisons or rankings. For that reason we set Türkiye's value only beside the world value and the income groups, on the same measure, and did not include any other single country.

## A counter-reading: what a share cannot tell us

Agriculture's falling share does not mean the number of people in agriculture fell. If total employment grows fast, agriculture's share falls even when the number of workers stays constant or rises a little. The series in this article give shares, not head counts; so it is more accurate to say agriculture's share fell than that people left agriculture.

Second comes the breadth of services. Services is a very broad category, covering more than half of employment, and it includes both low-paid and high-paid jobs. This series does not separate them. A rising share of services does not mean that the quality of jobs improved; it only says that jobs are collecting in this category.

Third come definitions. The database notes that countries define and measure employment status in different ways. In the world average these differences may partly cancel out, but at the regional or country level they can matter; we cannot measure this from these data.

Fourth come the income groups. Which countries belong to which group depends on the World Bank's classification, and countries can move from one group to another over time. That movement may play a part in how the group figures read; we cannot separate it with these data.

## How reliable are the model-based years?

The World Bank labels these series ILO modeled estimates, and that is not a detail. According to the database's notes, the series combines observations reported by countries with model-completed observations for countries that lack data; the aim is to capture regional and global trends with consistent country coverage. The same notes say that the models are used for projections as well as for filling gaps, and that completed observations carry high uncertainty. The World Bank database was updated on 8 October 2026; the figures for the latest years may change in later releases.

From these series we cannot tell which years are observations and which are model estimates or projections. For that reason we present the figure of the year 2025 as an ILO estimate, not as an observed fact. Of the 0.9-point rise in 2020, we cannot separate how much comes from observation and how much from the model.

For this reason we do not read the slowdown of the last ten years as a certain break. The estimates show a slower exit from agriculture since 2015; whether that is lasting, or whether later revisions will change it, we cannot say from these data.

## How we calculated

Our three series come from the World Bank database: employment in agriculture, in industry and in services as a share of total employment (SL.AGR.EMPL.ZS, SL.IND.EMPL.ZS, SL.SRV.EMPL.ZS). All three are compiled from ILO modeled estimates. The “World” and regional values are the World Bank's own weighted-average aggregates; the income groups follow the World Bank's classification.

The calculation had these steps:

- For each chart and table we pulled the values for the chosen years through the World Bank's programming interface and recorded each value, with its source address, in the evidence file.
- We calculated the difference between two years in percentage points. We wrote falls as start minus end, so a fall in agriculture is a positive number, and a negative number where a share rose.
- We built the five-year windows in five-year steps over 1991–2025; the regional table has three windows over 2010–2025.
- The regional comparison is limited to six World Bank regional aggregates; it does not cover every region.
- We included no single country other than Türkiye, because the source's own warning is against country comparisons.
- We calculated the differences from the rounded values shown in the article.
