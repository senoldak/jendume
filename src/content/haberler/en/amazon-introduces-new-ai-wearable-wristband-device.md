Technology companies are increasingly pitching AI hardware products designed to combat human forgetfulness. Amazon recently introduced a seventy-five-dollar wristband called the Bee Frontier through its Bee subsidiary. The screen-free device features two built-in microphones to record meetings and conversations, allowing owners to access transcripts and summaries on their phones or computers. Amazon is also preparing a phone accessory to track calls.

Amazon is not alone in this space. Apple unveiled Siri Recap for its latest Apple Watch models to summarize conversations, while smart glasses brands like Vonder and various work-centric recording devices are entering the market. While companies argue these tools help manage details, they instantly raise significant privacy and surveillance concerns.

To address these worries, Amazon states that audio captured by Bee devices is processed in real time and immediately deleted, with transcripts stored in an encrypted environment inaccessible even to employees. Devices also feature LED lights to signal recording. However, privacy advocates argue that small LED lights are easily missed and fail to satisfy legal two-party consent requirements in states like California and Florida.

Oxford University professor Viktor Mayer-Schönberger warns that the broader impact of pervasive AI recording goes beyond simple eavesdropping fears. The natural ephemerality of public spaces evaporates when every remark can be preserved forever. This omnipresent monitoring could cause people to retreat from public life, altering the fundamental fabric of liberal democracies and necessitating careful societal negotiations on permissible technology.
