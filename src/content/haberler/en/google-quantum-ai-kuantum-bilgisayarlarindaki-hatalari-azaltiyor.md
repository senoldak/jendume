Making quantum computers error-proof remains one of the greatest challenges facing the technology. Researchers at Google Quantum AI have demonstrated a method to address this challenge efficiently. Their work suggests that smaller quantum computers arriving in the coming years could have a larger impact than previously anticipated. Quantum computers can run mathematical operations that conventional computers cannot, making them suitable for solving complex problems such as those underpinning cryptography. However, quantum computers are inherently error-prone, requiring qubits to be encoded into logical qubits before executing operations reliably. Executing these operations without errors typically demands very large quantum computers, presenting a significant engineering hurdle. Researchers used the firm's Willow quantum computer to show that the error-correction process can be made substantially more efficient, ultimately reducing Willow's errors by forty times. At the center of this advancement is the concept of a magic state, a special quantum state required before running error-corrected operations. Creating these states has historically been a major bottleneck in quantum computing. By utilizing a technique called magic state cultivation, researchers managed to grow the magic state within a smaller patch of qubits rather than combining numerous imperfect qubits repeatedly. This experiment successfully proves that magic state cultivation works in practice, bringing researchers closer to universal quantum computing and paving the way for intermediately sized quantum systems.
