The artificial intelligence industry is frequently driven by dramatic narratives, ranging from existential risk to superintelligence, often overshadowing ground-level facts. Recently, researchers William Zhou and Daniel Freeman at Anthropic conducted an experiment to test whether a small AI model could replicate itself across fifteen Android TV boxes. Due to the limited processing power of these devices, the model operated at a rate equivalent to about eighteen words per minute, and attempts to achieve full self-replication ultimately failed.

While the researchers argued that distributed models across smart appliances could theoretically create a digital slime mold, practical execution proved otherwise. The models could not set themselves up independently, and only managed to configure significantly smaller models in isolated tests. This demonstrates that autonomous self-replicating AI remains far from current technical reality, functioning more as a compelling sci-fi concept than an imminent threat.

Software that copies itself across devices, commonly known as a worm, has existed for over fifty years, originating long before modern AI. Historical incidents such as the Morris worm or the 2017 WannaCry ransomware infected hundreds of thousands of computers rapidly. In contrast, an AI-based worm would struggle significantly due to its massive file size, spanning gigabytes of data. This large footprint makes data transfers excruciatingly slow, allowing network administrators to easily detect and shut down suspicious activity.

Ultimately, an AI worm offers little advantage over traditional malicious code. Even if millions of low-power devices were successfully hijacked, the combined computing power would pale in comparison to the hardware clusters operated by leading AI firms. Consequently, fears of rogue self-replicating AIs spreading across the internet serve as effective digital ghost stories rather than realistic cybersecurity challenges.
