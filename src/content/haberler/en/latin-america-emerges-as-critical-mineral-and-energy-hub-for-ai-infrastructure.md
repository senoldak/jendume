The global AI race is no longer confined to software and chips; it has expanded into a massive demand for physical infrastructure and energy resources. As data centers and power grids expand, Latin America's natural resources are becoming central to the AI economy. Projections indicate that the global electricity requirements for data centers are set to expand by 170% by 2030. This surge is driving a critical need for copper, essential for electrical transmission, and the energy sources required to power the next generation of computing.

Among regional players, Chile stands out as the world's largest copper producer. However, operational disruptions and declining ore grades led to an 8.5% drop in Chile's copper production during the first nine months of 2026. Despite this, AI-driven demand is expected to push copper prices to $5.95 per pound by 2027. Meanwhile, Argentina is poised to play a key role in energy supply through its vast Vaca Muerta shale reserves, and Brazil offers a sustainable alternative for data centers with an energy mix that was 89% renewable in 2025.

The primary obstacle to realizing this potential lies in the region's insufficient infrastructure and refining capacity. Data from 2025 reveals that the Latin American and Caribbean region processed just 26% of the copper it extracted. This indicates that raw materials are often exported without being converted into high-value products. Furthermore, while Brazil holds the world's second-largest rare earth reserves after China, its actual production capacity remains in its infancy compared to global leaders.

For investors, political stability and fiscal policies remain the most significant risks. Upcoming elections in Argentina in 2027 and concerns over Brazil's debt sustainability impact the financing costs of long-term infrastructure projects. Experts emphasize that for Latin America to emerge as a winner in the AI era, governments must go beyond providing raw materials and create a secure investment environment to build the processing facilities and pipelines necessary to bring these resources to market. Some projects, such as the $400 million debt facility secured by Marimaca Copper Corp, demonstrate that capital is already flowing into the region's development.
