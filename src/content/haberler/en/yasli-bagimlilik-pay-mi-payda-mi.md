In 2023, the population aged 65 and over was equal to 15.4% of the working-age population (aged 15–64) in the world. In 2000 the ratio was 10.9%. The difference is 4.5 points. So the number of older people per hundred people of working age rose markedly over this period.

This ratio is known as the old-age dependency ratio. It is a fraction: the older population on top, the working-age population below. When a fraction rises, two explanations come to mind at once. Either older people are becoming more numerous, or people of working age are becoming fewer. In this article we test them separately. For the world as a whole, the answer is surprising in one respect: the working-age population did not fall; it rose by 1,370,800,665 people. The rise in the ratio came together with the faster growth of the older population. But when we look at a selection of twelve countries, we see that in some of them the denominator also shrank.

The data come from the World Bank's World Development Indicators. The World Bank takes its population age-structure series from the World Population Prospects of the United Nations (UN) Population Division. The series are published under a Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY) licence.

## What exactly does the ratio measure?

The World Bank defines the ratio as the number of people older than 64 divided by the population aged 15–64, multiplied by one hundred. We rebuilt the ratio in the same way with our own calculation and compared it with the World Bank's ready-made ratio series; the results were the same for every value in 2000 and in 2023.

Its denominator covers a wide range: a 15-year-old student and a 64-year-old retiree both count as working-age population. The ratio is bound mechanically to these age limits; it does not look at who actually works.

One caution is needed from the start. The World Bank says that the ratio shows age structure, not economic dependency: some older people work, and many people of working age do not. In this article we use the ratio only as a measure of age structure.

Coverage is 2000–2023. The World Bank's series also contain later years, but we did not take the values that fall in the UN's projection period.

## Is the denominator shrinking?

Not in the overall picture. The working-age population was 3,868,030,547 people in 2000 and 5,238,831,212 in 2023, a rise of 35.4%. In absolute numbers that is an increase of 1,370,800,665 people.

Meanwhile the older population rose from 421,192,302 people in 2000 to 804,797,257 in 2023, a rise of 91.1%. The absolute increase is 383,604,955 people. So the absolute increase of the working-age population is 3.6 times that of the older population. Even so the ratio rose, because the older population started from a much smaller base and grew faster in percentage terms.

A fraction rises only if its numerator grows faster than its denominator. Here the numerator grew by 91.1% and the denominator by 35.4%; both rose, but at different speeds. So for the ratio to rise at world level, the working-age population does not need to shrink. At country level a shrinking denominator does appear; we take that up separately below.

## Is the numerator growing?

Yes, and without a break. Taking the population in 2000 as the base, in 2023 the older population stands at 191 and the working-age population at 135 on an index where the base is 100. The index of the older population stayed above the working-age index at every five-year point, and the gap widened.

At the five-year points, the index of the older population was 113 in 2005, 126 in 2010, 146 in 2015 and 174 in 2020. The working-age index was 109, 118, 125 and 132 in the same years. The ratio at these points was measured at 11.3%, 11.6%, 12.7% and 14.4%; that is, it rose at every five-year step.

## We split the four and a half points in two

We can split the 4.5-point rise in the ratio into the contributions of the numerator and the denominator. This is a calculation exercise, not a value that occurred: first we hold the denominator at its level in 2000. With only the rise in the older population, the ratio would be 20.8%; that is, it would rise by 9.9 points. Then we add the actual growth of the denominator: because the working-age population grew, the denominator contribution is minus 5.4 points. The net result is 4.5 points.

![Waterfall chart: The older population's contribution is positive and the working-age population's negative; the result depends on the order of the split: Contributions: Older population: 9.9, Working-age population: −5.4; Total: Change in the ratio: 4.5.](/grafik/yasli-bagimlilik-pay-mi-payda-mi/graf-7.svg)
*Change in the world old-age dependency ratio, points. Source: World Bank.*

Splitting also involves an order. If we reverse it, fixing the older population at its level in 2000 and then letting the denominator grow, the denominator contribution is minus 2.8 points and the numerator contribution is 7.3 points. The direction stays the same, the sizes change. For that reason we do not read the contributions as an exact division; we say only which side's contribution is positive and which is negative.

All twelve countries saw the ratio rise. On this measure the highest value in 2023 is Japan's, 50.3%, and the lowest is India's, 10.2%. Türkiye's value is 14.7%; the world value is 15.4%. In Türkiye the ratio rose by 5.6 points over 2000–2023, in the world by 4.5 points.

Rankings change in part. In 2000, among the twelve countries, the ratio was highest in Italy (27.0%), with Japan second (25.6%). In 2023, Japan is first and Italy second. South Korea's rank was ninth in 2000 and eighth in 2023; China's was eighth and ninth. Brazil and Türkiye changed places: in 2000, Türkiye was above Brazil, and in 2023, Brazil is above Türkiye. Türkiye's ratio rose from 9.1% to 14.7%, Brazil's from 7.9% to 15.3%.

## What happens where the denominator shrank?

Three of the twelve countries had a falling working-age population: Japan saw a fall of 15.4%, Germany a fall of 5.5% and Italy a fall of 2.4%. In these three the denominator contribution turned positive: 7.7 points in Japan, 2 points in Germany and 0.9 points in Italy. In the order of the split used here, Japan's numerator contribution is 17.0 points and its denominator contribution 7.7 points, a total of 24.7 points.

At the other end are India and Türkiye. The working-age population rose by 54.0% in India and 39.0% in Türkiye. The denominator contribution was minus 5.5 points in India and minus 5.7 points in Türkiye. Even so, the ratio rose in both: by 2.8 points in India and 5.6 points in Türkiye.

In all nine other countries the denominator contribution is negative; India and Türkiye are given above. In China the numerator contribution is 13.1 points and the denominator contribution minus 2.7 points; in the United States 12.9 points and minus 4.4 points; in Brazil 11.9 points and minus 4.5 points; in France 12.9 points and minus 2.1 points; in the United Kingdom 10 points and minus 4.0 points; in Canada 18.6 points and minus 7.4 points. In these countries the numerator contribution is larger than the denominator contribution.

Let us look at Türkiye closely. The older population rose from 3,802,691 people to 8,533,473, and the working-age population from 41,854,612 to 58,157,532. The numerator contribution is 11.3 points and the denominator contribution minus 5.7 points; the ratio rose by 5.6 points. In Japan, meanwhile, the older population rose from 22,123,742 people to 36,809,376 while the working-age population fell from 86,478,606 to 73,201,968.

China's older population rose from 88,605,807 people to 201,958,336; the ratio rose from 10.3% to 20.7%, an increase of 10.5 points.

South Korea is a separate example. The older population rose by 184.4% and the working-age population by 8.1%. The numerator contribution is 18.2 points and the denominator contribution is minus 2.1 points; the ratio rose by 16.1 points.

Both sides of the question are right: at world level the growth in the number of older people is decisive for the rise in the ratio, while at country level a shrinking working-age population is also involved in some places. In the order of the split used here, in the world and in all twelve countries the numerator contribution is larger than the denominator contribution; what differs from country to country is the sign and size of the denominator contribution. For that reason, reading the ratio through a single component alone would be incomplete; the numerator and the denominator have to be followed separately.

## How else can the same data be read?

One reading is to separate percentages from numbers. In percentage terms the older population grows faster; in absolute terms the working-age population grew more in the world. Both readings are correct, but they say different things.

Second, the order of the split matters, as shown above. When the order changes, the sizes of the contributions change.

Third, the weight of countries matters. India's working-age population rose from 635,137,976 people to 978,190,678, and China's from 864,134,387 to 974,720,366. In Japan it fell from 86,478,606 to 73,201,968. The growth in the world total is the result of these different paths; no single country represents the picture.

Fourth, age limits matter. We use a definition that counts 65 as the start of old age; if the limit were elsewhere, the ratio and the contributions would come out differently. We stay with the World Bank's definition of the series.

## What does this ratio not measure?

Age structure is what the ratio measures; it does not measure who works or who supports whom. Nor does this article explain the causes of the change in age structure. The data do not say why the older population rose or why the working-age population shrank.

Moreover, the World Bank reports that the series rest on five-year age groups and that annual values are produced by interpolation, so they may not fully reflect actual events or age composition. For that reason, small yearly movements should not be treated as meaningful on their own.

Small differences between countries, especially between values that lie close together, say nothing meaningful on their own; for that reason we read the rankings only as the result of one measure.

Finally, the twelve countries are a selection. Generalisations drawn from them should not be taken to hold for all countries.

## How we calculated

All values were retrieved from the World Bank's World Development Indicators API with a single-country, single-year query.

- Series: total population aged 65 and over, total population aged 15–64, and the old-age dependency ratio. Unit: people and per cent.
- Countries: Türkiye, Japan, South Korea, Germany, Italy, China, India, the United States, Brazil, France, the United Kingdom and Canada, plus the world total.
- Years: in 2000 and in 2023; for the world also in 2005, in 2010, in 2015 and in 2020.
- Ratio: the older population divided by the working-age population, times one hundred.
- Percentage change: the last-year value minus the first-year value, divided by the first-year value, times one hundred.
- Index: each year's population divided by the population in 2000, times one hundred.
- Split: first an intermediate ratio is computed from the older population in 2023 and the working-age population in 2000. The first step runs from the ratio in 2000 to the intermediate ratio (numerator contribution); the second runs from the intermediate ratio to the final ratio (denominator contribution). In the reverse-order calculation the denominator changes first.
- Point differences were computed from unrounded ratios, so they can differ slightly from the difference of the rounded values shown.

Every number we calculated is in the evidence file together with its inputs.
