Massive data centers required for training and operating artificial intelligence models are becoming a focal point of public concern worldwide. While major technology companies often frame the backlash against these facilities as a "culture war" or a reaction against corporate power, the reality on the ground suggests a more tangible issue: electricity consumption.

In regions where data centers are concentrated, the energy demand from these facilities is straining local infrastructure. For instance, a typical data center in Virginia operates with a power capacity of 54 megawatts, which exceeds the energy draw of a major international airport. Next-generation facilities designed for AI training operate at much larger scales, requiring at least 300 megawatts. An ideal facility is estimated to need 6,000 megawatts, equivalent to the total power consumption of a city like Chicago.

To avoid overwhelming existing power grids, companies are increasingly turning to onsite power generation. Natural gas turbines and diesel generators have become integral to this strategy. This shift is driving massive capital expenditures. For example, one AI company recently agreed to pay $3.4 billion for gas turbines to power its operations. Furthermore, global investments continue to accelerate; a major tech firm announced a $15 billion investment in data center projects in Malaysia.

This insatiable demand for electricity is not only fueling local opposition but also creating pressure on environmental regulations and energy policies. Companies are now looking to relocate to sparsely populated areas or regions with more favorable energy infrastructure to mitigate public backlash. Some tech leaders have even floated the idea of moving data centers off-planet, into space. Ultimately, the physical constraints of the AI world are defined not just by software, but by the fundamental challenge of securing and managing massive amounts of energy.
