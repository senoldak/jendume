Licensing deals between publishers and artificial intelligence companies appear to be slowing down as developers adjust their content acquisition strategies. Major model makers like OpenAI, Meta, and Amazon signed numerous agreements with publishers over recent years, but the pace of new deals for waiting outlets has notably decreased. Media executives note that discussions with AI companies have decelerated, signaling a shift in how content acquisition is approached.

At the same time, the demand for digital content from AI systems continues to rise. Media properties report tens of thousands of automated bots attempting to scrape content from their web pages daily, with publishers blocking the vast majority. In some instances, automated trial accounts attempt to access millions of articles within hours. This high volume of scraping forces publishers to balance site protection with the ultimate goal of monetizing their content within AI ecosystems.

To address these challenges, the industry is exploring alternative payment structures, moving away from broad licensing toward metered and usage-based pricing. Infrastructure providers offer tools that allow publishers to manage bot access and charge per crawl or per use. Research indicates that chatbots utilize only a fraction of the retrieved source material in their final answers, yet uncited background sources continue to shape overall responses.

Meanwhile, a layer of AI data brokers has emerged, crawling the internet and selling access to AI developers. Companies like Parallel have secured significant funding and established partnership programs with major publishers to compensate content creators based on their contribution to AI agent queries. As these payment mechanisms evolve, the media and tech industries continue searching for sustainable frameworks that properly value original journalism.
