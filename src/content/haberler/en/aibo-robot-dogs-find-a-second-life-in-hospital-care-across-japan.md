Sony's robotic dog Aibo, which first went on sale in 1999 and is no longer in production, has found a new purpose in hospitals and healthcare facilities. Through an adoption program managed by Tokyo-based Sony Group Corp., donated Aibo units receive repairs and upgrades before being placed in medical environments to bring comfort to patients and staff. Packed with sensors, digital cameras and behavioral programming, Aibo can remember its surroundings, recognize faces and simulate puppy-like actions such as barking, moving its ears, waddling and going to sleep while monitoring its own mechanical functions.

At the St. Marianna University School of Medicine in Kawasaki, the robotic dogs have proven particularly helpful in easing long wait times and encouraging hesitant children to undergo medical examinations. Dr. Manabu Komori from the department of otolaryngology notes that going to the hospital is usually unpleasant, but Aibo successfully helps motivate children during their visits. While hospitals sometimes use real animals for therapy, robotic alternatives offer practical advantages without the need for feeding, specialized care, or managing unpredictable animal temperaments.

Grounded in the Japanese cultural appreciation for cuteness and harmony, where endearing traits encourage social communication, Aibo facilitates human interaction and helps alleviate the stress of hospital visits. Jerry Wang, Executive Chairman of FF EAI Robotics Ecosystem, notes that people seeking emotional attachment naturally prefer cute shapes such as dogs, cats, or ducks. Sony official Yoshiyuki Katsuno, who oversees the program, explains that owners feel as though they are sending off their own children, and the robot successfully encourages people to talk to each other and keep going.

Sony officials note that the program aims to spread emotional healing through technology. The initiative demonstrates how companion robotics can create meaningful social interactions and emotional support in clinical settings, offering a blend of technology and compassionate care.
