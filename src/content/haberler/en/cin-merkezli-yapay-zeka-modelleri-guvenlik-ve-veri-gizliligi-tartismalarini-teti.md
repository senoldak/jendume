Intense debates are underway in the UK and Western nations regarding the security implications of cheap, open-weight artificial intelligence models developed in China. Companies such as DeepSeek and Xiaomi distribute core software allowing users to deploy models without paying expensive ongoing licenses. While this presents an attractive alternative for Western businesses and public services, it raises profound concerns over data governance.

Experts point out that utilizing Chinese AI tools comes with the risk of user data being shared with Chinese authorities. Michael Wooldridge, a professor of AI at the University of Oxford, notes that it is reasonable to assume data inputted into these chatbots could be shared with the Chinese state. Furthermore, evaluation reports by the US National Institute of Standards and Technology (Nist) have found evidence of political censorship embedded even within downloaded DeepSeek models running on local infrastructure.

Ross Burley, director of the Centre for Information Resilience (CIR), emphasizes that running a model on local servers does not automatically remove political censorship or address underlying security vulnerabilities. DeepSeek's online policy indicates that it collects prompts and personal data, processes and stores them in China, and permits disclosure to Chinese authorities. This strengthens warnings that relying on such systems for critical decision-making could compromise data security.

From an economic perspective, embracing inexpensive Chinese technology threatens to hollow out domestic industries. Analysts argue that Western policymakers should reject the lure of cheap Chinese AI and instead support domestic and European suppliers to safeguard national data and industrial integrity.
