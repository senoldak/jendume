Following the latest political outcomes in the United States, data center projects in Texas are facing potential roadblocks due to shifting leadership. With Democrats securing the governorship in a traditional stronghold, new policies are anticipated for a region that currently hosts roughly 25 percent of the United States' planned data center capacity through 2030. While the state's power load queue has reached 474 gigawatts, incoming local regulations and a push against subsidies have raised concerns among investors.

Brad Gerstner from Altimeter Capital noted that up to 10 to 15 gigawatts of the capacity slated for 2027 could be at risk due to the political transition. Markets have already reacted to these regulatory risks, with Nvidia shares dropping 2 percent and CoreWeave falling 6 percent. According to SemiAnalysis projections, total capacity in the U.S. was expected to reach about 38 gigawatts by the end of 2026, with an additional 43 gigawatts slated for the following year. However, these new political hurdles threaten to slow down expansion.

Amid these growing infrastructure challenges, Elon Musk remarked that space-based data centers are looking quite appealing. Musk's broader vision involves moving petawatt-scale artificial intelligence computing power into space, aiming to mass-produce computing satellites on the Moon. Under this concept, the satellites would be launched into deep space orbit using electromagnetic track systems.

As the exponential growth of artificial intelligence demands unprecedented energy and land resources, local policy friction is pushing tech leaders to explore alternative frontiers. These terrestrial bottlenecks highlight how the future of AI infrastructure may eventually transcend Earth's boundaries.
