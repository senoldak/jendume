For decades, developers have envisioned using mathematics to prove that their software is entirely bug-free. This process, known as 'formal verification,' requires engineers to write precise statements about software goals and convert code into formulas that can be validated. While highly effective, it has historically been so laborious and expensive that it was reserved for critical systems at NASA or Apple. A new wave of startups is betting that generative AI can finally make this rigorous process both practical and essential for the entire industry.

Startups like &Truth and Harmonic are leveraging AI to automate the complex reasoning required for formal verification. &Truth recently demonstrated this potential by using AI to verify parts of the encryption code used in the Signal messaging app. Ilya Sergey, &Truth’s chief science officer, compares the approach to civil engineering, noting that bridges and buildings don't collapse because of the math performed before construction. The goal is to bring that same level of mathematical certainty to the world of software code, ensuring systems don't just work, but are incapable of failing in specific ways.

The field is attracting significant capital. &Truth has raised $70 million, while Harmonic, co-founded by Robinhood CEO Vlad Tenev, has secured $295 million in funding. Harmonic’s AI agent, Aristotle, is already being evaluated by industry giants like Nvidia for verifying hardware designs and C-based software. Furthermore, Anthropic and the startup Theorem recently announced they used AI to identify bugs in open-source software that had previously evaded trillions of traditional automated tests.

Despite the excitement, the technology remains in the prototyping stage. Some experts remain skeptical, arguing that formal verification can be a 'trap' that fails to account for user errors or hardware breakdowns. However, as AI systems themselves become capable of launching sophisticated cyberattacks, proponents argue that mathematically verified code will become a vital defense. If successful, this shift could transform software from a product that is 'mostly reliable' into one that is mathematically guaranteed to be secure.
