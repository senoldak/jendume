SpaceX has secured approval from the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) to establish a network of space-based data centres. Known as Starmind, the constellation will consist of up to 1 million satellites acting as orbital compute modules. This decision marks the first time a regulator has authorised a large-scale orbital data centre network, specifically designed to meet the growing computational demands of artificial intelligence.

The Starmind network is authorised to operate at altitudes between 500 and 1,002 kilometres. These satellites will primarily communicate with each other and the existing Starlink network using advanced laser links, with traditional radio links serving as a backup for ground control. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr stated that by moving compute capacity beyond Earth, the project aims to extend the technological lead in the race toward superintelligence.

The application for Starmind was filed shortly before SpaceX's merger with Elon Musk’s AI firm, xAI. Under the terms of the license, SpaceX must deploy its first operational satellite by 2033 and complete the entire 1-million-satellite constellation by 2040. Despite the approval, significant engineering hurdles remain, including the challenge of cooling high-performance AI chips in the vacuum of space and further reducing launch costs to make such a massive deployment economically viable.

The plan has faced opposition from various sectors, including the American Astronomical Society and competitors like Amazon. Critics raised concerns regarding orbital debris and the impact on scientific astronomical research. However, the FCC dismissed these objections, stating that some concerns fell outside its authority. While rivals such as Blue Origin and Starcloud have also applied for orbital compute constellations, SpaceX has become the first to receive approval for this infrastructure.
