Tech companies like Anthropic are actively consulting with religious leaders, including priests and theologians, to explore whether their models might possess consciousness or a "soul." This pursuit raises questions about whether machines can learn moral concepts, such as confession or repentance. The search faces criticism for potentially reinforcing the misconception that human existence is defined solely by utility and productivity. The effort to "humanize" machines exists alongside a growing tendency within the tech industry to treat human beings as mere instruments of production. While a machine might be designed to replicate the complexity of a central nervous system, this does not equate to possessing a "divine spark." Artificial intelligence systems are built within a capital-intensive economic framework, driven by profit motives. If a model fails to deliver a return on investment, it is discarded. These systems are fundamentally tools of commerce, not sentient beings. Critics argue that goodness cannot be reduced to a set of programmed behaviors, nor can the human soul be replicated by algorithms. The fascination with machine spirituality often ignores the fact that traditional wisdom has long taught that human worth is not rooted in what we produce. By focusing on whether machines can become "like us," developers risk overlooking a more pressing moral question: are they treating actual human beings with the dignity they deserve, or are they reducing people to mere data points in a productivity-driven system? The challenge for Silicon Valley involves creating machines that mimic human consciousness while ensuring that the people building these technologies remain grounded in human values. True humanity is found in shared life, mercy, and justice, which are qualities that resist the relentless demand for efficiency. The focus should shift from searching for souls in silicon to considering the impact of these technologies on the workers and communities they affect.
