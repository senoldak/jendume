Artificial intelligence is being deployed to assist families in Mexico searching for tens of thousands of children who have vanished amid brutal cartel violence and turf wars. With official figures showing over 134,000 people currently being sought, families have long struggled against sluggish investigations and institutional inaction. In response, two Mexican universities have developed new AI-driven tools designed to provide answers and aid the search efforts.

Regresa, developed by Mexico's main public university UNAM, utilizes anthropometry and human body measurements to simulate how the faces of missing minors may have changed as they aged. Noting that standard facial recognition and age-progression software often rely on databases that fail to reflect typical Mexican traits, the developers built a custom image bank using data from local volunteers. This approach focuses on scientific craniofacial development rather than simply adding generic wrinkles or altering surface textures.

In a parallel initiative, an anthropologist at the private Ibero-American University is using AI to map missing persons by gender, age, and disappearance circumstances. Emiliano Zolla's mapping helps families refine their search parameters, particularly highlighting trends such as young women disappearing during their daily commutes to school or work. Parents who have spent over a decade searching for their missing children view these AI initiatives as a potential game-changer in their ongoing pursuit of answers.

Across Mexico, families have increasingly taken investigations into their own hands by forming search collectives, establishing peace boxes in churches, and posting flyers in public spaces. The introduction of academic AI tools brings a vital scientific dimension to these grassroots efforts. By updating likenesses to reflect how children actually grow over time, these technologies offer renewed hope to families demanding justice and resolution.
