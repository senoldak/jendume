Harvard University professor and legal scholar Martha Minow delivered this fall's Compton Lecture at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, examining the civic foundations of democracy and how artificial intelligence impacts modern governance. Noting recent challenges to constitutionalism and the rule of law, Minow cited a Gallup poll indicating that seventy-seven percent of people believe the founders of the United States would be disappointed in the nation's contemporary condition.

Minow focused her remarks on four key ingredients of democracy: education, reliable information, security from violence, and fairness. She warned that dominant artificial intelligence platforms and language models can induce passivity and endanger young people's critical thinking capabilities. Rather than replacing human effort, she suggested that artificial intelligence interfaces could be designed to act like tutors, offering nudges and questions to deepen curiosity and problem-solving skills.

Addressing educational disparities and the risks of misinformation, Minow observed that modern media technology has contributed to a major reduction in journalists over recent decades. She proposed that civic-minded technologists should work toward establishing reliable information commons to help people access trustworthy facts. Furthermore, she expressed concern over societal polarization and the loss of Enlightenment values centered on reason and evidence.

The lecture, held in MIT's Huntington Hall and introduced by MIT President Sally A. Kornbluth, concluded with a question and answer session. Minow emphasized that while contemporary digital technologies present severe risks to education and civic discourse, individuals have the agency to invent better tools that reinforce democracy and human capabilities.
