As embryos develop from stem cells into specialized tissues like the brain, liver, or muscle, they rely on chemical signals sent between neighboring cells to determine their ultimate function. Tracing these instructions has historically been difficult because scientists assumed signaling effects varied widely across cell types, requiring separate maps for each group. Now, a research team led by Pulin Li and Nicholas Hutchins at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Whitehead Institute has discovered that each signaling pathway leaves behind a unique gene activity fingerprint.

Building upon this finding, the team developed a machine learning model named IRIS to detect these pathway-specific fingerprints. The model can pinpoint which signals a cell received at various developmental stages, even across unfamiliar cell types. This artificial intelligence-driven approach overcomes traditional experimental limitations, allowing scientists to comprehensively reconstruct signaling histories across multiple cell types without testing every pathway individually.

The research team trained IRIS on a large experimental dataset measuring how thousands of human embryonic stem cells responded to six major signaling pathways during multiple developmental stages. When tested on single cells from mouse embryos, IRIS accurately predicted when and where specific signaling pathways would activate in cells designated for heart, gut, muscle, and spinal cord tissues. By identifying the exact combinations of signals required for specific developments, such as lung tissue creation, the model enables researchers to generate more reliable lab-grown tissue models.

These improved models provide scientists with practical tools to study complex conditions such as asthma, lung cancer, and pulmonary fibrosis. Researchers can utilize these lab-grown tissue representations to test potential medical therapies and design regenerative treatments aimed at repairing damaged human tissue.
