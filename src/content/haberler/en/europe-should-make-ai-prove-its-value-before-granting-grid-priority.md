The rapid expansion of artificial intelligence technologies is placing unprecedented demands on electricity networks worldwide. According to International Energy Agency projections, global data center electricity consumption is expected to reach approximately 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, more than double its level from 2024. This massive surge complicates grid integration and raises urgent questions about infrastructure costs.

Experts suggest that Europe should resist treating data centers and AI power demand as automatic priorities. Studies indicate that a significant majority of corporate generative AI pilots have failed to produce measurable returns, and the overall profitability of AI data centers remains unproven. Consequently, questions persist regarding who should pay for the supporting infrastructure and generation capacity.

Experiences from the United States offer practical insights into testing the seriousness of proposed energy demand. In Ohio, an initial pool of approximately 30 gigawatts in data center requests narrowed significantly after developers were required to pay for engineering studies and post meaningful credit. Such financial backing ensures that infrastructure decisions are based on credible, committed demand rather than speculative projections.

Europe currently faces generational challenges regarding industrial capacity and regional security. Essential sectors such as manufacturing and defense production drive local employment and must not be sidelined for AI data centers that lack proven economic value. Regulators and policymakers are urged to enforce strict development milestones, financial commitments, and clear public benefits before integrating large proposed loads into energy infrastructure plans.
