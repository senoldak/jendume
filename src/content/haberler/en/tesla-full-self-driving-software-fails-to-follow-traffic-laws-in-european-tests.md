Tesla's Full Self-Driving (FSD) software has demonstrated significant difficulties in adhering to traffic regulations during road safety tests conducted in Belgium. The evaluation, carried out by the pedestrian and road safety organization Johanna.be, revealed that the driver-assistance system frequently disregarded local traffic laws, specifically regarding speed limits in residential areas and regulations on shared cycle streets. According to the findings, the FSD system traveled at an average speed of 44 kilometers per hour in 55 percent of the tested 30-kilometer-per-hour zones. Furthermore, the system displayed incorrect speed limits to the driver in 90 percent of the zones, failing to adjust its behavior even after correctly identifying posted speed limit signs moments earlier.

The software's performance was particularly concerning on cycle streets, which are shared roadways designed to prioritize bicycles. In Belgium, where these streets are common, the FSD system repeatedly attempted to overtake cyclists, a maneuver that is illegal in these specific zones. Researchers noted that in none of the six tested cycle streets did the software correctly indicate or adhere to the applicable 30-kilometer-per-hour speed limit. On several occasions, the supervising driver was forced to manually intervene to abort dangerous overtaking maneuvers.

These findings highlight a significant tension regarding the capabilities of the technology as Tesla seeks to deploy its systems across Europe. While the automaker has received provisional approval for its technology in approximately a dozen countries, including Slovakia, Belgium, and Finland, the software appears to struggle with the continent's specific traffic environment. In response to regulatory scrutiny, Tesla agreed to rename its technology "Tesla Assisted Driving" in Europe. However, safety experts argue that the system creates a problematic gap between the marketing of "full" autonomy and the reality of vehicle responsibility. Given that European roads are often more integrated with pedestrian and cyclist traffic compared to the United States, these results suggest that the software requires substantial refinement to safely navigate local traffic laws and operational requirements.
