XPENG has unveiled its new humanoid robot, "IRON," marking a significant step in bringing artificial intelligence from digital screens into the physical world. Designed to mimic the flexibility and movement of the human body, the robot features 76 degrees of freedom, allowing it to move with human-like dexterity. Equipped with over 1,500 touch sensors covering most of its body, IRON can perceive and grasp objects with precision, effectively "feeling" its environment.

At the core of the robot lies the "Turing Physical AI Architecture," a three-layer brain system developed by the company. This structure consists of System 0, which manages reflexes; System 1, which handles immediate reactions; and System 2, which is responsible for complex, deep-level planning. This architecture enables the robot to balance rapid response times with sophisticated decision-making. The company reports that this system has accelerated training speeds by 10 to 12 times compared to previous models.

To ensure reliability in real-world applications, IRON is undergoing rigorous training across more than 250 scenarios and 15 different industries. The manufacturing process includes over 600 AI-powered holographic quality checks and more than 1,000 component-level tests. XPENG plans to deploy the robot as a sales assistant in its own stores in the first quarter of 2027, with a full public launch scheduled for the second quarter.

The impact of this technology extends beyond humanoid robotics. The same AI infrastructure is being integrated into the company's electric vehicles. An update arriving in December 2026 will introduce the "VLA Adaptive Deceleration" system, which analyzes traffic flow to optimize energy regeneration, increasing range in urban traffic by 12.4 percent. Furthermore, the YOYO-01, an autonomous taxi utilizing the same architecture, is slated for release in 2027. These developments highlight how artificial intelligence is evolving to become a central component in physical mobility and transportation solutions, bridging the gap between advanced software and real-world utility.
