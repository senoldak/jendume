A Decision of the European Parliament and the Council of the European Union dated 14 December 2022, sets several digital targets for the year 2030. One is about skills: at least 80% of adults should have basic digital skills. The measured figure, from Eurostat, the statistical office of the European Union (EU), for the year 2025, is 60.4%. The gap between target and measurement is 19.6 points.

One number is not even half of the story. The Decision sets targets for skills, infrastructure, enterprises and public services. This article follows three of them through four series: skills, ICT specialists, and cloud and AI, which are two parts of one enterprise target. Each series has its own unit, its own speed and its own limits. In this article we treat each series as a clue. For every clue we ask the same three questions: what does the target say, what does Eurostat measure, and where does the remaining distance stand when set against the ground covered in recent years? The article makes no judgement; it records what the targets and the measurements say and what they leave unsaid. In the Decision's own terms, the targets are set at the level of the EU.

## What exactly do the targets say?

The targets article of the Decision sets out the measures that concern us as follows. First, at least 80% of people aged 16–74 should have at least basic digital skills. Second, at least 20 million information and communication technology (ICT) specialists should be employed in the EU. Third, at least 75% of enterprises should use one or more of cloud computing services, big data or artificial intelligence.

Mind the structure of the third target. It concerns enterprises that use at least one of three technologies, not one technology alone. Eurostat publishes the three technologies in separate datasets. So the cloud and AI series we have show parts of the combined target, not the target itself. We return to this below. Beyond these measures the Decision also contains other targets on infrastructure, business scale and public services; this article does not cover them.

According to the Decision itself, the targets are also to be reviewed by the Commission by 30 June 2026. The Commission's State of the Digital Decade report, published in June 2026, says the review of the programme will take place later the same year and may propose changes to the targets. This article uses the targets as worded in the text adopted in December 2022; we did not find an EU document showing that the targets have changed.

## Skills: a distance that differs by country

Start with the EU total. The share of people with at least basic digital skills was 53.9% in 2021, 55.6% in 2023 and 60.4% in 2025. The rise between the first two measurements was 1.6 points and between the last two 4.8 points. So most of the 6.5-point rise over 2021–2025 came in the latest measurement interval. The remaining distance is 19.6 points.

A large spread across member states hides behind the total. The chart below shows ten countries and the EU total with two measurement years and the target line.

Among the ten countries shown in the chart, four are above 80% in 2025: the Netherlands at 83.6%, Ireland at 82.8%, Denmark at 81.5% and Finland at 81.0%. All four were below 80% in 2021: the Netherlands at 78.9%, Finland at 79.2%, Ireland at 70.5% and Denmark at 68.7%. Among the countries shown, the gap between the highest and the lowest value, the Netherlands and Romania, is 51.8 points; Romania stands at 31.8%. Bulgaria is at 38.3%, Poland at 50.4%, Italy at 54.3%, Germany at 59.6% and France at 65.7%.

Change differs by country as well. Over 2021–2025, among these ten countries, the largest rise was in Denmark (12.8 points), followed by Ireland (12.3 points) and Germany (10.6 points). Italy rose 8.7 points, Poland 7.5 points and Bulgaria 7.1 points. Smaller rises were seen in the Netherlands (4.7 points), Romania (4.0 points), France (3.8 points) and Finland (1.8 points). An average hides this spread: because the target is set for the EU total, one country being above the target does not close the distance of the EU total.

Eurostat publishes the same measure for Türkiye, where the share with basic digital skills is 31.5%. The target belongs to the EU, so we give this figure only as a measure.

## Who is missing? Levels, areas and age groups

With 19.6 points to go, a question arises: who would have to move to which level for the share to rise? Eurostat measures skills in five areas and derives an overall level from them: a person who is at least at the basic level in all five counts as having at least basic skills overall. In 2025, 31.4% of EU adults were above the basic level and 29.0% were at the basic level. Together they make 60.4%; the target looks at the share of these two groups.

![Unit chart: Part of those below the basic level are at least at the basic level in four of the five areas: EU: Above basic: 31.4%, Basic: 29.0%, Low: 17.4%, Lower or not assessed: 22.2%.](/grafik/dijital-on-yil-hedef-mesafesi/graf-6.svg)
*EU adults, overall digital skill level, in 2025, per cent. Source: Eurostat.*

Some of those below the target are close to the line: the group at least at the basic level in four of the five areas, called the low level, is 17.4%. Adding this group to the current share gives 77.8%; so even if the whole group closest to the line moved to the basic level, the share would remain below 80%. Those at lower levels, together with people whose skills could not be assessed because they had not used the internet in the last three months, make up 22.2%.

Among the five areas the lowest value is in digital content creation: 70.5% of people are at least at the basic level there. Online safety stands at 74.6%, problem solving at 86.1%, information and data literacy at 86.5% and communication and collaboration at 92.1%. The data gives no reason; it only shows which area is lowest.

Between age groups the gap is large. In the 16–24 age group the share is 74.6%, in the 65–74 age group 33.0%; the difference is 41.6 points. Since the target is set for ages 16–74, these age groups are part of the total.

## ICT specialists: the headcount and the target figure

Next comes a measure about numbers of people. According to Eurostat, 10.5 million ICT specialists were employed in the EU in 2025. The target is 20 million ICT specialists; the measured number is 52.3% of the target. The difference is 9.5 million ICT specialists. That target text also covers promoting women's access to this field and increasing the number of ICT graduates; this article measures only the headcount.

![Key figure: ICT specialist employment rose over five years; the target figure is higher than the measured one: Gap: Remaining: 9,549,200.0; Comparison: 8,442,400.0 in 2020, 10,450,800.0 in 2025, Target: 20,000,000.0.](/grafik/dijital-on-yil-hedef-mesafesi/graf-7.svg)
*EU, number of employed ICT specialists and the difference from the target figure. Source: Eurostat, European Union.*

Let us look at the last five years. Over 2020–2025 the number of ICT specialists went from 8.4 million to 10.5 million, a rise of 2.0 million ICT specialists. We set the same two numbers side by side: the rise over the past five years is 2.0 million, the difference from the target is 9.5 million. We draw no relationship between them and do not carry the past rise into the future.

In total employment, the share of ICT specialists went from 3.4% in 2014 to 5.0% in 2025, a rise of 1.6 points. Eurostat publishes the values in this series with an estimate flag, so they are not final and may be revised.

## Enterprises: cloud and AI, taken separately

Now the third target. Cloud first. The share of enterprises with 10 or more employees using paid cloud computing services was 41.0% in 2021 and 52.7% in 2025, a rise of 11.8 points. On its own this series is 22.3 points from the 75% threshold of the target.

AI moves at a different speed. Eurostat asks enterprises about technologies such as text analysis, speech recognition, language generation, image recognition, machine learning, workflow automation and autonomous robots; an enterprise counts if it uses at least one of them. The share of enterprises using at least one AI technology was 7.7% in 2021, 8.1% in 2023, 13.5% in 2024 and 20.0% in 2025. The rise over 2021–2025 is 12.3 points; 6.5 points of it came over 2024–2025, while the rise over 2021–2023 was only 0.4 points. The series has not moved in one steady direction; the pace changes from year to year.

Neither series gives the combined target directly. The combined indicator is the share of enterprises using at least one of three technologies, so its value cannot be smaller than the cloud share. That means, if the definitions match, the distance of the combined indicator from the target is at most 22.3 points. Eurostat's big data series was not used here, and the combined indicator itself cannot be computed from these two datasets.

Cloud use varies a great deal with enterprise size. Among enterprises with 10–49 employees (small) the share is 49.3%, among those with 50–249 employees (medium) 66.8%, and among those with 250 or more employees (large) 84.7%.

![Bar chart: Cloud use rises with enterprise size: Cloud: Small: 49.3%, Medium: 66.8%, Large: 84.7%, All: 52.7%, Threshold: 75.0%.](/grafik/dijital-on-yil-hedef-mesafesi/graf-8.svg)
*Share of enterprises using paid cloud services on their own, EU, in 2025, per cent; dashed line is the target threshold. Source: Eurostat, European Union.*

Only in the largest class is the cloud share above 75%; that value is the threshold of the three-technology combined target, and cloud on its own is not the target. The medium class is 8.2 points from the threshold and the smallest class 25.7 points. AI shows the same order: the share is 17.0% in the small class, 30.4% in the medium class and 55.0% in the large class, a gap of 38.0 points between small and large. At country level the cloud share is 79.2% in Finland, 75.6% in Italy and 75.1% in Malta; all three are above 75%. The share of enterprises using AI is 42.0% in Denmark and 5.2% in Romania. For Türkiye the same measures are 7.4% on AI and 20.3% on cloud.

## The other reading: why the distance is not one number

Four reasons keep the distances from being added together. First, the units differ: skills are a share of adults, ICT specialists are a headcount, and the enterprise indicators are shares of enterprises. Second, the target year is the year 2030 for all of them, but starting points and past speeds differ; for one series we have a difference of 9.5 million ICT specialists, for another 19.6 points. Third, the cloud and AI series are parts of a combined target, not the target itself. Fourth, the measurement calendars differ: skills data comes every other year, and the enterprise series also miss some years. Reading the four distances side by side as if they were measured on the same day would ignore the time gap between them.

Another reading equates past speed with future speed. We do not do that. The difference between the 2024–2025 pace and the 2021–2023 pace of the AI series shows that past speed cannot be summed up in a single figure. Whether the targets will be reached cannot be said from this data, and it is not the job of this article.

## Where the series and the target indicators may not match

The Decision says the indicators for each target will be set by the Commission in a separate implementing act. This article uses the series Eurostat publishes as close counterparts of those indicators; the indicator texts themselves were not verified in this work. For that reason the figures here may differ by small margins from the figures in official progress reports.

Individual skills data is collected by survey and exists only in 2021, in 2023 and in 2025; the years in between are empty. The skills measure rests on survey answers; the details are in Eurostat's method notes. The enterprise data cover enterprises with 10 or more employees and exclude agriculture, forestry, fishing, mining and the financial sector; the Decision refers to enterprises across the EU without these details. Whether the two scopes are the same cannot be told from this data.

On ICT specialists, the series carries Eurostat's estimate flag. The AI series is short, with only four observations, and the pace differences between them may come from a change in the survey or the definition as well as from a real change. This data does not say which.

## How we calculated

We pulled four datasets from Eurostat: the level of digital skills among individuals, employed ICT specialists, enterprises using AI, and enterprises using paid cloud services. For the EU we took the combined series; for countries we took the member state series. We read the target values from the text of the Decision.

Here the remaining distance is the target value minus the measured value, in points for share indicators and in thousands of people for ICT specialists. The past rise is the difference between two measurement years. The percentage of the target is the measured number divided by the target. The share at lower skill levels is the sum of four separate level series from Eurostat. Age limits and enterprise size-class limits are taken from the source definitions.

Each figure's source, the retrieval address and the raw value of every figure are recorded in the evidence file. Every figure we calculated ourselves can be recomputed from the source values.
