Technology author Kevin Roose sheds light on the inner workings of the race toward artificial general intelligence (AGI) in his new book, The AGI Chronicles. For years, leading AI researchers worldwide have raced to build a technology many of them fear. According to the insights shared, a surprising number of key figures entered the field not out of pure optimism, but because they were terrified of what a superintelligent machine might do to humanity and wanted appropriate precautions taken.

Roose categorizes the AI world into three distinct tribes: scientists who view AGI as the ultimate intellectual challenge, seekers who see it as an evolutionary turning point, and survivalists who believe the technology must be built by responsible actors rather than bad agents. However, as everyone strives to finish first, they all conclude that the only way forward is to accelerate, trapping leaders like Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei inside a continuous paradox.

While early AI pioneers assumed building intelligent machines required encoding human reasoning rules, the true breakthrough came from a remarkably simple combination of vast neural networks and immense compute power. Amodei's 2017 hypothesis of a big data blob redirected research toward general models, laying the groundwork for 's large language models. In tech hubs, this rapid progress fosters a spiritual awakening where developers view their creations less as software products and more like an emerging alien species.

For a long time, industry leaders operated under the assumption that the race would eventually end once AGI was achieved, allowing founders to take sabbaticals or tend to farms. However, by 2025, the industry adopted the concept of the Red Queen's race from evolutionary biology, where it takes all possible running just to stay in the same place. Companies continuously match each other's breakthroughs within months, keeping the finish line perpetually out of reach while modern models easily handle complex cognitive tasks.
