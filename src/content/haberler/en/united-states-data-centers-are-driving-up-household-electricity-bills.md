Massive data centers used to power artificial intelligence models are placing an unprecedented load on electrical grids across the United States. The computing power behind chatbots, image generators, and smartphone AI features draws enormous amounts of electricity from the same grid that powers everyday homes. After consuming about 4.4% of all U.S. electricity in 2023, data center power consumption is projected by the Department of Energy's Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory to scale from 6.7 percent up to 12 percent of the nation's overall electricity use by 2028.

What should concern homeowners is that a data center does not need to be nearby to affect an electric bill. When massive facilities connect to the power grid, utilities often need to build new power plants and transmission lines. Depending on local regulations, those billion-dollar expenses can be passed along to all electricity payers in the region, meaning residents do not need to use AI or live next to a facility to help cover its power costs.

The financial impact is already visible. PJM Interconnection manages the electrical transmission network supplying power to sixty-seven million individuals across thirteen states plus Washington, D.C., and has observed capacity auction prices surge dramatically over a two-year span. Prices hit a record $333.44 per megawatt-day in December 2025 before registering $325 in July 2026. Consulting firm ICF issued warnings indicating that residential electricity rates throughout the impacted area might surge by thirty to sixty percent heading toward 2030.

In adjacent Maryland, local families face projected annual increases scaling from $168 to $216 primarily due to data center demand. Public backlash is growing as well; an October 2026 Consumer Reports survey found that 81% of long-term residents saw their electric bills rise, while 84% support creating a separate rate category for large users like data centers. While some states have begun passing laws to shield residents from these infrastructure costs, and certain tech companies have pledged to cover their own power needs, consumer advocates remain skeptical about enforcement.
