Researchers from the Helmholtz Association of German Research Centres have proposed a novel strategy based on game theory to address the rapid increase of cybersecurity vulnerabilities in the era of artificial intelligence. Presented at the Conference on Game Theory and AI for Security in Michigan, the research calculates how the sequencing of new AI model releases can provide software vendors with a strategic advantage over cybercriminals. The phenomenon where AI capabilities multiply with every new release has raised significant concerns regarding software security. Researchers point out that when Claude Opus 4.6 was released in February, the model discovered over 500 high-impact vulnerabilities in open-source software within its first two days. This highlights how dramatically the time required to go from a disclosed vulnerability to an actual attack has dropped to 10 hours. Traditional responsible disclosure practices, which once gave defenders a reliable head start to build patches, are struggling to keep pace with automated discovery. To counter this dynamic, the research team adapted the Stackelberg game model to involve three players: the frontier lab, software vendors, and cybercriminals. By utilizing exact values from their game theory framework, the authors suggest that frontier labs should incrementally release models and grant software vendors early access. This strategic head start creates an incentive for patches to be ready before public release, balancing market share opportunity costs with overall system welfare.
