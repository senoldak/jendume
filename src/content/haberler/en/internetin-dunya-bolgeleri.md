In 2025, the World Bank's regional aggregates put the share of the population using the internet at 95.6% in North America, 92.4% in Europe and Central Asia and 88.4% in East Asia and Pacific. On the same measure Latin America and the Caribbean stood at 83.7%, South Asia at 67.8% and Sub-Saharan Africa at 35.7%. The value for the world as a whole was 73.6%.

That ranking is a single snapshot. The story lies in the change over 2005–2025. In 2005, North America's value was 68.3% and Sub-Saharan Africa's was 2.0%. The two regions were 66.3 points apart, and the ratio was 34.2 times. In 2025, the gap in points had fallen to 59.9 points and the ratio to 2.7 times. In points the gap fell by 6.4 points; as a ratio it fell from 34.2 to 2.7. What the measure shows, and what it hides, is the subject of this piece.

Below we look first at how the measure is defined, then at the regional curves, the changes in order and the two ways of measuring the gap. We also place Türkiye's value in the same frame. The piece relies only on the series the World Bank publishes, and we offer no judgements about causes.

## Seven aggregates, one measure

The series is the World Bank's indicator of individuals using the internet as a share of the population, in the World Development Indicators (WDI) database. The underlying compiler is the International Telecommunication Union (ITU). By its definition an internet user is a person who has used the internet from any location in the last 3 months, whether on a computer, a mobile phone or another device. So the measure is not a count of subscriptions or a connection speed; it records whether a person used the internet in a given period.

Regional aggregates are calculated as weighted averages of country values. In this piece we use the following regional aggregates: East Asia and Pacific, Europe and Central Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean, North America, South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa. We add the world aggregate to them. We do not cover all the regions in the World Bank's classification; statements about regions in this piece apply only to these aggregates. The regional aggregates start in 2005; there are no regional values for earlier years. That is why our comparison stays within 2005–2025.

Because the measure is a percentage, it has a ceiling. North America's value reached 95.6% in 2025, so the room for further increase there is narrow. Regions with lower values are far from the ceiling. That is where comparison becomes difficult: the same increase in points means something different from different starting values.

## Each region, its own curve

Each region has its own panel on a common scale in the first chart. The world aggregate appears in the next chart.

East Asia and Pacific stood at 14.7% in 2005, 48.3% in 2015 and 88.4% in 2025. Over the same period Europe and Central Asia went from 34.7% to 69.9% and then to 92.4%. The difference between these two regions was 20.0 points in 2005 and fell to 4.0 points in 2025. Latin America and the Caribbean went from 16.6% to 54.4% and then to 83.7%. North America's curve ran from 68.3% to 76.1% and then to 95.6%.

Starting from the lowest values, two regions followed separate paths. South Asia was at 2.1% in 2005, reached 14.7% in 2015 and 67.8% in 2025. Sub-Saharan Africa recorded 2.0%, 16.2% and 35.7%. In 2005 the two began almost at the same point, 0.1 points apart. In 2025, they are 32.1 points apart.

Expressed as multiples, the picture changes. In 2025, South Asia's value is 32.3 times its value in 2005, and Sub-Saharan Africa's is 17.9 times. In North America the multiple is 1.4. A region that starts from a small value produces a very large multiple from the same absolute rise; that is a natural property of the measure.

## Which decade added how much?

If we split the twenty years into two equal slices, something else appears. Over 2005–2015 East Asia and Pacific rose by 33.6 points, Europe and Central Asia by 35.2 points and Latin America and the Caribbean by 37.8 points. Over 2015–2025 the rises in the same regions were 40.1, 22.5 and 29.3 points respectively. So in Europe and Central Asia and in Latin America and the Caribbean the second decade added less than the first; in East Asia and Pacific it added more.

Among the two regions with lower values, the rise in the second slice is larger than in the first. South Asia's rise was 12.6 points over 2005–2015 and 53.1 points over 2015–2025. In Sub-Saharan Africa the two numbers are 14.2 and 19.5 points. In North America they are 7.8 and 19.5 points. The world aggregate's rise went from 24.3 points to 33.7 points.

There is a small coincidence here: over 2015–2025 North America and Sub-Saharan Africa rose by exactly the same amount, 19.5 points. That is why the gap between the two regions is 59.9 points both in 2015 and in 2025. In that decade the measure moved the same distance in points in the two regions, while the ratio kept falling.

## Where did the order change?

The slope chart sets the seven aggregates side by side, in 2005 and in 2025. The order is largely the same in the two years: North America and Europe and Central Asia are in the top two places in both years, and Sub-Saharan Africa is at the bottom in both. In the middle, three aggregates changed order. In 2005, East Asia and Pacific, the world aggregate and Latin America and the Caribbean stood, from lowest to highest, at 14.7%, 15.6% and 16.6%. In 2025, the world aggregate, Latin America and the Caribbean and East Asia and Pacific stood at 73.6%, 83.7% and 88.4%.

East Asia and Pacific's value was 1.9 points below that of Latin America and the Caribbean in 2005, at 14.7% against 16.6%. In 2025, it was 4.7 points above, at 88.4% against 83.7%. South Asia's value was only 0.1 points above Sub-Saharan Africa's in 2005, at 2.1% against 2.0%; in 2025, it was 32.1 points above, at 67.8% against 35.7%. The world aggregate went from 15.6% in 2005 to 73.6% in 2025, a rise of 58.0 points.

These changes in order are not a ranking of merit. Each regional aggregate is a weighted average of a different number and a different size of countries; the order simply sets the values the measure gives in these two years side by side.

## Difference or ratio?

We can measure the distance between two regions in two ways. The first is the difference in percentage points: we subtract Sub-Saharan Africa's value from North America's. The second is to divide one by the other.

In 2005, the gap in points was 66.3 points. In 2007 it reached 71.7 points, the highest of the years we observe. It fell to 59.9 points in 2015, rose to 67.8 points in 2017 and was 59.9 points again in 2025. In none of the years we look at, every second year, did it fall below 59.9 points. The total change over 2005–2025 is minus 6.4 points.

Why did the gap narrow? The arithmetic is simple: Sub-Saharan Africa's rise over 2005–2025 was 33.7 points and North America's was 27.3 points; the difference between the two is 6.4 points. The region with the lower value gained more points, but that gain reduced the 66.3-point distance between the two regions by only 6.4 points.

For the ratio, a very different curve emerges. It was 34.2 times in 2005, 4.7 in 2015 and 2.7 in 2025. The fast fall in the ratio is arithmetic: the denominator, Sub-Saharan Africa's value, rose to 17.9 times its starting value, while the numerator, North America's value, rose to 1.4 times.

Both measures are correct, and neither replaces the other. The difference in points says for how many people out of a hundred there is a gap. The ratio says how many times larger one region is than the other, and that question produces very large numbers when values are small. Which one is relevant depends on what the reader is looking at.

## Where does Türkiye sit on this map?

Türkiye is counted by the World Bank in the Europe and Central Asia region. Türkiye's value was 15.5% in 2005, almost the same as the world aggregate for that year (15.6%). It rose to 53.7% in 2015 and 89.8% in 2025. The rise over twenty years is 74.3 points.

Türkiye's value was 13.8 points above the world aggregate and 16.2 points below the Europe and Central Asia aggregate in 2015. In 2025, it is 16.2 points above the world aggregate and 2.6 points below the Europe and Central Asia aggregate. So Türkiye's distance from the regional aggregate fell from 16.2 points to 2.6 points over that decade. Here we only report the measure.

## Does the average describe the region?

Here is the counter-reading: regional aggregates combine the differing values of the countries inside them into a single number. Countries with very high and very low values can sit together in the same aggregate. None of the figures in this piece shows the distribution inside a region. So a sentence saying that internet use in South Asia is 67.8% does not hold for every country in the region; it describes only the weighted average.

Another counter-reading concerns the percentage itself. For a person to count as an internet user it is enough to have gone online once in the last 3 months. The same value can correspond to daily use in one region and occasional use in another. The series does not separate the two. So seeing that two regions have the same value does not mean that use in the two regions is the same.

One more counter-reading concerns looking every second year. That values for the years in between, for example in 2006 and in 2008, do not appear in the charts hides how the curves moved between two observations. The annual series stays on the page the World Bank publishes; we took only as many points as it takes to show the shape of the curves.

## What these aggregates do not measure

There are two visible steps in the series, and we report both as they stand. North America's value rose by 3.3 points over 2013–2015, and by 11.7 points over 2015–2017. In South Asia the rises are 4.1 points over 2015–2017, 10.9 points over 2017–2019 and 18.3 points over 2019–2021. We do not know the reasons for either, and this piece does not look inside the series to produce an explanation. A regional aggregate is a weighted average; this piece does not investigate where the steps come from at country level.

On the limits of the indicator, the World Bank writes this: data quality varies with differences in the rules for providing data across reporting countries, and in some countries the fiscal year ends on a different date from the calendar year ITU uses. The regional aggregates are therefore the values the compilation gives, not exact measurements. We also write the figures to one decimal place.

Finally, all values in this piece come from the version of the World Bank database published in October 2026. Values may change in later versions.

## How we calculated

- We downloaded the IT.NET.USER.ZS series from the World Bank WDI for seven aggregates (the regions and the world), every second year over 2005–2025; for Türkiye we took the values in 2005, in 2015 and in 2025. Each value was rounded to one decimal place and stored in the evidence file together with the raw value from the source.
- Gap in points: for the same year we subtracted Sub-Saharan Africa's value from North America's.
- Ratio: for the same year we divided North America's value by Sub-Saharan Africa's.
- Change over twenty years: we took a region's two end values, in 2005 and in 2025, and subtracted one from the other (points) or divided one by the other (multiple). For the decade slices we took the value in 2015, as the midpoint.
- All calculations were made on record values rounded to one decimal place.
- We chose to look every second year so that the shape of the curves stays visible while the number of records stays manageable. Values for the years in between exist in the series but did not enter the charts or the calculations.
