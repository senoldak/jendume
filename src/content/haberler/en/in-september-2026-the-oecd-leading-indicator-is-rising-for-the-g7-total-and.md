In September 2026 the OECD composite leading indicator stands at 100.9 for the G7 total and 99.2 for the total of the five major Asian economies. The two numbers are calculated with the same method for the same month, yet they are moving in opposite directions. A year earlier, in September 2025, the G7 total was 99.9 and the Asian total was 100.3. So the positions of the two values changed within a year.

According to the OECD, a leading indicator is an index designed to give qualitative information on short-term economic movements. This article looks at where that index points today in the large economies: where it is rising, where it is falling, and which series resemble each other. We say nothing about the future; we only put the published values side by side, from January 2025 to September 2026.

Readers need to know one thing: the index is not a growth rate. According to the OECD's definition, the main message is the direction of movement, that is, an increase or a decrease. We read the numbers below within that frame.

## What does a leading indicator measure?

Per the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), the composite leading indicator is a time series formed by aggregating many component series. The components are chosen from indicators that show a consistent relationship with a reference series at turning points; since March 2012 the reference series has been gross domestic product (GDP). According to the OECD, the indicator is designed to give qualitative information on short-term movements, especially at turning points, rather than a quantitative measure. So the main message of movements in the indicator is an increase or decrease, not the size of the change.

Beyond this, the OECD states that its headline indicator is the amplitude adjusted composite leading indicator. The series we use is that one: amplitude adjusted, OECD harmonised, monthly. According to the institution's description, turning points in the de-trended reference series have been found on average 4 to 8 months after signals of turning points were detected in the headline indicator. That is an average and an observation about the past; we do not apply it to the values for September 2026.

Indicators are calculated for the G20 countries plus Spain and five zone aggregates. The series we cover are seventeen countries and three totals: the G7, the G20 and the five major Asian economies.

## Two totals, two directions

Chart one shows three series in two-month steps: the G7, the five major Asian economies and the G20 total. In January 2025 the G7 total was 99.6, the Asian total 100.6 and the G20 total 100.2. In September 2026 the values are 100.9, 99.2 and 100.1 respectively.

Over two-month steps, the G7 total rose at every step after March 2025, while the total for the five major Asian economies fell at every step since January 2025. From January 2025 to September 2026 the G7 total rose by 1.3, the total for the five major Asian economies fell by 1.4, and the G20 total hardly changed.

So the split in direction is not specific to the last year; in two-month steps the two totals moved in opposite directions after March 2025.

## Türkiye's series went through two falling periods and then rose again

Türkiye's series moved in both directions and went through two falling periods. In the first, a value of 100.2 in January 2025 fell to 99.8 in March 2025 and to 99.2 in May 2025; it was 99.2 in July 2025 as well and rose to 99.6 in September 2025. It was 100.1 in November 2025 and 100.4 in January 2026. In the second period the value fell to 100.1 in March 2026 and to 99.9 in May 2026. The net change from January 2025 to September 2026 is a rise of 0.5; so despite the two falling periods the series stands above where it started. Over the same period the G20 total was 100.2 in January 2025 and 100.1 in September 2026. After a fall to 99.9 in May 2026, the series reached 100.7 in September 2026. The second chart shows this series together with the G20 total.

## Twelve economies in one table

Our third chart puts the value for September 2026 and the one-year change of twelve economies in one table. In ten of the twelve selected economies the indicator rose over a year, and in two it fell. The largest rises are in Korea (2.9), Brazil (2.3), Canada (2.1) and India (2.0); the largest fall is in China (2.9). In Italy the change is a small decline (0.2). Outside the selection, the one-year change is a fall of 0.5 in Australia and a fall of 0.3 in South Africa.

To read the table it is enough to look at two columns: the left one is the level in September 2026 and the right one is the difference from a year earlier. They should be read together, because a level alone carries no information about direction. The selection is twelve of the large economies for which the OECD calculates the indicator; the selection itself is not a ranking. In detail, in the United States the value is 101.0 in September 2026, and the one-year change is a rise of 1.4. In Germany the value is 101.0 and the rise is 0.5. In France it is 100.7 and a rise of 0.7. In the United Kingdom it is 101.4 and a rise of 0.5. In Japan the one-year rise is 0.6. In China the value is 97.4 in September 2026, and it fell by 2.9 in a year.

## The six-month step and distance from selected months

A one-year difference compares two months; the six-month difference and the values in selected months show better where the movement turned. In eleven of the seventeen countries the one-year change is a rise, and in six it is a fall. For the six-month change the number of rises drops to nine and the number of falls is eight. So the series do not move in one direction on either measure, but the split is sharper over a year.

Countries with a six-month rise are the United States (0.6), Japan (0.3), Germany (0.4), France (0.2), the United Kingdom (1.0), Canada (0.9), Türkiye (0.7), India (1.1) and Korea (1.0). The G7 total also rose by 0.5. Among the countries with a six-month fall the changes are: Italy fell by 0.1; China fell by 1.5; Brazil fell by 0.8; Mexico fell by 0.2; Australia fell by 0.6; Indonesia fell by 2.2; South Africa fell by 0.4; Spain fell by 1.3. The total for the five major Asian economies fell by 0.7 and the G20 total fell by 0.2.

Brazil and Mexico form a separate group: for both, the one-year change is a rise and the six-month change is a fall. These two series have risen since September 2025 but turned back after their values in April and May 2026. In Brazil the value was 103.2 in April 2026; the value in September 2026, is 0.8 lower than in that month. In Mexico the value was 102.7 in May 2026, and today's value is 0.4 lower. In Korea the value was 102.7 in July 2026, and in September 2026, the value is 0.2 lower.

In the series that moved downward the distance is larger. In China the value was 101.4 in February 2025, and in September 2026, the value is 4.0 lower than in that month. In Indonesia the value was 101.8 in December 2025, and in September 2026, the value is 3.1 lower. In Spain the value was 101.2 in December 2025 and the fall is 1.6. For the Asian total, the value was 100.6 in February 2025, and the fall since then is 1.4.

Italy, Australia and South Africa had values of 100.8, 100.7 and 100.7 respectively in December 2025. In September 2026 the distance from those months is 0.5 lower in Italy, 0.8 lower in Australia and 0.5 lower in South Africa. ## A counter-reading: level or direction?

Two readings of the table are possible. The first looks at level: in September 2026, India's value is 102.0 and China's is 97.4, so India's value is 4.6 higher. This reading is tempting but contradicts the OECD's own caution: the main message of movements in the indicator is increase or decrease, not the amplitude of the change. We do not turn level differences between countries into a ranking.

The second reading looks at direction. It is also limited: a one-year difference compares only two months. As the Brazil example shows, a series that rose over a year may have fallen over the last six months. So the six-month and one-year differences should be read together.

Totals bring a third reading. The opposite movement of the G7 and Asian totals does not mean every country inside a total moved the same way. This article did not rebuild the composition of the total series.

## Measurement limits

First, the indicator is not a forecast. The OECD description stresses that the information is qualitative. Second, past values of composite indicators of this kind can be updated; we did not verify this from an OECD document, so the values in this article are the version pulled in October 2026. Third, according to the OECD a country indicator is made up of components chosen from a wide range of short-term indicators; components differ under the same index name. Fourth, monthly series can fluctuate from month to month; two-month steps may hide part of that fluctuation. Fifth, all the numbers in this article come from a single OECD data flow; we did not cross-check them against another source. Sixth, the article covers only January 2025 to September 2026; over a longer period, the position relative to the peaks and troughs of the series could look different; we did not record the whole monthly series.

The data say nothing about what economic growth will be, or about the results of policy decisions.

## How we calculated

- From the OECD data interface we pulled the composite leading indicator series (amplitude adjusted, OECD harmonised, monthly): the values for September 2025, March 2026 and September 2026 for twenty economies and totals; and, for the G7, the G20, the five major Asian economies and Türkiye, two-month steps from January 2025 to September 2026.
- We calculated changes as the difference between two values: the one-year change is calculated from the values for September 2025 and for September 2026, the earlier subtracted from the later; the six-month change is calculated from the values for March 2026 and for September 2026 in the same way.
- We took the OECD's average lag of 4 to 8 months from the institution's data flow description and did not use it in any calculation.
- We wrote values to one decimal; the evidence file holds the two-decimal values.
