During the midterms, Americans voted on 41 ballot measures aimed at cracking down on data center development, rejecting all but one. The lone exception was the town of Harwood, North Dakota, with a population of 845.

Residents ultimately voted against measures that would add roadblocks to the 3 billion dollar, 1.8 million square foot facility primarily out of fear that neighboring Fargo would secure the contract instead. Led by Texas-based firm Applied Digital, the project is estimated by Mayor Blake Hankey to bring in 1.65 million dollars in property taxes once fully constructed, which is six times the town's total tax revenue collected in 2024.

While residents expressed traditional concerns regarding noise and environmental impacts, local pragmatism won out. Mayor Hankey noted that since the data center was bound to be built in the vicinity anyway, the town might as well reap the tax benefits. Applied Digital also claims the facility will create 200 jobs, though analysts remain skeptical about long-term employment projections following construction.

Exit polls across the nation showed that 76 percent of voters opposed new data centers in their communities, with 57 percent viewing artificial intelligence as outright bad for society. Despite these broader sentiments, Harwood's vote highlights the complex economic pressures small towns face when confronting the inevitable wave of AI infrastructure expansion.
