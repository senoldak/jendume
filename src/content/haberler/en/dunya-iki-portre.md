One line from a table: the share of the world population with access to electricity, 78.22% in 2000 and 91.60% in 2023. The other lines of the same table tell a similar story, though each moves at its own pace. Life expectancy at birth went from 67.65 years to 73.33 years, a difference of 5.68 years. The number of children who die before reaching age five fell from 76.7 per thousand live births to 38.3 per thousand, a fall of 50.1%. World population grew from 6.16 billion to 8.06 billion, an increase of 30.9%. GDP per person, at constant prices of the year 2015, rose from 7,879.62 dollars to 11,670.82 dollars, an increase of 48.1%.

This article puts the two portraits side by side. The measures come from the World Bank's World Development Indicators (WDI), and every value was pulled from the source one by one. The World Bank mostly compiles these series from other bodies: access to electricity and clean cooking from the energy-goal tracking work (Tracking SDG), drinking water and sanitation from the joint monitoring programme of the World Health Organization and the UN Children's Fund, school enrolment from the UNESCO Institute for Statistics, the urban share, life expectancy, fertility and population from the United Nations (UN) Population Division, under-five mortality from the UN inter-agency group for child mortality estimation, and GDP per person from national accounts. The text moves between pairs: 2000–2023, the world and Türkiye, increases in points and increases in per cent. For each comparison we say what the measure tells and what it does not.

Definitions come first. Most of the shares show the part of the world population that has access to a service or lives in a certain condition. A point is the difference between two shares; a percentage change is that difference as a proportion of the first value. They say different things, and much of the article is spent on that difference.

## Two portraits of six shares

We chose the measures by one rule: the world total has a published value at both ends of 2000–2023, the World Bank metadata shows an open licence, and the indicator concerns a wide part of the population. The rule left out some familiar measures; we say why in the limits section.

There are six measures: access to electricity, cooking with clean fuels, safely managed drinking water, safely managed sanitation, urban population, and gross enrolment in secondary education. All are given in per cent. Gross enrolment is the number of students enrolled in secondary education, whatever their age, divided by the population of the official secondary school age group; in principle it can exceed the whole.

*Six measures as a share of the world population in 2023, per cent, and the per cent change over 2000–2023. Source: World Bank.*

In points, the largest rise is in cooking with clean fuels: from 47.76% to 74.52%, 26.76 points. Safely managed sanitation follows closely: from 31.29% to 57.80%, 26.51 points. Gross secondary enrolment rose by 17.77 points, access to electricity by 13.38 points, drinking water by 10.74 points and the urban population share by 10.47 points.

In per cent the order changes. Sanitation shows 84.7%, cooking with clean fuels 56.0%, secondary enrolment 29.9%, the urban population 22.4%, drinking water 17.5% and electricity 17.1%. A measure that starts from a low value shows a larger percentage change for the same point increase, so the two orderings do not measure the same thing. Points tell us how large a share of the population gained something new; the percentage change tells us how far we have come relative to the start.

## Electricity and clean fuels over the years

To see the road between the two portraits, look at the years in between. Access to electricity was 80.70% in 2005, 83.45% in 2010, 86.93% in 2015 and 90.40% in 2020. The share cooking with clean fuels in the same years was 52.08%, 56.70%, 63.94% and 71.31%.

![Dot chart: The share cooking with clean fuels rose faster than the share with electricity: Access to electricity: 78.2% in 2000, 80.7% in 2005, 83.5% in 2010, 86.9% in 2015, 90.4% in 2020, 91.6% in 2023; Clean fuels for cooking: 47.8% in 2000, 52.1% in 2005, 56.7% in 2010, 63.9% in 2015, 71.3% in 2020, 74.5% in 2023.](/grafik/dunya-iki-portre/graf-6.svg)
*Share of the world population, selected years, per cent. Source: World Bank.*

Electricity stood 30.46 points above the clean fuel series in 2000, and 17.08 points above it in 2023. The clean fuel series rose faster in these years, and the gap narrowed. The last interval, 2020–2023, covers three years, so we do not compare it directly with the five-year intervals.

The other shares show the same direction in the years between. Safely managed sanitation was 35.12% in 2005 and 55.19% in 2020; drinking water was 62.81% in 2005 and 70.48% in 2020; gross secondary enrolment was 64.41% in 2005 and 76.51% in 2020. Over the last three years, 2020–2023, the rise is 2.61 points for sanitation, 1.51 points for drinking water and 0.73 points for secondary enrolment.

One point of caution in reading the two together: access to electricity and cooking with clean fuels measure different things. One is whether a household has a service; the other is which fuel is mainly used for cooking.

## Longer lives, fewer child deaths

Life expectancy at birth shows how many years a newborn would live if the death rates of that year stayed the same. For the world this value was 67.65 years in 2000, and 73.33 years in 2023. The difference is 5.68 years, or 8.4% in per cent.

![Key figure: Life expectancy at birth rose by 5.68 years over 2000–2023: Rise: Rise, 2000–2023: 5.68; Years: Year 2000: 67.65, Year 2023: 73.33.](/grafik/dunya-iki-portre/graf-7.svg)
*World, life expectancy at birth, years; the large figure is the difference over 2000–2023. Source: World Bank.*

Years in between also show a rise: 69.11 years in 2005, 70.68 in 2010, 71.97 in 2015 and 72.18 in 2020. The rise over 2015–2020, 0.21 years, is smaller than any other interval between the selected years. Per the World Bank's note on the indicator, the annual series from the United Nations (UN) Population Division is interpolated from five-year period data, so the detail of single years does not describe particular events.

Under-five mortality shows a sharper fall. Of every thousand children born alive, 76.7 died before reaching five in 2000, 62.3 in 2005, 50.6 in 2010, 42.9 in 2015, 39.2 in 2020 and 38.3 in 2023. The total decline is 38.4 children; in per cent it is a fall of 50.1%.

![Column chart: The under-five mortality rate is lower in each selected year than in the one before: Under-five mortality rate: 76.7 in 2000, 62.3 in 2005, 50.6 in 2010, 42.9 in 2015, 39.2 in 2020, 38.3 in 2023.](/grafik/dunya-iki-portre/graf-8.svg)
*World, children dying before age five, per thousand live births, selected years. Source: World Bank.*

At each step the rate is lower than the one before, but the size of the fall shrinks: 14.4 over 2000–2005 and 3.7 over 2015–2020. Over 2020–2023 it is only 0.9. The slowdown may also reflect the fact that absolute differences get smaller as values near lower levels; this data does not separate the two.

## Population, cities and output

World population rose from 6.16 billion to 8.06 billion over the two years: 30.9%. The share living in cities went from 46.84% to 57.31% in the same period. What counts as a city varies by country: each country uses the definition of its own statistical office, and the UN Population Division collects and smooths these data. So this share is built from national definitions, not from one common measure.

Births per woman fell from 2.73 to 2.20, a decline of 19.4%. GDP per person, that is gross domestic product divided by population, rose, at constant prices of the year 2015, from 7,879.62 dollars to 11,670.82 dollars, an increase of 48.1%. While the population grew by 30.9%, GDP per person grew by 48.1%; this means total output rose faster than population. GDP per person is an average here; it does not show who earns how much.

Internet use could not enter this portrait. The World Bank's series on the share of internet users, for the world total, starts in 2005: 15.6% that year and 69.2% in 2023, a difference of 53.6 points. But since no world value is published for the year 2000, we left this measure out of the common set of the two portraits.

## The same measures in Türkiye

Four measures for Türkiye are given in this section: life expectancy, safely managed sanitation, cooking with clean fuels and GDP per person. The World Bank also has both years for Türkiye for other measures; at the end of the section we say which ones we left out and why. Life expectancy at birth was 71.93 years in 2000, and 77.16 years in 2023; the rise is 5.23 years. In the same year the world value is 73.33 years, so Türkiye's value is 3.83 years higher.

Clean fuel cooking in Türkiye was published as 92% in 2005, 93% in 2010, 97% in 2015 and 98% in 2020. Sanitation was 48.27% in 2005, 55.42% in 2010, 63.34% in 2015 and 72.32% in 2020.

Safely managed sanitation use rose in Türkiye from 44.23% to 77.19%, 32.96 points. The world value in 2023 is 57.80%; the difference is 19.39 points. The share cooking with clean fuels rose in Türkiye from 91% to 98%, by 7.00 points.

GDP per person at constant prices of the year 2015 rose in Türkiye from 6,326.26 dollars to 14,932.89 dollars, an increase of 136.0%. In the year 2023 the world value is 11,670.82 dollars, and Türkiye's value is 3,262.07 dollars higher. These comparisons are measures only. The dollar value of GDP per person depends on exchange rates and price adjustment, and comparison between countries has its own limits.

Left out are these measures. Access to electricity: the share in Türkiye was 99.9% and 100% in the two years, which adds no information to the comparison. Urban population share: the definition of a city varies by country, so it is not directly comparable. Secondary gross enrolment: a gross ratio can exceed the whole, so it is not directly comparable. Under-five mortality: in 2023 the series contains a one-year jump that departs from the earlier years; we found a single-year comparison misleading and did not take it. Fertility and population: this article focuses on access to services and on health measures, so we did not take them. No drinking water series is published for Türkiye.

## Counter-reading: what does an average hide?

First, what the average is. The World Bank defines the world total in the indicator metadata as a weighted average; for access to electricity it is population-weighted. What exactly the weight is can differ from indicator to indicator, and we did not verify it separately for the six indicators. Countries with larger weights pull the average harder. So saying that access to electricity is 91.60% in the world does not mean that access is at that level in every country. This article does not look at the distribution across countries; below the average, very different values can be found.



Second, method. Presenting the difference between two years as one direction assumes that the change in between was smooth. Most series above rise steadily, yet in life expectancy the rise over 2015–2020 is small, and the fall in under-five mortality slows step by step. A portrait shows us two states; it does not show the film. Even the series for which we give the years in between hold only selected five-year points, not every year.

Third, the distinction between points and per cent. A rise of 84.7% in the sanitation share looks very large; in points, the rise of 26.51 points is 13.13 points above the rise of 13.38 points in access to electricity. Which measure stands out depends on which comparison we choose, so we give both.

Fourth, the measures do not weigh equally; the change in one indicator can describe a heavier or lighter phenomenon than the change in another. Life expectancy squeezes a great deal into a single number, while the sanitation share counts the presence of one specific service. Reading them on the same line would weigh different things on the same scale.

## Limits of the measurement

First, models. Life expectancy, fertility and population come from the UN Population Division's World Population Prospects. Annual values are produced by interpolating five-year period data. The under-five mortality rate rests more on surveys and indirect estimation than on national registration systems; a UN inter-agency group prepares the common estimate.

Second, definitions. Safely managed drinking water and sanitation look for several conditions together, such as accessibility at the point of use, availability and protection from contamination. How these conditions are measured may differ from country to country.

Third, selection. The same database contains series whose methodological notes do not support historical comparison, and series with no world value for the year 2000; we left them out of the portraits.

Fourth, period. The two portraits do not describe the years in between or the differences in them. There are only two cross-sections.

## How we calculated

Values of the six measures, life expectancy, the under-five mortality rate, fertility, population and GDP per person, for the world (WLD) and Türkiye (TUR), were pulled from the World Bank's WDI database, each with a separate query for one country and one year. The selected years run at five-year steps over 2000–2023; for population, fertility and GDP per person only the two ends of the span. The metadata of each indicator lists the licence of the World Bank series as open (CC BY). The one exception is the clean cooking series: its original source is published under a licence that allows non-commercial use (CC BY-NC); since this site is non-profit, we used the series.

Four kinds of calculation were done. Difference: the gap between two values, for shares in points. Percentage change: the difference between the first and the second value divided by the first and turned into a percentage; for sanitation, for example, the gap between 31.29% and 57.80% divided by the first value gives 84.7%. Gap between the world and Türkiye: the difference of the values for the year 2023. Gap between intervals: the difference of consecutive selected years.

Our comparison takes the world total and Türkiye; we added Türkiye as a meaningful reference for readers in Türkiye. Results were computed from records rounded to two decimals.
