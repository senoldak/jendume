The year 2026 has been recorded as a historic turning point for mathematical discoveries in the AI world. Models developed by technology giants such as OpenAI and Anthropic have managed to solve the Riemann hypothesis, considered the 'Holy Grail' of mathematics, and the Navier-Stokes problem, which forms the basis of fluid mechanics. These developments occurred much faster than even leading mathematicians expected, as many did not anticipate such solutions before 2050.

The solution of mathematical problems is seen as proof of AI's ability to perform complex logical inference, not just data processing. In particular, proving the Riemann hypothesis has the potential to affect many fields, from number theory to cybersecurity. However, experts emphasize that while the digital and logical nature of mathematics is a suitable playground for AI, it is too early to expect the same speed of progress in areas involving the complexity of the physical world, such as biology or climate change.

Looking at the key figures, two of the Millennium Prize Problems were solved by AI within this year alone. Previously, the target for solving these problems was the 2030s. Furthermore, while clinical testing of AI-designed drugs continues, the accuracy of mathematical proofs can be instantly verified through computer code, placing these achievements on a much more solid footing.

In terms of context, these successes strengthen the claim of AI companies that they can 'solve any problem.' However, progress in fields like biology and medicine requires not just intelligence, but physical experiments and long-term observations. For example, results from human trials of an AI-supported drug will not be available until 2029. This situation highlights the gap between AI's speed in the digital world and the realities of the physical world.
