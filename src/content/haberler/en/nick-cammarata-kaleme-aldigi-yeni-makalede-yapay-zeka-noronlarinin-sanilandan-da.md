Goodfire researcher Nick Cammarata argued in a new essay that the inner workings of artificial intelligence models are much cleaner and easier to understand than previously assumed. For years, the prevailing theory was superposition, which suggested that neural networks pack unrelated concepts into the same neurons when they run out of capacity. However, the new analysis argues that famous examples of this phenomenon were misinterpreted, and that neurons are often simpler detectors of specific shapes and features rather than tangled messes of unrelated concepts. In tests involving models like Qwen3-32B, researchers found that roughly 90 percent of neuron poles have one main job, while about 7 percent have two related ones, leaving only 2 to 4 percent unexplained. Researchers examined large models using automated agents and found that the vast majority of neurons focus on a primary function rather than random overlapping concepts. This shift in perspective could transform how scientists study AI interpretability, moving from complex decomposition methods back to direct observation at a massive scale. Proponents believe this clarity could make AI safety and alignment significantly more achievable, turning alignment into a more predictable science. On the other hand, leading researchers in the field remain skeptical, arguing that the findings downplay the real complexity of large language models and that superposition still plays a major role in how models store vast amounts of information.
