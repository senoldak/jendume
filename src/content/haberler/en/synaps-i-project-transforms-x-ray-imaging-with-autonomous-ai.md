The SYNAPS-I project, a collaborative effort involving five U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) national laboratories, has directly integrated artificial intelligence models into experimental workflows at X-ray and neutron facilities. Led by Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, this multi-lab collaboration aims to transform the entire scientific process from data collection to discovery. While traditional methods required collecting data first and analyzing it later, this new system allows AI to autonomously identify which regions of a sample should be measured during the experiment.

Demonstrations at the National Synchrotron Light Source II (NSLS-II) at Brookhaven National Laboratory proved that the system could identify scientifically interesting regions within complex samples without human intervention. This autonomous approach enables experiments to run for hours, surveying large areas and collecting high-fidelity data only from critical locations. Consequently, scientists can make much more efficient use of their limited beam time at these state-of-the-art facilities.

Another significant milestone of the project involved the three-dimensional imaging of microelectronic chips. Through a partnership with NVIDIA, the team established a real-time workflow capable of processing 200 images per second. Foundation models trained at Argonne National Laboratory replaced the computationally intensive iterative processes traditionally used for image reconstruction. Furthermore, Meta’s open-weight Segment Anything Model 3 (SAM3) was fine-tuned to automatically identify different materials and structures within the microchip data.

This technology not only increases data processing speed but also significantly reduces the amount of experimental data required. Researchers successfully reconstructed high-resolution 3D images using nearly one-tenth of the data normally needed. This reduction is crucial for lowering radiation doses and protecting sensitive samples. In the future, these systems are expected to become standard in various fields, from studying degradation in battery electrodes to developing next-generation semiconductors.
