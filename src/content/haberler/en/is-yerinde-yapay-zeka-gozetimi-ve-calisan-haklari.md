Workers around the world are increasingly facing microscopic surveillance in their workplaces driven by artificial intelligence. In the United Kingdom, consumer cooperative Co-op implemented automated listening technology to record and grade customer service phone calls. While whistleblowers described the monitoring as oppressive, the company stated its advisors are highly engaged.

In response to mounting workplace surveillance, legislative action is emerging. In California, new laws have been enacted to prohibit intrusive workplace tracking tools, such as heat maps monitoring bathroom breaks and systems evaluating emotional states. Hospital nurses and warehouse workers have similarly reported being subjected to automated behavioral metrics.

Beyond surveillance, economic disparities persist in the AI landscape. Data shows a significant gender pay gap in AI-related occupations, alongside reports of remote data workers in developing regions facing precarious conditions and opaque reward systems while training the very models reshaping the global economy.
