How much does solar electricity swing over a year? Eurostat, the statistical office of the European Union (EU), publishes monthly figures for the EU as a whole, and they answer this directly. The series covers only electricity from solar panels, that is photovoltaic plants, and “solar electricity” in this article means that. In 2025, photovoltaic plants in the EU generated 45,625 gigawatt-hours (GWh) of net electricity in June. In December the figure was 10,113 GWh. The peak month produced 4.51 times as much as the lowest month.

Within the same data there is a second comparison. In June, solar supplied 22.0% of all net electricity generated in the EU; in December it supplied 4.1%. For the year as a whole the share was 12.9%. Saying that solar provides 12.9% of annual electricity is correct, but the same sentence describes June and December very differently.

This article measures the size of that seasonal gap. We first look at six countries in 2025, then at the EU total. After that we follow a timeline across 2017–2026 and ask whether the monthly shape changed while the annual total grew. In the text, “summer” means June–August and “winter” means January, February and December; the method section explains the definition. Net generation, in Eurostat's own data definition, is the electricity left after the power plant's own use is deducted.

## Six countries, one year

Let us start in January. The small panels below show the year 2025 for six countries. In each panel a month's value is divided by that country's annual solar total, so the panels compare the shape of the year, not the size of the country. These six countries are not a ranking or a full list; they put three southern countries, one large producer in the middle and two northern countries side by side.

In the three southern countries the peak is broad and gentle. In Spain the highest month was July, with 12.5% of the annual total; in December the share fell to 4.5%. In Italy June was highest at 12.8% and January lowest at 3.4%. In Greece July peaked at 13.6%, and the lowest month was November, not December, with a 5.2% share.

In Germany the curve is steeper. The highest month is June at 14.0%, and the share falls to 2.2% in December. In Sweden June peaks at 15.0% and December bottoms out at 0.6%. In Finland July carries 19.3% of the annual total, while December drops to 0.3%.

A solar plant works with sunlight, and sunlight varies over the year, so some seasonal gap is expected. But an expectation is not a measurement. This article does not look for causes; it reports the size the data shows. The vertical axis is shared across the panels and does not start at zero; it runs between the smallest and the largest of all values. A curve that looks higher in one panel does correspond to a higher share.

## The EU total, month by month in 2025

Stacking the countries gives the EU total. In January 2025 the EU's solar plants produced 10,409 GWh. In March the figure rose to 28,158 GWh. May followed at 41,298 GWh and June saw the peak at 45,625 GWh. In September the value fell to 32,275 GWh; in November it was 14,236 and in December 10,113 GWh.

Two things stand out in the chart. First, the summer peak is not a single month but a broad plateau: June, July and August together carry 38.6% of annual output. Second, the winter trough is just as wide: January, February and December together hold only 10.8%. The gap between the two shares is 27.9 points. Splitting the year into two half-years says the same thing: the six months from April to September carried 70.5% of annual output. Output is not spread evenly across the year; the months in the middle carry clearly more than the months at the start and the end.

## Output rose three times, the shape stayed

Now let us go back in time. In 2019 the EU's solar output totalled 113,753 GWh. In 2025 it reached 343,137 GWh, so annual output rose to 3.02 times its starting level. Did seasonality shrink or grow with that growth? To find out we look at each month's share: we divide a month's output by that year's total and place the two years side by side.

![Dot chart: The spread of output across months follows the same shape in both years: in 2019: Jan: 3.04%, Feb: 5.50%, Mar: 8.23%, Apr: 10.54%, May: 11.12%, Jun: 13.58%, Jul: 12.90%, Aug: 12.29%, Sep: 9.60%, Oct: 6.78%, Nov: 3.25%, Dec: 3.18%; in 2025: Jan: 3.03%, Feb: 4.77%, Mar: 8.21%, Apr: 10.41%, May: 12.04%, Jun: 13.30%, Jul: 12.89%, Aug: 12.47%, Sep: 9.41%, Oct: 6.39%, Nov: 4.15%, Dec: 2.95%.](/grafik/gunes-elektriginin-mevsimleri/graf-7.svg)
*EU, share of each month in the annual total, per cent. Source: Eurostat.*

Almost on top of each other lie the two curves. June carried 13.6% of the annual total in 2019 and 13.3% in 2025. July was 12.9% in both years. December was 3.2% in 2019 and 2.9% in 2025. The largest gap between the two years is in May: the share rose from 11.1% to 12.0%, which is 0.9 points. November shows a similar gap, from 3.3% to 4.1%, again 0.9 points. These differences are too small to change the shape of the year.

Absolute values also show growth: June output was 15,448 GWh in 2019; in 2025 it was 45,625 GWh. December output went from 3,620 GWh to 10,113 GWh. Both months grew, but growth did not bring the months closer together. This points to an important distinction. Total output can rise three times while its spread across the year stays the same. The data does not measure capacity or investment; it only says in which months the electricity was generated.

## Nine years of peaks and lows

To see whether the shape belongs only to the year 2025, we found the highest and lowest month of every year over 2017–2025 and computed the ratio between them. The result falls in a narrow range: the ratio stayed between 4.39 and 5.55 times every year. The lowest ratio came in 2024, at 4.39 times, and the highest in 2022, at 5.55 times. In 2025 the ratio was 4.51 times. Year by year the ratios were 4.73 in 2017, 5.00 in 2018, 4.47 in 2019, 5.38 in 2020, 5.30 in 2021, 5.55 in 2022, 5.08 in 2023, 4.39 in 2024 and 4.51 in 2025. In 2017, the first of the nine years, the peak month was June (12,995 GWh) and the lowest month was December (2,746 GWh).

The peak month was June in five years and July in four. The lowest month was December in six years and January in three. Over the same period annual output went from 98,850 GWh to 343,137 GWh. Output rose to 3.47 times its starting level, while the peak-to-low ratio kept its narrow range.

Solar's annual share of all net electricity generated in the EU grew over the same period too: 3.6% in 2017, 5.7% in 2021 and 12.9% in 2025. The share grew and the ratio stayed put; the growth of the share did not change the shape within the year.

## From south to north in the six countries shown: the ratio and the summer–winter gap

Back to the countries, in the six countries we show two measures give the same order: Greece, Spain, Italy, Germany, Sweden, Finland. The first is the ratio between the peak month and the lowest month. The second is the difference between the summer share and the winter share. The table shows both side by side; rows follow this order, with the EU total in the middle and Türkiye last, as a measure.

Greece has a ratio of 2.61 times, Spain 2.79 and Italy 3.72. In Germany it rises to 6.30 times. In Sweden it is 23.82 and in Finland 63.00. The gap between the summer share and the winter share rises in the same direction: 18.7 points in Greece, 20.6 points in Spain, 24.8 points in Italy, 31.2 points in Germany, 37.5 points in Sweden and 47.5 points in Finland. The EU total sits between these two ends: a ratio of 4.51 times and a gap of 27.9 points.

Taken alone, the summer share gives the same picture. It is 35.4% in Greece, 36.1% in Spain and 36.9% in Italy, then 39.7% in Germany, 43.0% in Sweden and 50.3% in Finland. The winter share is 2.8% in Finland, 5.5% in Sweden and 8.6% in Germany, then 12.1% in Italy, 15.5% in Spain and 16.7% in Greece.

Finland's ratio needs careful reading. In December 2025, Finland generated only 3 GWh. When a small amount shifts by one GWh, the ratio moves a lot; 63.00 times is a figure sensitive to rounding. The summer–winter gap is steadier because it combines three months. That is why the table has two columns: one looks at the extremes and the other at three-month blocks, and if both give the same order, the ranking does not hinge on a single month.

Eurostat publishes the same series for Türkiye. In 2025, Türkiye’s peak month was July and its lowest month December; the peak-to-low ratio was 2.74 times, the summer share 35.6%, the winter share 14.2%, and the gap between them 21.4 points. We give these values as a measure in a separate row of the table, and we make no judgement about a target or the EU total.

Greece also has a detail: its lowest month is November, not December. November produced 457.8 GWh and December 504.0 GWh. The lowest month is not the same calendar month in every country, so for each country we took its own lowest month when computing the ratio.

## January–July 2026: the first seven months

Our data reaches into the year 2026. Over January–July 2026, the EU's solar output was 254,863 GWh. For the same seven months in 2025, output was 221,791 GWh, a difference of 14.9%. In July 2026, output was 54,352 GWh, which is above the highest month in 2025, June, at 45,625 GWh. Comparing July with itself, output was 44,215 GWh in July 2025; in July 2026 it was 22.9% higher.

We do not turn this into a forecast. Writing a level or a direction for the rest of the year is not the job of this article. We only say that the January–July total exceeded the same period of the year before. Once the year 2026 is complete, its monthly shape can be compared again with the 2017–2025 line.

## The other reading: what the share does not say

There are three places where this data can be misread. First, a monthly share shows shape, not level. Finland produced 978 GWh in 2025, and Italy 44,323 GWh; Finland's summer share may be higher, but its output is far smaller. Second, Eurostat's series is a compilation of values reported by countries, and this dataset alone does not show how far their coverage matches (for example, how rooftop systems are counted). Third, the peak-to-low ratio rests on only two months; if one month's value changes, the ratio changes. There is also a question of weight. In 2025, Germany supplied 25.5% of the EU's solar output, Spain 14.6% and Italy 12.9%. The EU curve is therefore not an equal mix of northern and southern curves; the shape of the large producers sets it.

Another reading treats seasonality as a problem. We do not. That solar output peaks in summer is a fact the data shows; the dataset holds no judgement on whether this is good or bad, and we will not write one. Grids, storage and demand are outside the scope.

## The data are not final: revisions and small values

Eurostat's monthly series can be updated. The dataset was updated on 22 October 2026, and later releases may revise past months. Among the values we used, only Spain's values in 2025 are published by Eurostat with an estimated flag, so they may not be final. Spain's figures may differ somewhat from the final values.

Small values are sensitive to rounding. Finland's December is 3 GWh and its January 5 GWh; at that level a change of one unit shifts the ratio a great deal. This is why two measures are given together in the country comparison.

Finally, this article covers only solar photovoltaic generation. Total net generation was used only to compute solar's share.

## How we calculated

We pulled the data from Eurostat's table of monthly net electricity generation by type of fuel. We chose the solar photovoltaic fuel code for solar, the total code for the all-sources figure, and gigawatt-hours as the unit. For the EU we took the combined series; for countries we took the series for Spain, Italy, Greece, Germany, Sweden and Finland.

Monthly share is a month's value divided by the sum of that year's twelve months, in per cent. The summer share is the sum of June, July and August divided by the annual total; the winter share is the same for January, February and December. The summer–winter gap is the difference between these two shares, in points. The peak-to-low ratio is the highest month of the year divided by the lowest. We found each year's peak and lowest month from the data itself. For solar's share of net generation we divided solar output by total net output for the same period. For the comparison with the year before we added up January to July.

Each figure's source, retrieval address and raw value of every figure are recorded in the evidence file. Every figure we calculated ourselves can be recomputed from the source values.
