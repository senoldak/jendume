Artificial intelligence tools have rapidly transitioned from workplace novelties to common tools for professionals like accountants, attorneys, and consultants. Gallup found that 52 percent of U.S. employees and 68 percent of professional services workers were using AI by February 2026, while separate research showed that 74 percent of professionals use AI multiple times a week for work. However, this widespread adoption brings a major drawback: AI can produce information that sounds authoritative while being completely wrong, generating fabricated citations and inaccurate content that can easily mislead unsuspecting users.

In Clinco v. Commissioner, a case before the U.S. Tax Court, an attorney submitted a legal brief containing AI-generated fake citations of nonexistent court cases, prompting the court to warn against such hallucinations. In another incident, a 237-page report produced by Deloitte for the Australian government contained fabricated academic citations and quotes generated by artificial intelligence, leading the firm to issue a revised report and partially refund its fees. Similarly, Air Canada was ordered to pay damages after its customer service chatbot provided incorrect bereavement travel policy information to a passenger.

Experts and standards organizations note that AI hallucinations and confabulations present misleading information in a confident manner, making them difficult to spot without independent verification. A Stanford University study examining specialized legal AI tools found erroneous results in 17 percent to 33 percent of tested responses. Despite these documented accuracy risks, many law firms and businesses continue adopting AI systems to save time and improve efficiency, often without sufficient oversight mechanisms.

However, recent surveys indicate that businesses lack adequate safeguards. A 2026 survey by Nationwide found that only 36 percent of surveyed businesses have established written AI policies, and only 37 percent provide training on responsible AI use. Researchers and experts emphasize that telling employees to simply check AI outputs is no longer sufficient, as organizations must clearly define responsibilities and verification processes. Ultimately, while artificial intelligence transforms professional tasks, the fundamental accountability for the final output always remains with the human professional.
