Thirty-five researchers from institutions including Harvard University, the International Centre for Theoretical Sciences, KU Leuven, and Google DeepMind have released a new evaluation framework called TheoPhysEval. The benchmark tests leading artificial intelligence agents on fifty unpublished theoretical physics problems spanning eleven subfields, from fluid dynamics to cosmology. Tested without human hints, OpenAI's GPT-6-Astra model emerged as the top performer, successfully solving forty-nine out of fifty problems, achieving a ninety-eight percent accuracy rate. The average cost per solved problem remained under three dollars, totaling less than one hundred sixteen dollars for the entire test suite. Anthropic's Opus model secured second place with a ninety-two percent success rate, while other models achieved lower scores. Experiments revealed that internet access made surprisingly little difference for top models, suggesting they have effectively memorized extensive theoretical physics literature. While these results demonstrate that modern models have effectively mastered complex calculations previously restricted to a handful of human specialists, authors note that current agents still lack the scientific taste and intuition required to decide which research questions are worth pursuing. Furthermore, researchers warn that as computational complexity increases, disparities in research funding could create accessibility challenges in a field historically known for requiring fewer resources than experimental physics.
