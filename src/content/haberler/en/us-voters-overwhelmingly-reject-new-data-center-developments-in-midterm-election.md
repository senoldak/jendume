The 2026 US midterm elections marked a significant turning point for the physical infrastructure of artificial intelligence, as voters across 10 states cast ballots on 41 measures concerning data center expansion. In a widespread rejection of the tech industry's development agenda, voters supported anti-data center proposals in nearly every instance. Out of the 41 measures on the ballot, only one resulted in an outcome favorable to the AI industry, while the rest were passed by voters to restrict or regulate development.

These results demonstrate that the physical backbone required to train and run AI models is increasingly clashing with the environmental and social concerns of local communities. Data centers, which demand significant energy and land resources, have transitioned from being purely technical infrastructure projects to becoming central issues in local politics. The backlash indicates that the pace of AI industry growth is now facing substantial political and regulatory hurdles.

Candidates who campaigned on platforms opposing new data center projects frequently outperformed their opponents. In states such as Nevada, Ohio, Texas, and Georgia, politicians who took a firm stance against these facilities secured gubernatorial seats. According to exit polls, 56 percent of voters identified data centers as a "crucial" or "major" factor influencing their ballot choices. Furthermore, despite nearly $450 million in spending by prominent tech figures to influence election outcomes, these efforts failed to sway public opinion against the prevailing anti-data center sentiment.

Ohio emerged as a focal point for this trend, with 21 separate proposals on the ballot, all of which saw voters reject advances from the data center lobby. The only exception to this nationwide trend occurred in Harwood, North Dakota, where citizens voted against adding an extra review layer to zoning procedures. This election cycle serves as a clear warning for the AI industry, suggesting that future infrastructure expansion will face increased legal challenges and difficulty finding communities willing to host new facilities.
