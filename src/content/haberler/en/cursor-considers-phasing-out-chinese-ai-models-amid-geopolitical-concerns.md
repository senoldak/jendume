A joint report released by the US House Homeland Security Committee and the Select Committee on China has revealed that the software development platform Cursor is planning to phase out Chinese open-weight AI models. The company cited "geopolitical risk" as the primary motivation for this potential shift. Cursor had previously built its flagship model, Composer 2, on top of the Kimi K2.5 model developed by the Chinese firm Moonshot AI.

This development has reignited debates regarding the security implications of American tech companies' reliance on foreign AI infrastructure. The committees have scrutinized not only Cursor but also major firms like Airbnb and DoorDash. Airbnb currently utilizes Alibaba's Qwen model to manage approximately 80,000 guest and host conversations daily, while also summarizing 250,000 support cases. DoorDash, meanwhile, reported using 25 different Chinese model families in its operations.

The report emphasizes that cost, customization, and high performance are the primary drivers for adopting these Chinese models. The lack of affordable, open-weight domestic alternatives in the US forces companies to rely on these foreign options. The committees are now calling for an expansion of federal bans and the launch of a national initiative to foster American open-weight AI models.

Conversely, the situation is evolving differently on a global scale. India-based startup Sarvam, despite being a government-backed initiative, has begun commercializing access to DeepSeek and other Chinese models. Sarvam reports serving over 450 billion tokens daily on the DeepSeek V4.1 Flash model. This contrast illustrates how the balance between openness and security in AI technology varies significantly across nations. US officials warn that forcing companies to abandon these models before trusted domestic alternatives exist could significantly increase operational costs.
