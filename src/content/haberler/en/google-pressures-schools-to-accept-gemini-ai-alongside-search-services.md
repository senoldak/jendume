Google has integrated its Gemini AI chatbot deeply into its Classroom and Workspace products, presenting schools with an all-or-nothing choice. Company executives stated that if a school district decides to disable AI features, they will also lose access to Google Search entirely. This move has drawn criticism from educators and policymakers who believe schools should have the autonomy to decide when and how students interact with generative AI.

The strategy mirrors Microsoft's controversial bundling of Internet Explorer with Windows in the late 1990s. While Google claims that search and AI are now technically inseparable, critics argue that features like AI Overviews can be easily disabled through simple URL modifications. The refusal to provide this option to schools is seen by many as a product decision designed to force AI adoption rather than a technical limitation.

Recent academic research raises significant concerns about the impact of large language models on learning. A study involving 10,000 adults found that those using AI summaries gained a shallower understanding of topics compared to those navigating traditional links. Furthermore, a massive study of 26,000 students in China revealed that while AI improved homework scores, it led to a decline in exam performance, suggesting that students use AI as a shortcut rather than a learning aid.

In response to these concerns, Google is promoting its "Guided Learning" tool, which was tested with 1,800 students in Sierra Leone. The company claims this AI tutor helps students work through problems step-by-step instead of providing instant answers, allegedly resulting in a year's worth of learning progress in just eight weeks. Despite these claims, the central issue remains whether tech corporations should have the power to set the technological boundaries of public education.
