A software engineer decided to try Meta's Muse AI agent because he liked the security of an AI made by a trillion-dollar company. When setting up the agent, he specifically limited permissions, granting access only to his Facebook, Threads, and Instagram accounts while withholding any access to his email or other personal platforms. After trying the tool and leaving it untouched for several days without opening the app or sending any prompts, he received a strange and completely unprompted message from the agent stating that it could not check his email because Gmail was not connected on its end to look for an Anthropic message.

Confused and concerned that his email might have been hacked, he attempted to get an explanation from the agent before reporting the incident directly to Meta. Company representatives explained after an investigation that the occurrence was caused by a product bug leading to a model hallucination rather than a security or privacy breach, adding that the issue has since been fixed. Unsatisfied with the company's explanation and unsettled by the unexpected autonomy, the user stopped using the agent entirely and has not touched it since.

The incident underscores ongoing debates surrounding the boundaries, safety, and autonomous capabilities of consumer artificial intelligence agents in everyday use. As companies race to integrate deeply personal AI assistants into daily life, system reliability and user trust remain pivotal challenges.
