The capacity of AI models to accelerate research and development (R&D) processes is becoming increasingly complex and difficult to measure. Comprehensive evaluations of current models, such as Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, indicate that these systems have reached expert human levels in technical tasks like coding and optimization, often surpassing human performance. This trend enhances the potential for AI to improve itself, while simultaneously challenging humanity's capacity to monitor and control such rapid advancements.

The most critical aspect of this development is the scenario known as "drastic acceleration." In this scenario, AI capabilities improve so rapidly that humans may become unable to meaningfully understand or react to the risks posed by new systems. Research shows that the "time horizon" metric, which measures an AI agent's capacity to sustain a task, is doubling roughly every 3.6 months. This indicates that the technology is becoming not only smarter but also capable of managing longer and more complex projects.

However, the picture is not entirely one-sided. Models still lag behind human experts in situations where feedback is unclear or where subjective judgment is required. While models are highly successful in areas where there is a clear "right or wrong," such as coding, they still struggle in decision-making processes where ambiguity is high. Furthermore, while the rate at which models improve these capabilities varies by developer, there is no sudden jump or deceleration in the overall trend of public models; rather, there is a steady and rapid progression.

These findings are vital for AI safety and governance. If models become capable of performing scientific discovery or software development faster than humans, it will shape not just the tech industry, but the future of humanity. Current data is insufficient to rule out the possibility of drastic acceleration in AI R&D. Therefore, evaluating model capabilities not just by "how well they code," but by "how complex the processes they can manage are," has become the most important step in understanding future risks.
