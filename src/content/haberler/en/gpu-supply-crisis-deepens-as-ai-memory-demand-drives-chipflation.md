Artificial intelligence data centers are consuming memory chips as fast as manufacturers can build them, prompting GPU makers to reallocate resources toward the ongoing boom. This resulting memory shortage has sparked severe consequences for consumer technology, driving up component prices and dwindling supplies. Dubbed chipflation, the surge in GPU costs has vastly outpaced general inflation. According to a JPMorgan report, dynamic random access memory prices are projected to rise by four hundred percent by the end of two thousand twenty-six compared to two thousand twenty-four. Nvidia's flagship GeForce RTX 5090 currently retails significantly above its launch price from January two thousand twenty-five.

At the core of the issue is a severe shortage of high-bandwidth memory, which AI developers desperately need to train and run their models. Suppliers have continuously pivoted manufacturing capacity toward advanced HBM chips due to higher profit margins, leaving traditional DRAM products underproduced. Major manufacturers like Micron, Samsung, and SK Hynix are heavily prioritizing data centers over consumer GPUs. Micron CEO Sanjay Mehrotra warned during an earnings call that supply will remain much tighter through two thousand twenty-seven and two thousand twenty-eight, while SK Hynix CEO Kwak Noh-jung forecasted next year to be the most difficult in industry history regarding supply constraints.

Leading suppliers have already committed the vast majority of their future manufacturing capacity. Micron has reportedly sold seventy-five percent of its two thousand twenty-seven HBM output, while Samsung has locked in roughly eighty percent of its memory production for that year. Although figures like Acer CEO Jason Chen remain skeptical about long-term shortages due to potential new market entrants, Nvidia head Jensen Huang maintains that memory demand will continually expand to meet advancing technological needs.
