A new era of labor is emerging where human employees work alongside autonomous software entities. Startups in the San Francisco tech scene, such as Anything, are pioneering this shift by deploying AI agents to handle everything from office management to technical troubleshooting. These agents operate without human intervention, scheduling meetings, managing customer onboarding, and even handling search engine optimization. At Anything, the workforce consists of 15 humans and roughly 150 AI agents, a ratio that leadership believes will soon become the industry standard.

This trend is rapidly moving beyond niche startups into corporate America. According to McKinsey’s State of AI Global Survey, approximately 40% of large organizations utilized AI agents in some capacity between May 2025 and June 2026. The primary driver is the ability to scale operations while maintaining lean teams. A report from Boston Consulting Group suggests that companies expect to reduce their personnel numbers by 10 to 15 percent in the coming years as they delegate more responsibilities to these semi-autonomous systems.

Major technology providers are fueling this transition with specialized tools. New digital assistants from OpenAI, Anthropic, and Microsoft are designed to integrate with existing business software to manage projects long after human employees have finished their day. In industries like film production, where budgets are often tight, AI agents are now being used to scout locations, generate call sheets, and manage scripts—tasks that previously required multiple human assistants working for several weeks.

Despite the efficiency gains, the rise of AI coworkers presents significant challenges for the next generation of workers. Industry experts worry that entry-level roles, which traditionally served as a 'foot in the door' for young professionals, are being eliminated. As routine tasks are offloaded to software, the value of traditional skills is shifting toward the ability to coordinate, delegate, and oversee AI outputs. Human oversight remains essential, however, as agents are still prone to hallucinations and errors that require thoughtful correction by experienced professionals.
