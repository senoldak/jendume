Meta has officially launched its new AI assistant, Muse, designed to perform tasks such as shopping, booking tickets, and conducting negotiations on behalf of users. The rollout spans several countries, including the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and Japan. Muse is built to operate autonomously, with the capability to connect to apps, access bank details, and send emails, provided it receives explicit permission from the user for each action.

This deployment has sparked significant concern within the corporate sector. Employers are increasingly worried about staff connecting their personal AI assistants to work emails and internal databases. The potential for data leakage or unauthorized access to sensitive company information has prompted businesses to reconsider their internal guidelines and security protocols regarding agentic AI.

Regulatory bodies are also responding to the rise of these autonomous tools. The UK Information Commissioner’s Office has stated that firms utilizing agentic AI must be able to demonstrate ongoing compliance with data protection laws. This includes identifying and implementing specific steps to mitigate risks of harm to both users and the wider public.

As autonomous agents become more capable, the boundary between personal convenience and professional risk continues to blur. Employers are now tasked with managing the problem by issuing new advice or changing rules to govern how employees interact with these technologies. This shift highlights the growing need for robust frameworks that ensure AI tools can be used safely without compromising corporate integrity or data privacy.
