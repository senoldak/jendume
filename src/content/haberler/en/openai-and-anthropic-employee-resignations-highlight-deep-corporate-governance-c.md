Over the past two years, a significant pattern has emerged across the world’s leading artificial intelligence laboratories. Safety researchers and engineers have repeatedly stepped down, pointing out that commercial pressures and financial priorities are frequently overriding internal safety protocols. A prominent safety official who oversaw product release reports at OpenAI resigned, stating that the company's internal culture was broken and that the broader industry was operating with excessive carelessness regarding technological risks. Similarly, an Anthropic researcher left the company after warning about severe risks associated with advanced models by the end of the decade. These departures highlight a deeper institutional failure within the sector. When conscientious engineers find that leaving the organization is the only effective way to raise alarms about potential hazards, vital expertise required to steer these systems safely is lost. Experts, policymakers, and labor representatives gathering in London have emphasized that regulating artificial intelligence purely from the outside remains insufficient as long as the organizations building these systems function as internal autocracies. Corporate lobbying efforts often frame safety regulations as burdensome obstacles to innovation, encouraging a false trade-off between progress and protection. However, researchers argue that artificial intelligence functions less like a conventional consumer product and more like a foundational governance technology. Within frontier laboratories such as OpenAI, Anthropic, Microsoft, and Google, a small circle of executives and capital investors ultimately decides which values are embedded into models affecting millions of lives, exposing the limitations of traditional corporate governance models.
