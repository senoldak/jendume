Facing a scarcity of public and synthetic data, artificial intelligence labs are increasingly purchasing the digital remains of defunct startups. Specialized closing-down services have established asset hubs where founders can sell their leftover code bases, emails, and workspace chat logs. These real-world archives help AI developers build better training environments where autonomous agents can practice complex problem-solving and decision-making.

While the market began as a taboo and rare occurrence, it has grown rapidly as major tech players and foundation labs seek deeper, more nuanced datasets. Real archives avoid the common errors found in synthetic data, offering varied human interactions that improve how AI models handle complex reasoning tasks. Recent high-profile transactions, including data acquisitions from struggling enterprises, indicate that this secondary market for corporate archives is expanding.

However, this practice navigates murky legal and ethical waters. Privacy advocates and employment law experts point out that employee records, calendars, and personal conversations often lurk within these acquired archives, usually sold without the explicit consent of the workers. As the industry continues to push the boundaries of model training, the lack of clear federal regulations in this digital Wild West leaves significant room for privacy concerns.
