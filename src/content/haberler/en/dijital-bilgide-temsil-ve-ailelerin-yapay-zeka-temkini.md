In the digital age, whether a person, discovery, or culture is remembered often depends on how well it is documented online. Our relationship with digital knowledge faces a dual reckoning: who gets represented in our shared archives, and how families are rethinking artificial intelligence’s place in everyday life.

Through the *Mappa Mundi* project—a collaboration between writer and broadcaster Sandi Toksvig and researchers at the University of Cambridge—scholars are working to address a striking imbalance on Wikipedia. Although the platform hosts roughly 65 million articles across more than 300 languages and draws over 15 billion views a month, only 19% of its biographical and historical coverage features women.

## From Encyclopedias to Artificial Intelligence

Researchers warn that gaps in collaborative encyclopedias no longer affect search results alone—they directly shape the foundation of artificial intelligence:

- **How AI learns the world:** Large language models rely heavily on Wikipedia and open web archives as verified training material. When women scientists, regional thinkers, or non-Western perspectives are missing from the source material, AI outputs inherit those blind spots.
- **Rebuilding the map:** The Cambridge-backed initiative brings together editors and historians worldwide to document overlooked scientists, writers, and pioneers across languages using verified archival sources.
- **Parents drawing new boundaries:** At home, a parallel shift is underway as growing numbers of parents reconsider early smartphone access and unmoderated AI chatbots for children, returning to deliberate boundaries and offline reading.

## From Information Access to Genuine Understanding

Having access to more information is not the same as understanding the world more deeply. True understanding in the age of artificial intelligence requires both questioning whose stories are missing from our collective memory and ensuring technology remains guided by human judgment rather than replacing it.
