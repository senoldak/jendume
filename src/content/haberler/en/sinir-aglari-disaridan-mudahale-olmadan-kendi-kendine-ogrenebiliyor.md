As artificial intelligence continues to advance, understanding how its underlying mechanics relate to the human brain remains a central question for neuroscientists and engineers alike.

In research published in PNAS, scientists at MIT’s McGovern Institute for Brain Research and the K. Lisa Yang ICoN Center showed that artificial neural networks can organize themselves without an external developer, mimicking the way biological brains operate.

Engineers typically design explicit rules for how artificial neural networks adjust connections based on training feedback. However, the human brain lacks an external architect; it must organize itself through fundamental biological interactions. The MIT team demonstrated that a group of randomly connected units can reshape into an effective information-processing network using simple, local rules.

The team proved that such self-organizing networks can successfully recognize patterns and classify images. Unlocking these fundamental learning rules marks a critical step toward developing advanced neurotechnologies, such as brain-computer interfaces for paralyzed patients, and creating better prevention strategies for neurological diseases.
