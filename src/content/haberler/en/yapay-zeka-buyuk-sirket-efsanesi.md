Is AI used only by large companies? The survey of enterprises for the year 2025 by the statistical office of the European Union, Eurostat, answers briefly: no, but the gap between size classes is wide. Across the EU, 55.0% of enterprises with 250 or more employees use at least one AI technology. Among enterprises with 50–249 employees the share is 30.4%, and among those with 10–49 employees it is 17.0%. The gap between the smallest and the largest class is 38.03 points.



When Eurostat counts use, it means that an enterprise uses at least one of eight technologies: text analysis, speech recognition, text or code generation, image recognition, machine learning, workflow automation, autonomous robots, and picture, video or audio generation.

This article asks how robust the size gap is. We first set the three classes side by side, then look at the change over 2023–2025, at the technologies and at the countries. After that we read the data from the other direction: 44.84% of even the largest class uses none of these technologies, and among the countries listed the gap within one size class is of the same order as the gap between classes.

## Three classes, three different pictures

Enterprises are grouped by employment: those with 10–49 employees are small, those with 50–249 are medium, and those with 250 or more are large. The survey population is enterprises with at least 10 employees. Micro enterprises (0–9 employees) are covered on an optional basis, and this series carries no published value for them.

*Share of enterprises using AI technologies, by employment size class, EU, in 2025, per cent. Source: Eurostat.*

Use rises with size: 17.0% in the small class, 30.4% in the medium class, 55.0% in the large class. The small-class share is 30.9% of the large-class share. The medium-class share is 55.2% of the large-class share. The gap between the medium and the large class is 24.67 points.

Eurostat's methodological note reports that of the 1.53 million enterprises in the population, about 83% are small, 14% medium and 3% large. The survey covered a sample of 157,000 enterprises. The classes differ in weight; this article does not turn those weights into numbers of users, and compares only the share within each class.

## How far did each class rise in two years?

Back in 2023 the share was 6.41% in the small class, 13.07% in the medium class and 30.48% in the large class. In 2025 the values were 17.0%, 30.36% and 55.03%.

![Dumbbell chart: In two years the share of enterprises using AI rose in all three size classes: in 2023: 10–49 employees: 6.4%, 50–249 employees: 13.1%, 250+ employees: 30.5%; in 2025: 10–49 employees: 17.0%, 50–249 employees: 30.4%, 250+ employees: 55.0%.](/grafik/yapay-zeka-buyuk-sirket-efsanesi/graf-6.svg)
*Share of enterprises using AI technologies, EU, in 2023 and in 2025, per cent. Source: Eurostat.*

Measured in points, the rise is largest in the large class: 10.59 points for small, 17.29 points for medium, 24.55 points for large. In percentage terms the order reverses: 165.2% for small, 132.3% for medium, 80.5% for large. The same point increase from a low starting value gives a larger percentage change; this is arithmetic, not a sign of superiority.

Read together, the two measures say this: the gap in points widened while the gap in ratio narrowed. The difference between the small and the large class was 24.07 points in 2023, and it was 38.03 points in 2025. Over the same period the small-class share went from 21.0% of the large-class share to 30.9%.

Eurostat also publishes the series for the year 2021. That year the share was 6.12% in the small class and 28.41% in the large class. Over 2021–2025 the small-class share rose by 177.8% and the large-class share by 93.7%. The years in between are not smooth: in the small class a value of 6.41% in 2023 became 11.21% in 2024, and in the large class 30.48% became 41.17%.

A caution applies here. Eurostat states that changes in definitions, made so that the survey can follow the development of information and communication technology, limit comparison over time. A value for the technology of picture, video and audio generation is published only for the year 2025: 8.1% among enterprises with 10–49 employees and 27.92% in the large class. Because that technology is part of the definition of using at least one technology, the values for the year 2025 may not be built from exactly the same question as earlier ones. For that reason we read the change over 2023–2025 as an ordering of the classes relative to each other, not as a precise measure.

## Where is the gap widest among technologies?

Across all eight technologies the large-class share is higher than the small-class share. The size of the gap differs by technology.

![Slope chart: For every technology, the share of large enterprises using it is higher than that of small ones: 10–49 staff: Text mining: 9.9%, Speech recognition: 5.9%, Text/code generation: 7.0%, Image recognition: 3.0%, Machine learning: 3.8%, Workflow automation: 4.1%, Image/audio generation: 8.1%; 250+ staff: Text mining: 35.0%, Speech recognition: 26.1%, Text/code generation: 31.7%, Image recognition: 16.7%, Machine learning: 25.1%, Workflow automation: 24.4%, Image/audio generation: 27.9%.](/grafik/yapay-zeka-buyuk-sirket-efsanesi/graf-7.svg)
*Share of enterprises using each technology, EU, in 2025, per cent; 10–49 employees on the left, 250 and over on the right. Source: Eurostat.*

In both classes the most widespread technology is text analysis: 9.87% in the small class and 35.04% in the large class. In the small class it is followed by picture, video and audio generation (8.1%) and text or code generation (6.96%). In the large class the second and third places go to text or code generation (31.68%) and picture, video and audio generation (27.92%). So the top of the ordering is made of the same technologies in both classes; the difference lies in how widespread they are.

Now the ratios. For machine learning the small-class share is 3.81% and the large-class share 25.05%; the small-class share is 15.2% of the large-class share. For autonomous robots the values are 1.0% and 8.55%, a ratio of 11.7%. For text and code generation they are 6.96% and 31.68%, a ratio of 22.0%. For picture, video and audio generation they are 8.1% and 27.92%, a ratio of 29.0%. For text analysis they are 9.87% and 35.04%, a ratio of 28.2%.

On this measure the two technologies with the highest small-to-large ratio are picture, video and audio generation and text analysis. The lowest are autonomous robots and machine learning.

Purposes of use show a similar split. For marketing and sales, the share of small enterprises using AI is 5.79% and that of large ones 20.85%, a ratio of 27.8%. For the security of information and communication technology, the small class stands at 2.47% and the large class at 26.14%, a ratio of 9.4%.

There is also the breadth of use. Of the AI-using enterprises with 10–49 employees, 62.2% use at least two technologies together. In the large class the share is 79.9%. Among users, the share of those content with a single technology is higher in the small class than in the large one.

## Same size, different country

Beside the gap between classes there is a gap between countries. Among enterprises with 10–49 employees, the share using AI in 2025 was 37.45% in Denmark, 33.51% in Finland, 28.76% in the Netherlands, 23.06% in Germany, 17.0% in the EU overall, 15.04% in France, 14.23% in Italy, 6.55% in Türkiye, 5.79% in Poland and 4.13% in Romania.

Among the countries listed, the highest value is in Denmark and the lowest in Romania. The difference between them is 33.32 points; within the EU the difference between the small and the large class is 38.03 points. So among the countries listed, the country an enterprise sits in separates enterprises of the same size by an amount close to that of the size class.



Ratios between the classes also vary by country. In Denmark the small-class share is 50.3% of the large-class share, in Germany 40.5%, in Türkiye 27.9%, in Italy 26.8%, in Romania 19.9% and in Poland 12.6%. In Finland it is 42.2%, in the Netherlands 42.5% and in France 25.9%.

Over 2023–2025 the small-class share rose in every country in the table. The change is 32.3% in Türkiye, 138.2% in Germany, 157.3% in Poland, 191.9% in Finland, 202.7% in Denmark, 226.4% in Italy and 286.0% in Romania. When the starting value is low, a percentage change grows quickly; the share in Romania was 1.07% in 2023.

## The half of the large class that does not use AI

Many enterprises that do not use AI are found among the large ones too. Of enterprises with 250 or more employees, 55.03% use at least one technology and 44.84% use none.

Non-users make up 81.91% of the small class. The survey also asks non-users for their reasons. In the small class the most frequently marked reason is a lack of relevant expertise: 7.22%. Next come lack of clarity about legal consequences (5.58%), concern about data protection and privacy (5.35%), difficulty with the availability or quality of data (4.45%), incompatibility with existing equipment and software (4.25%), and costs that seem too high (3.95%). Ethical considerations stand at 2.58% and the view that the technologies are not useful for the enterprise at 1.96%.

Among large enterprises a lack of expertise stands at 10.32% and cost at 5.51%. The share of large enterprises saying the technologies are not useful to them is 1.55%, lower than among small ones. The share of enterprises reporting that they have ever considered using one of the technologies is 10.24% in the small class and 15.97% in the large class.

Our reading of these lines is limited to measurement. The Eurostat table's unit is the percentage of enterprises in the class. From the survey table it cannot be told which enterprises were asked for reasons or whether respondents could mark several; for that reason we do not compare reason rates with the share of non-users, and only compare the answers of the two classes with each other.

## Counter-reading: size or sector?

Size may not be the only explanation of the size gap. If enterprises are spread across sectors unequally between classes, part of the visible class gap may come from sector differences.

Sector figures cover all enterprises with at least 10 employees together. In 2025 the share using AI was 62.52% in information and communication, 40.43% in professional, scientific and technical activities, 17.27% in manufacturing, 11.98% in accommodation and food service, 11.15% in transportation and storage and 10.79% in construction. The difference between information and communication and construction is 51.73 points, which is larger than the 38.03-point difference between the small and the large class in the EU.

Placing these two results side by side, we cannot say how much size and sector each determine separately. In the tables Eurostat publishes, sector and size do not cross: there is no cell for large enterprises in information and communication. So part of the class gap may come from the sector mix. This article can neither confirm nor rule that out.

## What does this data not measure?

First comes coverage. The series includes all activities except agriculture, forestry, fishing, mining and quarrying, and the financial sector. Enterprises with fewer than 10 employees are outside it. That leaves out the smallest part of the enterprise population.

Second, definition. Using means using at least one of the listed technologies for any purpose. An enterprise that did text analysis once and one that runs many functions with AI fall into the same box. This data gives neither the level of use nor spending nor effect.

Third, time. As described above, definitions and the list of technologies may have changed from year to year, and a new technology was added in 2025. Eurostat flags a break in the time series for the 10–49 class in the Netherlands in 2025, and in France in 2023, when its value was 4.72%; both countries are therefore left out of the 2023–2025 change table. Level comparisons (chart and ratios) still use the value for the Netherlands in 2025.

Fourth, sampling. The survey is a sample survey; yearly values can change, especially in the small class.

Fifth, self-reporting. The survey rests on what enterprises report themselves. What is measured is not use as such but an enterprise saying that it uses.

## How we calculated

We pulled all values from Eurostat's dataset on AI by size class of enterprise (isoc_eb_ai), and sector values from its dataset on AI by activity (isoc_eb_ain2). The measure is the percentage of enterprises in the given class that use AI technologies. The classes are enterprises with 10–49, 50–249 and 250 or more employees; coverage is non-financial activities; the EU total is the aggregate with Eurostat's current membership. Class weights and sample size come from Eurostat's methodological note. We pulled the values through Eurostat's data interface in October 2026, each with its own query; the dataset was last updated in June 2026.

Three kinds of calculation were done. Difference: the gap between two percentages, in points. Percentage change: the difference between the new and the old value divided by the old value, turned into a percentage. Ratio: the small-class share divided by the large-class share, as a percentage. Calculations used the two-decimal values in the source, and results were rounded to the digits shown. For example, the small-class share went from 6.41% to 17.0%; dividing that increase by 6.41% gives 165.2%.

The country comparison uses the 10–49 class. We chose the countries by hand: Denmark, Finland, the Netherlands, Germany, France, Italy, Türkiye, Poland and Romania, with the EU overall shown separately. The choice includes the highest and the lowest small-class values in the Eurostat table (Denmark and Romania) and the large economies; we added Türkiye because it is a meaningful point of comparison for readers in Türkiye, and Türkiye also appears in the Eurostat survey. Country comparisons therefore hold only for the countries listed. In the 2023–2025 comparison the Netherlands and France were left out because of the time-series break flag.
