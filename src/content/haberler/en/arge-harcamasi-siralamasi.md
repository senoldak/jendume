World Bank data cover R&D spending as a share of gross domestic product (GDP) for 84 countries in 2022. The value is 4.85% for South Korea and 3.49% for the United States. Türkiye's row reads 1.32%, which puts it thirtieth on this measure among the 84 countries of the full list. On the same measure, the World Bank's total for the members of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) is 2.94%.

It is easy to read the top of such a list and conclude that these countries do the most research. Look at the same countries through the number of researchers and the order changes. The United States is third on R&D share and nineteenth on researchers per million people. Denmark is tenth on R&D share and second on researchers. Singapore is nineteenth on R&D share and third on researchers.

This article puts the two rankings side by side. It looks at where they overlap, where they part, and where Türkiye stands on each measure. Here "R&D" means research and development: systematic work to create new knowledge, or to turn existing knowledge into new products and methods.

## What does each ranking measure?

The first measure is total spending on R&D carried out inside a country in a year, divided by GDP. The World Bank takes this series from data compiled by the Institute for Statistics of the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO). Spending by businesses, government, higher education and private non-profit bodies is included, and current and capital spending are counted together.

Researchers per million people are the second measure. The series again comes from the UNESCO Institute for Statistics. According to the World Bank's metadata note, researchers are counted in full-time equivalents: the hours actually spent on R&D are added up and divided by the hours a person conventionally works. Master's and doctoral students engaged in R&D count as researchers. The total is divided by the mid-year population and multiplied by one million.

These two measures divide by different things: one by the size of the economy, the other by the size of the population. So there is no reason for the two rankings to say the same thing. The number of countries with data also differs: in 2022, R&D share is available for 84 countries and researcher numbers for 82. Two countries near the top of the R&D share list have no researcher figure for the year 2022, and one of them is the United Kingdom. This is why, when we compare a country's place on both lists, we can only use countries where both series are filled in. The rank numbers in the text come from these full lists; the charts show only the countries we selected.

## The order changes when we count researchers

On researchers, the highest value is South Korea's: 9,435 per million people. Denmark (9,037), Singapore (8,782), Sweden (8,639) and Finland (8,073) follow. Looking at the top five of both rankings together, only two countries appear in both: South Korea and Sweden.

Per million people, the United States has 4,937 researchers; third on R&D share, the country is nineteenth on this measure. Japan's R&D share of 3.40% puts it fifth, but with 5,630 researchers per million people it is thirteenth on researchers. China is thirteenth on R&D share (2.49%) and fortieth on researchers (1,849 per million). Denmark goes the other way: tenth on R&D share (2.87%) and second on researchers. Norway shows a similar split: twenty-second on R&D share (1.55%) and sixth on researchers (7,395 per million).

South Korea, Sweden and Finland, high on R&D share, are also in the top five on researchers. Germany sits in similar places on both lists: eighth on R&D share (3.07%) and eleventh on researchers (5,802 per million people). But these two series do not show why the order differs in the countries where it does. The data say only this: the two rankings are not the same, and in some countries the gap is more than fifteen places.

One year's ranking is a snapshot. Over 2015–2022, the R&D share rose in all eight countries we selected.

South Korea went from 3.79% to 4.85% (1.06 points), the United States from 2.77% to 3.49% (0.72 points), China from 2.02% to 2.49% (0.47 points), Poland from 1.00% to 1.44% (0.44 points) and Türkiye from 0.97% to 1.32% (0.35 points). The rise was 0.23 points in Sweden, 0.19 points in Germany and 0.16 points in Japan. The country with the largest rise in points is South Korea; the smallest is Japan.

For Türkiye the series runs to the year 2023. The R&D share was 0.97% in 2015 and 1.42% in 2023, a rise of 0.45 points. Over the same period the OECD members total rose from 2.46% to 2.93%, a rise of 0.47 points. Türkiye's share rose while staying below the OECD members total; the gap was 1.62 points in 2022. In 2015 the same gap was 1.49 points; in 2023 it was 1.51 points. So while Türkiye's share rose, the gap stayed almost the same over this period. Poland is twenty-fifth on R&D share with a value of 1.44%; there are 0.12 points between it and Türkiye's 1.32%.

Here it helps to keep points and percentage changes apart. Türkiye's rise from 0.97% to 1.42% is about the same size in points as the rise in the OECD members total. The change column of the table in the third chart shows the percentage change of the share itself: over 2015–2022 it is 44.0% in Poland, 36.1% in Türkiye, 28.0% in South Korea, 26.0% in the United States, 23.3% in China, 19.5% across OECD members, 7.1% in Sweden, 6.6% in Germany and 4.9% in Japan. In points the rise is largest in South Korea and the United States, while in percentage terms it is larger in Poland and Türkiye; if a share starts small, the same rise in points is a larger jump in relative terms.

## Researchers in Türkiye rose fast, from a lower level

![Slope chart: The number of researchers rose in all eight selected countries, fastest in Türkiye: Year 2015: South Korea: 7,020, Denmark: 7,566, Sweden: 6,846, Finland: 6,856, Germany: 4,755, Japan: 5,198, United States: 3,950, Türkiye: 1,136; Year 2022: South Korea: 9,435, Denmark: 9,037, Sweden: 8,639, Finland: 8,073, Germany: 5,802, Japan: 5,630, United States: 4,937, Türkiye: 2,479.](/grafik/arge-harcamasi-siralamasi/graf-8.svg)
*Researchers per million people. Source: World Bank.*

Researchers per million people in Türkiye numbered 1,136 in 2015 and 2,479 in 2022. That is a rise of 118.2%. Over the same period the OECD members total went from 3,471 to 4,464, a rise of 28.6%. The rise was 34.4% in South Korea, 26.2% in Sweden, 25.0% in the United States, 22.0% in Germany, 19.4% in Denmark, 17.8% in Finland and 8.3% in Japan. In China it was 59.0%.

Türkiye's percentage rise is higher than that of every other country on this list. The level tells a different story: in 2022 the OECD members total is 1.8 times Türkiye's value, and South Korea's value is 3.8 times. Türkiye is thirty-fourth among 82 countries on this measure, while on R&D share it is thirtieth among 84 countries.

A percentage rise is sensitive to the starting level: the same number of extra people looks bigger in percentage terms when added to a small base. So the percentage rise and the level per million people should be read together. In Türkiye the rise is fast and the level is below the comparison values.

## The counter-reading: what a rank cannot say alone

One counter-reading is not to put too much weight on a single year. Around Türkiye, values are close: there are only 0.12 points between Türkiye and Poland in twenty-fifth place, and in such a range the rank can change with a small measurement difference. The R&D share moves from year to year; in Türkiye it was 1.40% in 2021, 1.32% in 2022 and 1.42% in 2023. So Türkiye's thirtieth place might come out differently with another year's data. We built the rankings for the year 2022, because the researcher series is filled in for fewer countries in the year 2023 than in the year 2022.

Another is the denominator of the share. The ratio depends on both R&D spending and GDP. A ranking by R&D share is therefore not a ranking of the countries that spend the most money on R&D.

Third comes the division by population. The measure shows researcher density relative to population, not a country's total research capacity.

Last, the two measures complement each other. One counts money, the other counts people. These two series do not show how a country’s research system is built.

## Limits of measurement

In its own note, the World Bank says that R&D data rest on national research surveys. It says the periodicity of these surveys, the institutions and sectors they cover, and the sampling and estimation methods can differ from country to country. So the values of two countries may not have been produced in exactly the same way, and small differences can look large in a ranking.

Data vintage matters too: in its source note the World Bank says the UNESCO Institute for Statistics data were published in February 2025, and later revisions may change these values. Some countries are missing. R&D share is published for 84 countries and researcher numbers for 82; countries without data appear nowhere in the ranking. When we say thirtieth, that is the place among the countries with data.

For its totals, the World Bank states that it uses a weighted-average method. We took the OECD members total as the World Bank's series; it is not an average published by the OECD itself. The two may not be the same.

Because researcher numbers are given in full-time equivalents, a country with many part-time researchers will show a value that differs from its head count. The rankings reflect total working hours, not head counts.

Finally, no ranking in this article carries a judgement about the quality of any country's research. Quality is something these series do not measure.

## How we calculated

- From the World Bank's World Development Indicators (WDI) database we took two series: R&D spending as a share of GDP (code GB.XPD.RSDV.GD.ZS) and researchers per million people (code SP.POP.SCIE.RD.P6). The original source of both is the UNESCO Institute for Statistics.
- We built the rankings for the year 2022. We did not count regional totals, that is, records that are not countries. We ordered countries by value from highest to lowest; the rank numbers are our own ordering, and we kept the full lists as a file.
- We calculated percentage rises from the values of the two years: the later value minus the earlier value, divided by the earlier value, times one hundred. We wrote differences in shares as differences between two values, in points. Ratios are one value divided by another.
- As the comparison value we used the World Bank's OECD members total. Values are rounded from the raw values in the source; calculations were done on the rounded values.
