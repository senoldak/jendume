Researchers have confirmed numerous planets beyond our solar system over decades of observation. A worldwide community of amateur astronomers has long contributed to this search by combing through public telescope data. Recently, advanced artificial intelligence tools have made it possible for amateur researchers to design and run their own data searches independently.

Individual contributors utilized AI models in separate studies to process vast amounts of celestial data. One researcher used Codex to examine observations of numerous stars from the Kepler telescope mission, while another chose the Astra model to analyze telescope data from millions of stars across the sky. Both searches successfully identified potential exoplanets, which the researchers then submitted to professional astronomers for independent assessment and verification.

The search algorithms flagged multiple signals in the public datasets, highlighting periodic changes in starlight. For instance, observations in the constellation Lyra revealed recurring dips in brightness that matched planetary transit signatures. An astronomer at the SETI Institute independently evaluated similar signals. Meanwhile, additional candidates derived from space telescope observations were sent to mission researchers for further technical review and follow-up analysis.

AI-driven analyses greatly facilitate the extraction of faint signals from massive datasets. Software agents scanning starlight curves can quickly report regular brightness changes that might otherwise escape human observation. However, definitively confirming the physical cause of a light curve dip can take considerable time. Astronomers continue to test these candidates independently to rule out alternative explanations and gather additional empirical observations.
