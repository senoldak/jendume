UK-based Co-op has reportedly begun using OpenAI models to continuously record and monitor customer service interactions. The AI system assesses more than 50 different aspects of each phone call to assign pass and fail scores. Employees report being monitored by AI for up to several hours each day, describing the system as oppressive and dystopian. According to staff statements, the monitoring goes far beyond occasional compliance checks, capturing absolutely every word uttered by workers during their interactions.

This deployment highlights how AI is reshaping the modern workplace. In the United States, an estimated 61 percent of workplaces already use AI analytics software to gauge worker productivity. Workplace surveillance trends extend to other sectors as well; earlier, Burger King introduced AI headsets for staff to monitor interaction friendliness, and Meta briefly tested a tool tracking worker keystrokes and screens before pausing the initiative. Co-op defended the technology, stating it serves only as a support tool for quality assurance rather than an autonomous decision-maker.

However, labor experts remain highly critical of these automated management practices. John Chadfield from the Communication Workers Union argued that AI diminishes sensible workplace practices and intensifies working lives without justification, noting that accountable computer systems should not act as managers and that hardworking employees deserve greater respect.

As businesses increasingly adopt AI for operational optimization, balancing efficiency, privacy, and worker respect remains a central ethical challenge for the evolving workforce, leaving the future of algorithmic management open to widespread scrutiny.
