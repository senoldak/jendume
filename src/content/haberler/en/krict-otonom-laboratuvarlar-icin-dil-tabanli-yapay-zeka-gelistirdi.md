Researchers at the Korea Research Institute of Chemical Technology have developed a human-in-the-loop optimization framework for self-driving laboratories. The system utilizes large language models to translate what experts describe naturally into structured prior information for experimental optimization. Conventional automated facilities frequently face initial knowledge gaps, forcing systems to rediscover established chemical principles through repeated trials, which proves especially expensive during catalyst synthesis. To resolve this hurdle, the study combined large language models with Bayesian optimization techniques.

Testing within simulated environments driven by actual data revealed substantial performance gains. Regarding tests measuring target metrics, trials needed for specific multicomponent reactions fell down from roughly 30 to fewer than 10. Similarly, evaluations involving conductive composite materials saw necessary test iterations drop from approximately 20 down to a range between three and five. Such outcomes provide efficiency gains equivalent to having pre-existing experimental records, sparing researchers extensive preliminary exploration time during complex catalyst testing phases.

The team further incorporated an adaptive validation mechanism designed to prevent misleading expert inputs from distorting automated optimization paths. This component actively oversees alignment between prior expectations and incoming test outputs, systematically turning off faulty assumptions once conflicting evidence surfaces. Additionally, an interactive workspace allows scientists to modify testing parameters dynamically via conversational prompts while algorithms run optimization cycles.

Project leads noted that the resulting framework acts simultaneously as a collaborative research partner and as a central computing core directing autonomous laboratory functions. Investigators intend to proceed with physical testing validations utilizing actual laboratory hardware setups soon.
