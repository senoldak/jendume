In August 2022, the energy price index of the International Monetary Fund (IMF) stood at 376.4 (set to 100 at its level in 2016). In December 2020 it was 118.5, so it had risen 217.6% in twenty months. Over those same twenty months, from December 2020 to August 2022, the index of the energy prices households pay in the European Union (EU) rose 55.8%. The household index reached its highest level, 67.5% above December 2020, two months after the world index peaked, in October 2022. In this article “highest level” holds for the window from December 2020 to December 2024; months outside it are not in the article.

That the two series part ways within the same window shows that the world market price and the price a household pays do not measure the same thing. Electricity, gas and fuel items did not move at the same pace or in the same months either; some items peaked before the world index, others after it.

This article sets the two series side by side. For household prices we used the harmonised index of consumer prices (HICP) of Eurostat, the statistical office of the European Union; for world prices we used the IMF energy index. Setting them side by side makes no causal claim; it only shows which movement was seen in which month.

## The two prices do not measure the same thing

The IMF energy index is a weighted average of world market prices of energy products such as crude oil, natural gas and coal. The weights follow global import shares over 2014–2016, and the index is set to 100 at its level in 2016. All prices are in US dollars.

On the household side, the EU's HICP measures the retail prices households pay. Eurostat builds the energy total from electricity, gas and other fuels together with fuels for personal transport; in this article we also show one item separately, which Eurostat calls liquid fuels and fuels for personal transport equipment and which we call liquid and transport fuels. Eurostat publishes the index set to 100 at its level in 2025; to compare the two series we rebased both so that the base month is December 2020, set to 100. We state the base used in every place it matters.

There are three differences between the measures. The first is coverage: the household index covers the items households consume, the world index wholesale product prices. The second is currency: the world index is in dollars and the household price in euros. Between December 2020 and August 2022 the euro fell 16.8% against the dollar, which means a rise in dollars looks larger in euros. The third is the links in between: how a wholesale price becomes a retail price depends on contracts, tariffs and taxes. The series in this article do not separate those links.

We did not read the two series as levels on one axis; we compared them as indices recalculated from the same starting month. What is set side by side is then relative change since the start, not a difference in level; which series has the higher level says nothing here.

## 2021–2022: price rises accelerated first in gas and electricity

Start with annual averages (Eurostat HICP, average change against the previous year). In 2021 the average price rise across all items in the EU was 2.9%; in the energy item it was 12.6%. In 2022, those rates reached 9.2% for all items, 35.2% for energy and 29.5% for liquid and transport fuels. The largest jump was in gas, from 10.6% to 55.2%. In electricity it was from 9.4% to 31.8%.

Among the energy items, liquid and transport fuels had the largest rise in 2021: 17.9%. Food rose too, from 1.9% to 10.2%, while the rise in services stayed smaller, from 1.9% to 4.4%. These series do not show how the items affected one another. They only show which item rose how much in the same period.

Looking at monthly annual rates, energy prices in June 2022 were 41.1% higher than in the same month a year earlier. In October 2022 the rate was 38.7%.

Taking December 2020 as the base, the two series follow different paths.

A first high came in March 2022, at 331.8, and the window's highest level in August 2022, at 376.4. From August to December 2022 the world index fell 31.3%. The household energy index (set to 100 at its level in 2025), by contrast, kept rising until October 2022: 107.6 in September 2022 and 112.56 in October. In December 2022 it stood at 105.0, down 6.7% from October.

By June 2023 the world index, at 139.9, had fallen below the household index (148.5). In March 2024 the two series met at the same value, 151.7; in December 2024 the world index was 149.7 and the household index 149.4. From December 2020 to December 2024 the world index rose 49.7% and the household index 49.4%. That equality looks like a coincidence: with another starting month or another month in between, for example 172.7 against 154.3 in September 2023, the picture would be different.

## Peaks in different months, at different heights

Within the window, take the peak of each series, meaning its highest monthly value between December 2020 and December 2024. The world index rose 217.6% from December 2020 to its peak. Among household items the largest rise to the peak was in gas: 111.1% from December 2020 to October 2022. Liquid and transport fuels reached the window's highest level in June 2022, a rise of 69.7%. Electricity was highest in October 2022, a rise of 56.9%. The total energy item reached its highest level in October 2022, a rise of 67.5%.

Put the starting and peak values side by side: the world index went from 118.5 to 376.4; the household energy index (set to 100 at its level in 2025) from 67.19 to 112.56, gas from 58.78 to 124.09, electricity from 68.53 to 107.51 and liquid and transport fuels from 72.67 to 123.29. Gas is the household item with the largest rise to the peak, yet its rise was still below that of the world index.

Over the same window, from December 2020 to August 2022, we also calculated every series. The world index rose 217.6% and the household energy index 55.8%. Among the items, gas rose 81.4%, liquid and transport fuels 53.9% and electricity 46.1%.

Timing differs as well. Liquid and transport fuels reached the window's highest level two months before the world index peak, in June 2022. Gas and electricity did so two months after it, in October 2022. So the peak of that item came before the world index peak, and those of gas and electricity after it. This order is not evidence of causation; it only shows in which months the peaks fell.

## Energy inside inflation

Energy's place in overall inflation is a separate question. In the euro area, annual consumer inflation in October 2022 was 10.6%. Of this, 4.44 points came from energy, 2.74 points from food (including alcohol and tobacco), 1.62 points from non-energy industrial goods and 1.82 points from services. The four contributions add up to 10.62 points. Energy's share of that total is 41.8%. Energy's contribution was larger than the 2.74 points from food and the 1.82 points from services.

Here we used the euro area rather than the EU, because Eurostat publishes the contribution series for the euro area. The euro area is a subset of the EU; the figures may not match the EU totals exactly.

## Counter-readings and measurement limits

One counter-reading is that the order of the peaks may be chance. The window holds a single wave; one example does not make a rule, so we do not give a single number for the delay. The two months are only the difference between the peak months of this wave.

An annual rate and an index level are not the same thing either. The annual rate reached 41.1% in June 2022; in October 2022 it had slipped to 38.7%, yet the index level, at 112.56, was at the window's highest point. The annual rate measures change against a year earlier, not the level; the two can peak in different months.

Another point is the starting month. We chose December 2020 as the base, and the percentage rises are calculated from that base. With another month the percentages change. We ended the window in December 2024; later months are not in the article, and our peak statements hold only for this window.

Third is coverage: the contribution series is for the euro area only, the other series are for the EU. Fourth is classification: Eurostat has published the HICP under a new classification (the second edition of ECOICOP) since 2026 and gives rates under it since 2021. We took the household series from it; earlier months are not in this classification. The contribution series stays in the old classification and ends in 2025.

## How we calculated

We took the world energy index from the IMF's Primary Commodity Price System (PCPS), monthly, with the world country code and the energy index indicator. We took the household price from Eurostat's HICP data: the EU total, and the energy, electricity, gas and liquid and transport fuel items, as index and annual rate. The euro area contribution series and the dollar exchange rate also come from Eurostat.

- For the series rebased with December 2020 as the base, we divided the value of each quarter-end month by the value for December 2020, and multiplied by 100. The rebased series are our own transformation; the IMF and Eurostat do not publish them on this base.
- We calculated the rise to the peak as the percentage change from the value for December 2020 to the peak value of each series; in the same-window comparison the end month is August 2022 for every series.
- We calculated the sum of the contributions and energy's share ourselves; Eurostat's annual rate is 10.6%, and our sum is 10.62 points.
- We recorded every value in the evidence file with the single-value query address of its source.
