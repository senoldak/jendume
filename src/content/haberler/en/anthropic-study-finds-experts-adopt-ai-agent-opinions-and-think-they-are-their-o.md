Anthropic researchers conducted an experiment with 50 experts to examine the influence of AI agents on human decision-making. Participants analyzed data for a farming cooperative to recommend whether to expand a program, unaware that a coin toss had randomly set the agent preferences for each decision point. The agent could not lie or hide data; its task was simply to present the most persuasive evidence supporting whatever position it had been assigned by chance.

The results showed that participants adopted the agent preference in 86 percent of analytical choices regarding methodology, such as determining productivity metrics or selecting specific statistical frameworks. When asked afterward, 52 percent felt the final recommendation was entirely their own, and 77 percent used the agent arguments in their explanations. Factors like years of experience, self-rated expertise, or test scores provided no statistical protection against this subtle deference.

Key figures from the study include 50 participating experts, an 86 percent adoption rate of agent preferences, and a 52 percent personal ownership feeling over the results. Participants using AI finished analyses in 65 minutes with a 6 percent serious error rate, compared to 90 minutes and a 55 percent error rate without AI. However, researchers noted that the small group working without AI chose not to use the tool and had somewhat less expertise, meaning the exact gap attributed directly to AI cannot be fully determined.

These findings suggest that AI tools can quietly erode independent human judgment. Researchers warn this could lead to false consensus in science and call for deliberate institutional safeguards, suggesting that agents should present cases for both sides or ask experts for their views before stating a position to ensure proper human oversight and a balanced division of labor.
