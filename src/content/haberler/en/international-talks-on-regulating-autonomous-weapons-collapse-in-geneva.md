International efforts to establish a legally binding treaty on the use of autonomous weapons systems have collapsed at the Review Conference on Conventional Weapons in Geneva. After a week of intense negotiations, the conference failed to reach a consensus on a mandate for a treaty, primarily due to objections from Russia and resistance from several major military powers regarding legally binding obligations.

The outcome halts the global attempt to regulate AI-driven warfare. More than 100 nations had pushed for a binding framework to mitigate the risks posed by autonomous drones and AI-enabled targeting systems to civilians. However, the requirement for unanimous consensus within the UN forum effectively stalled the process, preventing the adoption of any legal restrictions.

Major defence technology powers, including the US, South Korea, Japan, Turkey, the UK, France, and Germany, expressed reluctance toward a binding protocol, advocating instead for non-binding agreements. Experts warn that AI-enabled targeting systems are already being deployed in active conflicts, blurring the lines between military operations and civilian life.

Reports indicate that the US military is aiming to achieve a capacity of 1,000 AI-assisted targeting decisions per hour. Furthermore, it was reported that over 1,000 targets were struck in Iran on the first day of the conflict using such systems. With discussions on this issue spanning 13 years, the collapse shows the widening gap between the rapid advancement of military AI and the slow pace of international regulation.

The inability to reach an agreement reflects the challenges in establishing ethical and safety boundaries for AI in warfare. As these systems become more prevalent, the risk of fully autonomous combat scenarios without human oversight continues to grow. The international community now faces the task of finding alternative pathways to regulate these lethal technologies before their use becomes standard practice.
