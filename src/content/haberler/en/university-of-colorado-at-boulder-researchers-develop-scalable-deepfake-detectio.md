Researchers at the University of Colorado at Boulder have developed a novel detection system designed to spot AI-generated deepfakes during video conferencing with drastically reduced computing requirements. Unlike commercially available systems that demand massive computational power, the new method analyzes compressed video data directly without decompressing every single frame.

Bad actors have increasingly used deepfakes to impersonate corporate officers and trick employees into transferring funds, such as a high-profile case in Hong Kong involving twenty-five million dollars. Major video conferencing platforms like Zoom, Apple FaceTime, and Google Meet currently lack real-time deepfake detection, while existing safeguards remain too expensive and difficult to scale for everyday users.

The research team discovered that clues to deepfakes already exist within the compressed data traveling across the network. By measuring how compression blocks change across frames, the system only needs to look at data from one out of every one hundred frames rather than every single image. A full AI check is triggered only when suspicious activity is detected.

During tests on a library of one thousand online videos containing deepfakes, the system demonstrated near-perfect accuracy while utilizing one hundred forty-three times less computing power than current models. The team aims to adapt the technology so it can eventually be scaled up for widespread use across large-scale video conferencing platforms.
