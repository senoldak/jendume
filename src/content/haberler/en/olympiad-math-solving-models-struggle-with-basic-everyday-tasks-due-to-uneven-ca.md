Artificial intelligence models from leading research labs can successfully solve complex mathematical problems while making surprising mistakes on basic everyday tasks. Researchers refer to this pattern as jagged intelligence, noting that models perform extremely well in certain structured domains while failing badly in others. Andrej Karpathy coined the term to describe how models achieve high performance in specific areas while exhibiting unpredictable failures elsewhere.

Unlike human abilities that tend to develop and correlate together from childhood to adulthood, capabilities in artificial intelligence models often do not move in tandem. A model that writes a working function may fail to count words in a simple paragraph, proving that impressive demonstrations or single benchmark scores predict very little about upcoming tasks. Various theories attempt to explain this unevenness across systems and workloads.

Studies from institutions such as Apple and MIT suggest that large language models often replicate reasoning steps from training data rather than applying genuine logical reasoning. For instance, models can correctly define a concept or a rhyme scheme while failing classification tasks or application steps on the exact same concepts. Additional tests involving analog clocks and grade-school word problems further demonstrate how minor changes in input formatting lead to severe performance drops.

This gap between benchmark success and real-world performance complicates how developers and users evaluate artificial intelligence systems. High scores in controlled environments do not always translate to practical reliability, underscoring the need for more comprehensive evaluations of capabilities. Understanding these underlying limitations remains essential for anyone deploying models in real-world scenarios where failures carry significant consequences.
