The AI detection tool Pangram has identified 99 percent of the award-winning novel "Donde el agua trabaja," written by Peruvian author David Silva Rojas, as AI-generated content. Despite these claims, the publisher Almadía has chosen to support the author after reviewing documentation provided by him. This incident highlights the growing tension between the rise of AI-generated literature and the tools designed to police it. The controversy underscores the unreliability of AI detection software and the potential damage to authors' reputations. When these tools are used as the sole arbiter of authenticity, they risk mislabeling human creativity as machine-generated, creating a chilling effect in the literary world.

Beyond the novel, Pangram also flagged the author's interviews and social media denials as over 80 percent AI-generated. This high rate of false positives raises significant questions about the tool's accuracy. Critics argue that literary juries often favor clichéd narratives that large language models are particularly adept at mimicking, which complicates the detection process. Similar debates have emerged in other literary circles, where authors have faced accusations of AI usage, sometimes leading to complex disputes over awards and integrity.

While major publishing houses are preparing to implement Pangram for manuscript screening starting next year, professional writers' organizations warn against relying solely on these detectors for critical decisions. The organization representing writers emphasizes that these tools should not be the only basis for judging a work's origin, especially given that they have been shown to misidentify human-written texts in various tests. The debate reflects a broader struggle to define human authorship in an era where the lines between human and machine writing are increasingly blurred. As the publishing industry integrates these technologies, the challenge lies in balancing the need for authenticity with the risk of unfairly penalizing human writers.
