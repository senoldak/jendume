Think of a school's enrolment register, in which the children who officially belong in a given grade are counted. Every child of that age may be in the register. Even so, the total number of children written in it can be larger than the number of children of that age. The reason is that pupils who repeat a grade, who started late or who started early are in the same register too.

That is what a rate above one hundred means in education statistics. According to World Bank data, the world's gross primary enrolment rate in 2017 was 101.8%. In the same year the net rate, which counts only children of official school age, stood at 89.3%. Both measures cover the same schools, the same year and the same source; the 12.5-point gap between them comes from how each is defined.

This article follows that gap through two questions. First, how large is it, and how much does it vary from country to country? Second, what does the size of the gap tell us, and what does it not tell us? The data come from the World Bank's World Development Indicators (WDI); the underlying compiler of these series is the Institute for Statistics of the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO). We compare the countries as they stood in 2017, and we explain why below.

## Two rates, two different numerators

The gross enrolment rate divides all pupils enrolled at a level of education, whatever their age, by the population of the age group that officially corresponds to that level. The net enrolment rate takes only enrolled pupils within that official age group and divides them by the same population. The denominator is the same; the difference lies in the numerator.

By definition, then, the net rate reaches its highest value when the whole of its age group is in school. The gross rate has no such ceiling. As over-age or under-age pupils enter the numerator, the rate rises and passes one hundred without difficulty.

The World Bank's indicator note makes the same point: the gross ratio indicates the capacity of a level of education, but a high ratio may reflect a substantial number of over-age children in each grade because of repetition or late entry. The same note describes the net rate as the measure that more accurately captures a system's coverage. Another passage in the note says that a shorter duration of primary education tends to raise the rate and a longer one to lower it.

Three situations separate the gross rate from the net rate. The first is repetition: the same child is counted at the same level for several years and moves beyond the official age range. The second is late entry. The third is early entry: a child younger than the official age is enrolled too. All three enter the numerator and none enters the denominator. This series does not tell us which of them dominates in which country; that would require the age distribution of enrolments.

Choosing a measure is therefore a choice in itself. Which rate you use can change where a country appears in a table.

## Primary school: countries above one hundred

Start with primary school. Among the countries whose net rate was also published in 2017, the highest gross primary rate belongs to Rwanda, at 139.3%. Its net rate is 96.9%. In Nepal the gross rate is 135.3% and the net rate 96.3%; in Seychelles 132.2% and 92.0%. In Namibia the figures are 125.9% and 97.3%, in Sweden 122.9% and 99.2%.

*Primary school enrolment, gross and net, in 2017, per cent. Source: World Bank.*

What the rows have in common is that the net rates sit close together. The net rates of the five countries lie between 92.0% and 99.2%. The gross rates, by contrast, spread out between 122.9% and 139.3%. In these countries most children of official age are enrolled; what pushes the gross rate far above one hundred is enrolment outside that age group.

For comparison we added two group values and Türkiye. For OECD members, the weighted average gives a gross rate of 102.4% and a net rate of 95.6%. For the world the figures are 101.8% and 89.3%. In Türkiye they are 97.5% and 87.9%. In Türkiye the gross rate is below one hundred and the net rate is below the gross rate; but the gap is smaller than in the countries with a high primary gross rate.

Türkiye's primary net rate is 7.7 points below that for OECD members, as a weighted average. Placing the two values side by side is enough to read the measure; the data cannot tell us why.

## How wide is the gap?

If we compute directly how many points the gross rate sits above the net rate, the picture shifts a little. The gap does not move in step with the height of the gross rate.

![Bar chart: Among the countries shown, the primary-school gap between gross and net rates is largest in Rwanda: Gross − net: Rwanda: 42.4, Nepal: 39.0, Seychelles: 40.2, Namibia: 28.6, Sweden: 23.7, OECD members: 6.8, World: 12.5, Türkiye: 9.6.](/grafik/okullasma-brut-ve-net/graf-6.svg)
*Gross rate minus net rate, in 2017, percentage points. Source: World Bank.*

Rwanda's gap is 42.4 points; in Nepal 39.0 points, in Seychelles 40.2 points, in Namibia 28.6 points, in Sweden 23.7 points. For the world it is 12.5 points, and for OECD members 6.8 points. In Türkiye it is 9.6 points.

A small gap does not mean high enrolment; it only shows that the two measures lie close together. Türkiye's gap of 9.6 points likewise describes two measures standing close together where the gross rate is below one hundred; it does not describe how high enrolment is.

## At secondary level the gap grows

At secondary level the gap is much wider than in primary school in some countries.

![Table: At secondary level the gap between gross and net rates is much wider than in primary school in some countries: Gap: Belgium: 63.8, Finland: 57.5, Australia: 61.3, Türkiye: 25.8, OECD: 17.4, Germany: 15.8, World: 8.7, US: 6.5; Vs primary: Belgium: 59.8, Finland: 56.6, Australia: 57.1, Türkiye: 16.2, OECD: 10.6, Germany: 4.2, World: −3.8, US: −0.7.](/grafik/okullasma-brut-ve-net/graf-7.svg)
*Secondary education, gross minus net rate, in 2017, percentage points; right column: difference from the primary gap. Source: World Bank.*

Belgium's secondary gross rate is 158.7% and the net rate 94.9%, a gap of 63.8 points. In Finland the figures are 153.6% and 96.1%, a gap of 57.5 points. In Australia they are 153.6% and 92.3%, a gap of 61.3 points. In all three the net rate is high and the gross rate is far above one hundred. That the secondary gap in these countries exceeds the primary gap can be read in the right-hand column of the table: it is wider by 59.8 points in Belgium, 56.6 points in Finland and 57.1 points in Australia.

For the world the picture is reversed. The world's secondary gross rate is 74.9% and the net rate 66.2%, a gap of 8.7 points. The primary gap was 12.5 points, so worldwide the primary gap is 3.8 points wider than the secondary one. In the United States the gross rate is 99.0% and the net rate 92.5%, a gap of 6.5 points; the primary gap is 0.7 points wider.

Türkiye's secondary gross rate is 113.0% and the net rate 87.2%, a gap of 25.8 points. For OECD members the figures are 106.7% and 89.3%, a gap of 17.4 points. Türkiye's gross rate is 6.3 points above this weighted average and its net rate 2.1 points below it. In Germany the figures are 101.1% and 85.3%, a gap of 15.8 points.

These numbers do not make one country better or worse than another. A wide gap says that, at secondary level, many enrolments lie outside the official age group; the series does not give the reason.

## How did the gap change in seventeen years?

We set the gap between the gross and net rates in the year 2000 and the gap in the year 2017. In WDI many countries and groups have all four values for both years; from among them we chose seven groups and countries.

![Slope chart: The primary-school gap between gross and net rates narrowed in some groups and widened in others in 2000–2017: in 2000: World: 15.7, Türkiye: 9.4, European Union: 6.3, North America: 5.2, Latin America and Caribbean: 21.5, Least developed countries: 16.4, South Asia: 23.2; in 2017: World: 12.5, Türkiye: 9.6, European Union: 4.6, North America: 6.4, Latin America and Caribbean: 13.4, Least developed countries: 20.0, South Asia: 29.4.](/grafik/okullasma-brut-ve-net/graf-8.svg)
*Gross rate minus net rate, primary, percentage points. Source: World Bank.*

Worldwide the gap was 15.7 points in 2000 and fell to 12.5 points in 2017. Over the same period the world's net rate rose from 83.4% to 89.3%, an increase of 5.9 points. The gross rate rose from 99.1% to 101.8%, an increase of 2.7 points. Because the net rate rose faster than the gross rate, the gap narrowed.

Latin America and the Caribbean saw the gap fall from 21.5 points to 13.4 points, and the European Union from 6.3 points to 4.6 points. In Latin America and the Caribbean the route was this: the primary gross rate fell from 115.7% to 107.1% while the net rate slipped from 94.2% to 93.7%. On the other hand, in South Asia the gap rose from 23.2 points to 29.4 points, and in the least developed countries (a United Nations classification) from 16.4 points to 20.0 points. In North America it rose from 5.2 points to 6.4 points and in Türkiye from 9.4 points to 9.6 points.

Over the same period, the two measures moved closer together in some groups and farther apart in others. In South Asia the primary gross rate rose from 98.6% to 117.0%; the gap widened along with that rise.

## A difference in definition and source

Data limits show here too. By definition the net rate should not exceed the gross rate: the denominator is the same, and the numerator of the net rate is a part of the numerator of the gross rate. According to the World Bank's source notes the two series come through different publication channels; in both, population data come from the United Nations Population Division.

When comparing the two rates row by row, the difference in source and publication is therefore worth keeping in mind. We only place published values side by side and calculate the differences between them; these series do not let us work out why a difference arises.

Another limit is the time coverage of the net rate. The net enrolment series covers a wide range of countries until 2017, and after that the number of countries drops markedly. That is why we chose the year 2017. For a later year we cannot place the two measures side by side.

## A counter-reading: what does a high gross rate say?

One reading runs against the findings of this article: for some readers a high gross rate is an indicator of coverage. As a measure of a system's capacity that is not wrong; the World Bank's note describes the gross rate for exactly this purpose.

But the warning in the note sits in the same place: a high ratio may reflect repetition or late entry. The two readings do not exclude each other. The gross rate speaks to capacity and the net rate to coverage. Reading them together is less misleading than reading either alone.

A further counter-reading concerns age boundaries, which differ from country to country. The length of education and the starting age vary; the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) tries to minimise this difference, according to the note, but does not remove it entirely. A difference between countries can therefore carry a difference in definitions as well.

## Uncertainty and measurement limits

This article shows two years, in 2000 and in 2017, and one cross-section. We do not see whether the gap moved smoothly or erratically in the years between; the year-by-year movement within individual countries is outside the scope of this article.

Enrolment rates rest on annual school surveys and, according to the World Bank's note, do not necessarily reflect actual attendance or dropout during the year. Being enrolled does not mean attending class. According to the same note, where birth registration is not strictly enforced in a community, age may be misestimated or misstated, and that affects the net rate directly.

Group values here (the world, OECD members, the European Union, regions) are World Bank weighted averages. When a country belongs to a group, the group value should not be read as independent of that country's value. The rows we show are a selection: five countries whose primary gross rate is far above one hundred, plus Türkiye and the groups; at secondary level the three countries with the highest gross rate, plus Türkiye, Germany, the United States and the groups. Other countries could have been chosen.

## How we calculated

Four series come from the World Bank's WDI database, with a separate query for each country and year: primary gross (SE.PRM.ENRR), primary net (SE.PRM.NENR), secondary gross (SE.SEC.ENRR) and secondary net (SE.SEC.NENR). All four carry a Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY) licence; the underlying compiler is the UNESCO Institute for Statistics.

Our comparison year is the year 2017: the last year in which the net rate series keeps its wide country coverage. For the comparison between periods we took the year 2000 and the year 2017.

Gap arithmetic is simple: gross rate minus net rate, in percentage points, rounded to one decimal. The change from primary to secondary is the difference between two gaps. The rise in the world's net rate and in its gross rate is the difference between the values in 2000 and in 2017. We worked from the rounded values the World Bank publishes, to one decimal; calculated from raw values, some gaps could change in the last decimal. The group values come ready-made from the World Bank; we did not compute any averages.

No value is a forecast or a series we produced ourselves. We do not answer the question of why; we only show how two official measures stand in the same period.
