Researchers Owen Queen and James Zou from Stanford University evaluated fifty widely cited assertions concerning large language models by comparing them across twenty distinct systems released between July 2024 and September 2026. Their findings revealed that forty-four percent of these claims have already expired. The study highlights that the rapid pace of artificial intelligence development is constantly outrunning the research meant to explain it.

According to the study, among findings carrying an anticipated expiration timeline, the mean duration of validity was merely 15.7 months. Following a one-year period, sixteen percent of the claims had expired, and after two years, that figure climbed to thirty-nine percent. Claims regarding what models cannot accomplish were found to lose validity the fastest. Well-known weaknesses, such as counting letters or performing arithmetic in unfamiliar systems, quickly disappear as developers train models toward clear targets.

In contrast, findings about preferences and behavioral tendencies proved to be longer-lasting. Traits such as sycophancy toward users and political biases tend to persist across different generations of models. As a case study, the researchers looked at the fabrication of scientific citations, noting a significant drop in reference fabrication among newer models compared to older versions, though some variations remain across different developers.

The researchers conclude that empirical findings about large language models should be presented as observations of specific models at a given point in time rather than general truths. They emphasize the importance of documenting specific model versions, dates, and publishing analysis codes so findings can be reliably re-tested on future systems.
