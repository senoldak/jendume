As the debate over artificial intelligence and the labor market continues, historical precedents provide valuable context. The transition of telephone switch operators and the introduction of personal computers illustrate how technological waves impact female employment. Research indicates that because artificial intelligence targets routine tasks, administrative and clerical jobs held largely by women face significant exposure. Economic studies consistently highlight how major technological shifts alter the structure of the workforce.

Decades ago, the introduction of automated telephone switches by AT&T eliminated tens of thousands of operator jobs. Research by economists James Feigenbaum and Daniel P. Gross shows that while incumbent workers suffered career disruptions, subsequent generations of workers successfully adapted to evolving labor markets. Similar transformations followed the advent of washing machines and personal computers, which reduced unpaid housework and allowed women to enter the paid labor force in growing numbers.

Data from the International Labour Organization indicates that nearly one in ten women in high-income countries works in occupations at the highest risk from artificial intelligence. With a large majority of secretaries and clerical workers being women, the rise of artificial intelligence presents both structural challenges and adaptation opportunities. Experts note that artificial intelligence may eliminate specific tasks rather than entire jobs, enhancing overall worker productivity.

Much like past technological revolutions, the ability of the workforce to adapt to emerging tools will shape future employment patterns. When personal computers and word processing software were introduced, clerical roles evolved, and workers who embraced the new technologies found ways to remain indispensable. The ongoing deployment of artificial intelligence is expected to trigger a similar adaptation cycle across various administrative and professional sectors.
