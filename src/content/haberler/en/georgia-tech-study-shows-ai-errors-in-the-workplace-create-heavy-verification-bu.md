A study by Karen Feigh, a professor of cognitive engineering at the Georgia Institute of Technology, reveals that promised workplace productivity gains from generative AI are being wiped out by the effort required to clean up model outputs. The time and energy spent correcting inaccuracies and hallucinations offset the speed advantages offered by automated tools.

Feigh highlights a concept known as the "verification burden," which describes the wasted time humans spend verifying the accuracy of machine output. According to Feigh, this burden represents the cognitive cost of attempting to regain authority over work delegated to an automated system while still retaining full personal responsibility for the final output.

Findings from a recent Stanford University survey reinforce this perspective, showing that employees forced to use AI tools often produce low-effort outputs. While these drafts initially resemble passable memos or reports, they inevitably demand substantial follow-up verification from colleagues down the line. Analyses indicate that workers who review AI outputs before submission waste more time than colleagues who refrain from using AI altogether.

Workplace verification tasks differ fundamentally from traditional creation. While creating a presentation manually offers a clear endpoint, auditing an AI-generated document lacks obvious boundaries. Employees cannot predict whether errors exist, where they might occur, or what form they will take, making the verification process significantly more onerous.
