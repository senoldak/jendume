The creator of the popular augmented reality game Pokémon Go, Niantic, is pivoting hard into one of Silicon Valley's hottest AI frontiers: world models. According to Brian McClendon, chief technology officer at Niantic Spatial, the company aims to reconstruct the real world using its vast troves of spatial data. Unlike text-based large language models, world models take visual inputs to build 3D virtual spaces that replicate physical laws.

This technology is set to accelerate the deployment of home robotics and driverless cars. Oliver Cameron, CEO of Odyssey, explains that while language models predict the next word in a sentence, world models predict the next visual state based on images. These digital spaces act as virtual training grounds, allowing machines to run millions of tests without physical movement.

Autonomous vehicle startups like Wayve are utilizing generative world models powered by dashcam footage to recreate hazardous road conditions that are unsafe for real-world testing. Chief scientist Jamie Shotton notes that these models must understand physics, cause and effect, and human responses. Beyond robotics and transport, major gaming entities like Tencent are also investing heavily in the technology.

Investors are pouring enormous sums into computer science pioneers working on world models. Yann LeCun recently raised over a billion dollars for his startup, while Stanford computer scientist Fei-Fei Li launched a startup that was acquired by AMD this year for over $8 billion.

Meanwhile, feeding these physical simulations requires massive amounts of real-world video data. AI labs are increasingly relying on everyday people recording household chores like washing dishes and folding laundry to gather training data. As companies strive to build better simulations, this trend is reshaping the gig economy behind the scenes.
