Researchers at York University have challenged a long-standing assumption about how closely artificial intelligence models resemble the human brain. For years, the scientific community has relied on artificial neural networks (ANNs) to understand how the brain processes visual information and recognizes objects. However, a new study reveals that while these models can successfully imitate brain activity, they use entirely different internal strategies to achieve those results.

To uncover this disconnect, the research team developed a method called 'reverse predictivity testing.' Traditional evaluations typically ask whether an AI model can predict the activity of neurons in the brain. The research team reversed this approach, investigating whether recorded brain activity could predict what was happening inside the AI models. They reasoned that if these systems truly operated like the brain, the relationship should be bidirectional. This diagnostic metric provides a more rigorous way to evaluate how 'brain-like' a computational model actually is.

The study involved testing responses to 1,320 natural photos and realistic computer-generated images featuring 10 common subjects, such as bears, elephants, and faces. These objects were presented in various styles, including outlines, drawings, and artistic interpretations. The results were striking: while AI models were generally good at predicting neuronal responses, those same neuronal responses were much less effective at predicting the internal features of the AI models. This finding has significant implications for neuroscience and psychology. AI is increasingly used to design experiments and study neurological conditions like post-traumatic stress disorder or autism. If the underlying processes of these models differ from actual primate brains, researchers could draw misleading conclusions. Rather than suggesting that AI models should be abandoned, the researchers believe these findings provide a way to make them more scientifically reliable. By identifying which parts of an AI model truly match brain activity, scientists can prioritize creating architectures that better represent the biological processes of perception.
