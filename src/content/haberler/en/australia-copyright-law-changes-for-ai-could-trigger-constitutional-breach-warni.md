Proposed changes to Australian copyright law that would allow artificial intelligence companies to scrape music, books, and journalism from the internet have raised serious legal concerns. Experts, senators, and music industry representatives warned that such legislative shifts could breach the Australian constitution and expose the government to massive compensation claims from rights holders worldwide. Coalition senators emphasized that the government should not sacrifice local creators in its rush to entice AI investment.

Anne Twomey, professor emerita of law at the University of Sydney, noted that granting AI companies permission to scrape copyrighted material could potentially amount to an acquisition of property by the government under section 51 of the constitution, triggering complex legal hurdles. Senator Ross Cadell warned that the move could leave the federal government liable for compensation to rights holders across the globe, potentially sparking history's largest compensation rights case. Representatives from the Australian recording industry and independent music companies also voiced strong opposition, arguing that current licensing frameworks are functioning effectively and that AI firms should utilize existing agreements rather than altering copyright protections.

As the federal government prepares for upcoming announcements regarding artificial intelligence, creative industries and legal experts continue to monitor the situation closely, warning of international ramifications if Australia weakens its copyright standards to accommodate AI training data. Efforts by AI companies to bypass traditional copyright for data training threaten to trigger major constitutional and global legal battles over intellectual property rights.
