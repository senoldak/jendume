Researchers from MIT, the University of British Columbia (UBC), and collaborating institutions have developed a unified deep-learning system named WorldParticle, capable of simulating multiple physical phenomena within a single architecture. Traditionally, engineers and scientists must write separate software tools hand-coded with specific equations for each physical regime, such as fluid dynamics or protein folding. WorldParticle replaces this fragmented approach by learning the underlying dynamics directly from data.

The system represents materials as thousands or millions of three-dimensional particles. Operating similarly to large language models that predict the next word in a sequence, WorldParticle predicts the positions and physical properties of particles step-by-step over time. By factoring in external forces and environmental conditions, the model generalizes effectively to materials and scenarios it was not exposed to during training.

During evaluation, the researchers tested WorldParticle across six different physical phenomena, including deformable objects, granular materials, and biological molecules. Notably, the AI model simulated changes in protein structures approximately 100 times faster than conventional numerical simulation methods. To handle the heavy computational load associated with tracking millions of particles, the team implemented a specialized particle merging and compression technique.

The development represents an important step toward creating general-purpose foundation models for scientific computing. The research team aims to expand WorldParticle to model complex interactions in soft robotics, RNA evolution, and astrophysical phenomena, offering new capabilities for scientific research.
