Meta and OpenAI are adopting a new strategy to make their personal AI assistants more appealing and trustworthy to users. Meta's 'Muse' assistant features a digital avatar named 'Jolly,' which resembles a blinking and waving teddy bear. Similarly, OpenAI has introduced 'Dots,' a family of brightly colored, globular mascot faces that float across screens to converse with users. This trend marks the rise of the 'cute economy' within the artificial intelligence sector.

The 'cutewashing' of AI agents aims to make complex and sometimes intimidating technology feel more accessible and comforting. However, scholars warn that this approach carries significant risks. When interacting with a cute character, users are more likely to share private information, moods, and daily routines that they might not disclose to a plain software tool. This allows companies to gather much richer data about users than traditional chatbots typically obtain.

The strategy leverages the natural human instinct to feel affection and trust toward cute entities. Research shows that people tend to be less skeptical when faced with a friendly character and are more likely to grant broad permissions without reading the fine print. For instance, Meta's Muse can manage emails, handle shopping, and control apps on a user's behalf. Yet, these capabilities also mean the agent can access private messages and location data, sometimes without the user's explicit awareness of the implications.

Ultimately, presenting AI assistants as 'digital pets' increases brand loyalty while potentially obscuring security concerns. Experts emphasize that users should question what data an agent can access before granting permissions. It is also argued that tech companies must explain these access rights in plain language rather than dense legalese to ensure ethical AI adoption. As AI becomes more integrated into daily life, distinguishing between a charming interface and the underlying data practices remains a vital challenge for consumers.
