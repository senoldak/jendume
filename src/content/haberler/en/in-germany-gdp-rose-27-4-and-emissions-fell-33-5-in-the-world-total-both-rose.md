Germany's gross domestic product (GDP), measured at constant prices, rose by 27.4% in 2000–2024. Over the same period the country's carbon dioxide emissions fell by 33.5%. Read side by side, the two numbers undo the intuition that economy and emissions must move together: in Germany the two curves separated.

This article asks the wider question: in which countries did emissions fall while the economy grew, in which did both rise, and how did the separation seen in individual countries show up in the world total? The data come from the indicator database of the World Bank. The World Bank compiles the emissions series from EDGAR, the database of the Joint Research Centre (JRC) of the European Commission; the metadata of the source also names the International Energy Agency (IEA) among the data providers; GDP is a constant-price series in United States dollars at the prices of the year 2015.

## Two measures, two separate results

The word decoupling can describe two different things. In absolute decoupling the economy grows while emissions themselves fall. In relative decoupling emissions also rise, but more slowly than the economy. The measure in this article is simple: we put the percentage change between the year 2000 and the year 2024 for GDP and for emissions side by side in the same country. On one side is a measure of the economy, on the other the carbon dioxide released into the atmosphere. Both may point the same way or apart; the measure only says which, not why.

GDP is at constant prices: because price rises are removed, the series follows the change in the quantity of goods and services produced. Emissions are given for carbon dioxide in million tonnes. These two series include the year 2024 in the update of October 2026.

We chose the countries ourselves. In building the list we aimed to show examples where emissions fell and where they rose: a list of large economies from Europe, North America, Asia and South America, with China and India included. There is no country from Africa or Oceania, and many other countries are left out. The list is not a distribution of all countries and not a random sample either: the results hold only for these countries, and other countries may show a different picture. In this article we call these countries the panel.

## Countries where emissions fell

*Change in GDP and carbon dioxide emissions, 2000–2024, per cent. Source: World Bank.*

On the chart the GDP of the United States rose by 65.7%; its emissions fell by 21.9%. In the United Kingdom GDP rose by 43.4% and emissions fell by 47.0%. In Poland GDP rose by 132.4% and emissions fell by 11.8%. In Germany GDP rose by 27.4% and emissions fell by 33.5%.

Three countries of the panel that are not in the chart point the same way: in Japan GDP rose by 16.8% and emissions fell by 21.8%; in France GDP rose by 34.3% and emissions fell by 31.5%; in Italy GDP rose by 9.6% and emissions fell by 34.1%. In these seven countries the two series moved in opposite directions.

What stands out is not a single direction but the gap between them: in Poland GDP rose by 132.4% and emissions again fell; in Italy the rise in GDP stayed small and the fall in emissions was large. The data do not say what lies behind these differences; they only show what happened over the same period.

Absolute values give a similar picture. Emissions of the United States were 5,928.8 million tonnes in the year 2000; in the year 2024 it fell to 4,632.2 million tonnes. In Japan emissions fell from 1,243.6 million tonnes to 972.3 million tonnes. In the United Kingdom they fell from 552.0 million tonnes to 292.4 million tonnes. In Poland they dropped from 313.0 million tonnes to 276.1 million tonnes. The GDP of the United States over the same period rose from 13,717.7 billion dollars to 22,731.5 billion dollars. The GDP of the United Kingdom rose from 2,314.3 billion dollars to 3,318.6 billion dollars, that of Japan from 4,031.0 billion dollars to 4,708.2 billion dollars and that of Poland from 283.2 billion dollars to 658.1 billion dollars. In none of these countries did the fall in emissions come together with a shrinking economy.

## Two curves in Germany

![Dot chart: In Germany the GDP index rose while the emissions index fell: GDP: 100.0 in 2000, 102.0 in 2004, 111.0 in 2008, 113.8 in 2012, 121.3 in 2016, 122.1 in 2020, 127.4 in 2024; Emissions: 100.0 in 2000, 99.1 in 2004, 95.2 in 2008, 91.8 in 2012, 90.6 in 2016, 73.7 in 2020, 66.5 in 2024.](/grafik/buyume-ve-emisyon-ayrismasi/graf-6.svg)
*GDP and carbon dioxide emissions, indexed to the year 2000, every four years. Source: World Bank.*

Divide both series by their value in the year 2000, set it to 100, and the paths of the curves become visible. In Germany the GDP index reached 127.4 in the year 2024; the emissions index fell to 66.5. In the points taken every four years the GDP index was 111.0 in the year 2008 and 121.3 in the year 2016; the emissions index fell to 95.2 and 90.6 in the same years.

A break seems to appear in the year 2020: the emissions index was 90.6 in the year 2016 and fell to 73.7 in the year 2020, while the GDP index rose from 121.3 to 122.1. The year 2020 is the pandemic period; reading that year on its own can mislead. The separation was visible before the year 2020 as well: in the year 2008 the GDP index was 111.0 while the emissions index was 95.2. The emissions index fell to 66.5 in the year 2024 and went below the level of the year 2020 as well.

Emissions in Germany were 871.4 million tonnes in the year 2000 and fell to 579.9 million tonnes in the year 2024; GDP rose from 2,886.0 billion dollars to 3,677.0 billion dollars.

## Emissions curves in the panel countries

*Carbon dioxide emissions, indexed to the year 2000, every four years. Source: World Bank.*

In the panel of countries the emissions index in the year 2024 is 78.1 in the United States, 53.0 in the United Kingdom, 68.5 in France, 78.2 in Japan, 88.2 in Poland and 65.9 in Italy. In every one of them the index is below 100. But the paths are not the same: the index of Japan was 105.2 in the year 2012, above 100, and the index of Italy had risen to 108.3 in the year 2004. So the fall did not come in every country as an uninterrupted decline.

Detail matters too. The index of the United States fell to 75.3 in the year 2020 and was 78.1 in the year 2024; so in the last four years the emissions index did not fall, it rose. The index of France was 91.0 in the year 2012 and 71.6 in the year 2020. The index of Germany is 66.5 in the year 2024, that of France 68.5, that of Italy 65.9. These three countries are at a close level, but the paths differed: the index of Italy was 86.8 in the year 2012 while that of Germany was 91.8.

In the United Kingdom the index is 53.0 in the year 2024, a little over half of the emissions of the year 2000. Poland's index is 88.2: emissions fell, but more slowly. The index of Poland was 103.7 in the year 2008, 102.1 in the year 2012 and 101.2 in the year 2016; so it stayed above 100 until the year 2016 and fell to 94.9 only in the year 2020. In the United Kingdom the index was 87.2 in the year 2012; it went down to 71.0 in the year 2016 and to 57.9 in the year 2020.

## Countries where emissions rose: relative decoupling

Emissions did not fall everywhere. In Canada GDP rose by 59.8% and emissions rose by 6.4%. In Brazil GDP rose by 70.9% and emissions rose by 40.7%. In Türkiye GDP rose by 218.1% and emissions rose by 102.6%. In China GDP rose by 554.3% and emissions rose by 259.8%. In India GDP rose by 329.0% and emissions rose by 216.4%. In all five countries the rise in emissions is smaller than the rise in GDP. So there is no absolute decoupling in these countries, but there is a relative one: the economy grew faster than emissions.

For Türkiye the measure is as follows: emissions of 227.1 million tonnes in the year 2000 rose to 460.0 million tonnes in the year 2024; GDP rose from 413.9 billion dollars to 1,316.6 billion dollars. This is not an assessment; it is the change of two series. The emissions of Canada rose from 543.1 million tonnes to 578.0 million tonnes and those of Brazil from 349.4 million tonnes to 491.5 million tonnes. In Canada emissions stayed almost in place: the rise is only 6.4%. In Brazil emissions rose more slowly than GDP: 40.7% against 70.9%.

## The picture in the world total

![Key figure: In the world total, emissions rose together with GDP: Emissions: Emissions: 54.2%; Comparison: GDP: 99.4%.](/grafik/buyume-ve-emisyon-ayrismasi/graf-8.svg)
*World total, change in 2000–2024, per cent. Source: World Bank.*

The separation seen in the seven countries of the panel is not visible in the world total. World GDP rose by 99.4% in 2000–2024; carbon dioxide emissions rose by 54.2%. Emissions went from 25,694.7 million tonnes to 39,632.7 million tonnes. The world total shows relative decoupling too: emissions rise, but more slowly than GDP.

If we add up the emission falls of the seven countries of the panel, they come to 2,437.4 million tonnes. The rise in the world total is 13,938.0 million tonnes. The fall of these seven countries equals 17.5% of the rise in the world total; the changes of countries outside the panel are not in this calculation. The fall of 291.5 million tonnes in Germany alone corresponds to 2.1% of that rise. World GDP rose by 48,279.4 billion dollars in the same period. In the panel there are seven countries with falling emissions on one side and five countries with rising emissions on the other. The emission rise of China is 9,476.8 million tonnes, which is 68.0% of the rise in the world total. The rise of India is 2,156.9 million tonnes, which is 15.5% of the rise in the world total. China's emissions went from 3,647.9 million tonnes to 13,124.7 million tonnes, India's from 996.9 million tonnes to 3,153.8 million tonnes. This comparison is not a ranking of countries; it only shows the difference in scale.

## Counter-reading: production-based counting

World Bank emissions data count emissions in the country where they are produced. The emissions released abroad while producing goods bought in one country appear in the line of the producing country, not of the consuming one. The separation between countries can therefore also be affected by the composition of foreign trade. The data we have offer no consumption-based measure; for this reason it cannot be told from this article how much of the separation is production moving elsewhere and how much is change inside the country.

A second reading: GDP measures the production of an economy; it does not directly measure welfare or emission intensity. Decoupling alone is not an indicator of good or bad; it only describes the difference between two series. While emissions in a country fall, the structure of its economy may also change: the share of industry, energy sources, population and household size are factors that do not show in this data. This article does not measure those factors and does not put forward any of them as the cause of a result; it only places side by side what two curves did over the same period.

## Measurement limits

This measure carries two large uncertainties, and both should be open to the reader. First, data revision: the series of the World Bank and EDGAR can be corrected backwards as new years are added; the values in this article belong to the update of October 2026. Second, the emissions series is an estimate, not a measurement: it is calculated from fuel use and production statistics.

World Bank series are published under the Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY) licence. The emissions series covers only carbon dioxide; other greenhouse gases such as methane and land use are not in it. The emissions series contains estimates at country level; this article does not interpret small differences between countries. The GDP series is in constant dollars at the prices of the year 2015; it depends on exchange-rate and price adjustment.

Choosing the start and end year also affects the result. The year 2020 is the pandemic period, and in Germany the emissions index fell markedly that year. We took the year 2000 and the year 2024; another pair of years gives different percentages. The list of countries is also our own choice. In economies that start from a small base, percentage rises look large: the GDP of Poland was 283.2 billion dollars in the year 2000, that of the United States 13,717.7 billion dollars. Percentages therefore do not compare the size of countries; they only show change relative to each country's own start.

Countries not in the panel make up a significant part of the world total, and some large emitters other than China and India are not in the panel either. For this reason there is no direct summing relation between the panel and the world line; the world line is the World Bank's own total.

## How we calculated

- From the World Bank indicators NY.GDP.MKTP.KD (GDP, constant United States dollars of the year 2015) and EN.GHG.CO2.MT.CE.AR5 (carbon dioxide emissions excluding land use, million tonnes) we took the values by country and year.
- We converted GDP values to billion dollars and rounded to one decimal; we rounded emissions in million tonnes to one decimal.
- Percentage change is the value of the year 2024, minus the value of the year 2000, divided by the value of the year 2000.
- For the index we divided the value of the year concerned by the value of the year 2000 and multiplied by 100.
- For the panel countries we took the same two indicators for the year 2000 and the year 2024; for the index charts we also used the intermediate years taken every four years between these years.
- For the world line we took the world total series of the World Bank; we did not add up the countries ourselves.
- The emission fall of the seven countries of the panel is, for each country, the value of the year 2000, minus the value of the year 2024, summed over the countries. We calculated the rise in the world total and the rises of China and India in the same way and took the ratios.
- Calculations use rounded values, so small differences may appear in the last digit.
