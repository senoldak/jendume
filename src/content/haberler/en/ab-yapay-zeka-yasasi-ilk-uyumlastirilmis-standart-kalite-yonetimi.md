A harmonised standard is a technical document that, once the European Union links it to a law, gives companies a ready route to show they comply. On 19 October 2026 the European Commission announced that the first such standard for the Artificial Intelligence (AI) Act had been referenced in the Official Journal of the European Union. It covers the quality management system that providers of high-risk AI systems must run, and the Commission says providers who apply it are presumed to meet the matching legal requirements.

It answers one question in the Act, how a provider should organise quality management, and leaves the other technical requirements to standards that have not yet appeared.

## What was published?

The new European standard was developed by the European Committee for Standardization (CEN) and the European Committee for Electrotechnical Standardization (CENELEC), the European standardisation organisations. According to the Commission, it “provides a clear legal pathway for providers of high-risk AI systems to comply with the quality management system requirements” of the Act’s article on quality management.

Behind it sits a Commission implementing decision of 12 October 2026, published in the Official Journal on 14 October 2026. The Commission’s page on harmonised standards for AI lists it as the decision on harmonised standards for high-risk AI systems.

Reaching this point took several years. CEN and CENELEC set up a joint technical committee in June 2021, the Commission says, to draft AI standards, including harmonised standards for the AI Act. The draft entered public enquiry on 30 October 2025; according to the Commission, it was the first harmonised standard for AI to reach that stage.

## What does the law itself ask for?

Under the Act, the obligation sits in its article on quality management. Its first sentence is short: “Providers of high-risk AI systems shall put a quality management system in place that ensures compliance with this Regulation.”

Next, the Act lists what that system must contain. One item matters directly here: providers must document the “technical specifications, including standards, to be applied”, and, where harmonised standards are not applied in full or do not cover every requirement, the other means they use to comply.

That article also contains two points that shape how the standard will be used. Implementing the system “shall be proportionate to the size of the provider’s organisation”, with small and medium-sized enterprises and start-ups named in particular. And providers that already run a quality management system under sectoral EU law may fold the AI requirements into that existing system.

## What does a presumption of conformity mean?

On its standardisation page, the Commission is clear that “The application of standards remains voluntary.” It adds: “Providers can choose any other framework to demonstrate their compliance with the AI Act.”

A company may still choose the standard because of the legal effect it brings. The Commission’s frequently asked questions describe the general rule: a product or system “is presumed to satisfy the essential requirements of the relevant EU legislation” if it complies with harmonised standards referenced in the Official Journal. The same page then says what that changes in practice: “This ‘presumption of conformity’ shifts the regulatory burden of proof on public authorities to disprove conformity.”

In plain terms, a provider that follows the standard does not have to argue from first principles that its quality management system satisfies the law. If an authority disagrees, the authority has to show why. A provider that uses another framework can still comply, but carries the task of demonstrating it.

Such a presumption has limits. According to the Commission, providers implementing the standard “will benefit from a presumption of conformity”, and that presumption covers the quality management requirements the standard addresses, not the whole Act. The Commission also says referenced standards come with an annex that “maps the legal requirements in the AI Act to the clauses in the standard”, which is how a provider can see which obligations a given clause answers.

## How is it different from existing AI management standards?

International management system standards already exist, and the Commission has explained why a new one was needed. Before the standard was published, the Commission addressed this in its frequently asked questions. It said the international AI management system standard from the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) and the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) helps to set up an AI management system, but that “its goals and definitions are not aligned with the quality management system that is required under the AI Act.” For that reason, it says, it requested “a new standard for a quality management system that focuses on regulatory compliance with the AI Act.”

In its announcement, the Commission says the new standard is mapped to the Act’s quality management article and is “built around the AI system lifecycle” rather than around the organisation. It is also “designed to be layered on top of the existing international standards” rather than replace them, with the Commission naming the international AI management system standard and sector-specific quality management systems as examples. The Commission adds that the standard “explicitly allows providers in regulated sectors to integrate management systems, rather than run separate ones.” It also says: “It is the only AI QMS standard with a presumption of conformity.”

## What is still missing?

The Commission asked CEN and CENELEC to develop standards in several areas, a list that runs from risk management to conformity assessment. This new standard deals with one subject, the quality management system. The Commission’s pages list no other harmonised standard for the AI Act in the Official Journal.

According to the Commission, “further harmonised standards” are “expected to follow”. Once CEN and CENELEC publish standards, the Commission “assesses whether they meet the intended objectives and legal requirements of the AI Act” before referencing them.

## Where does this sit in the timeline?

As the Commission sets out, the AI Act “entered into force on 1 August 2024 and became applicable on 2 August 2026, with some exceptions”.

Later dates apply to the rules this standard supports. The Commission says rules for systems in high-risk areas, “including biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, migration, asylum and border control”, apply from 2 December 2027. For AI integrated into products such as lifts or toys, the rules apply from 2 August 2028. The Commission links the timing to support tools: “This ensures the rules apply when companies have the right support tools to facilitate implementation, such as standards.”

On 14 October 2026 the Official Journal published the decision referencing the first harmonised standard, before the high-risk rules start to apply. The other requested standards have not appeared yet.

## What it shows, and what it does not

This publication shows that the link between the AI Act and technical standards now exists in law for one requirement: quality management for high-risk systems. It shows how that link works, through a voluntary standard, a presumption of conformity and an annex that maps clauses to obligations.

It does not show that a provider applying the standard complies with the whole Act. The presumption covers the quality management requirements the standard addresses, and the standard does not cover the other areas the Commission asked for. Nor does it say anything about how authorities will assess providers that choose another framework, or when the remaining standards will be referenced.
