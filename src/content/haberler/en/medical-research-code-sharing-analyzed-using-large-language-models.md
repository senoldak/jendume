Clinical prediction models in medicine are increasingly used to determine disease risks and plan treatment processes. However, understanding how these models are developed and trained is crucial for scientific transparency. An international team led by researchers at MIT and the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute utilized large language models (LLMs) to scrutinize code-sharing habits within medical literature.

The researchers examined nearly 4,000 clinical prediction studies available in the PubMed Central database. LLMs were employed to scan statements regarding code availability in these papers and to access the associated software repositories. Supported by human review, this process revealed significant transparency issues within the medical research community using concrete data.

The analysis results indicate that code sharing in medical research remains quite rare. According to the study, only about one in eight studies reported sharing their code. Furthermore, sharing code does not consistently mean providing adequate documentation; while some repositories are well-organized and descriptive, others offer almost no guidance at all. This inconsistency hinders the reproducibility and verifiability of scientific results.

These findings are now informing the development of "TRIPOD-Code," a reporting guideline aimed at establishing a new standard in the medical field. Bringing together experts from medicine, statistics, and computer science, this initiative seeks to provide a clear roadmap for how researchers should share their analytical code. Addressing this lack of transparency is seen as a vital step toward increasing the reliability of models that directly influence patient care. By automating the audit process, AI is helping to establish a more reliable foundation for scientific literature.
