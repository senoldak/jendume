Candles on a birthday cake are the most everyday form of the idea of a lifespan. So how far apart are the lifespans ahead of a baby born in one country and a baby born in another, and is that distance getting shorter? We asked the question with life expectancy at birth. The measure tells you how many years a newborn would live on average if the mortality pattern at the time of birth stayed the same for the whole of life.

The first clue is this. Rank the 217 countries and economies in the World Bank series from lowest to highest life expectancy. The value where the bottom tenth ends was 51.4 years in 2000. The value where the top tenth begins was 78.3 years. The gap between the two was 26.9 years. In 2023 the same two boundaries were 63.7 and 83.0 years, and the gap was 19.3 years. The distance between the ends shrank by 7.6 years.

That shrinking could have come from two places: the lower end could have moved up, or the upper end could have moved down. To see which, we split the distribution into parts.

## The first clue: what does the average hide?

One number for the whole world is easy to give. The World Bank's population-weighted world series was 67.7 years in 2000, and 73.3 years in 2023. That is a gain of 5.6 years at world level. But a single number does not say where the gain piled up. The same gain can arise in a world where everyone moved a little, or in a world where only one segment moved fast.

That is why we look at the ends separately. A distribution's middle and its tails can move at different speeds. In this piece, the tails are the boundaries of the tenth-sized slices at the two ends of the ranking. We did not use the single lowest or highest country. The reason is in the measurement section near the end.

We looked at the country in the middle first. The median country is the one in the exact middle of the ranking. Its value was 70.5 years in 2000, and 74.6 years in 2023. The gain is 4.1 years. This is a calm picture: a little over four years across two decades. But the median describes the middle of the distribution; it says nothing about the ends.

## The second clue: the lower boundary moved the most

Start at the bottom: the boundary of the bottom tenth was 51.4 years in 2000, and 63.7 years in 2023. The gain is 12.3 years. The boundary of the bottom quarter rose from 61.8 to 68.5 years, a gain of 6.7 years.

At the top the picture differs. The three-quarter boundary rose from 74.8 to 79.6 years: 4.8 years. The boundary of the top tenth rose from 78.3 to 83.0 years: 4.7 years.

Put side by side, the difference is plain: the lower-tenth boundary rose 12.3 years and the upper-tenth boundary rose 4.7 years. In relative terms the order holds: the lower-tenth boundary rose 23.9%, the median 5.8% and the upper-tenth boundary 6.0%.

Bar order says something: the gain falls as you go from the bottom toward the median. At the median the gain is 4.1 years, the lowest point. The gain at the top end is slightly above the median's. In the upper half of the distribution, progress is steadier than in the lower half.

## The third clue: in which years did the gain build up?

Split by year, the lower-tenth boundary is higher at every observation over 2000–2019 than at the one before. It was 51.4 years in 2000 and reached 54.0 in 2005, 57.2 in 2010, 60.1 in 2015 and 62.2 in 2019. The upper-tenth boundary also rises at the same points: 78.3, 80.0, 81.1, 82.0 and 82.7 years.

Steps in the two series are not the same size. The lower boundary rose 2.6 years over 2000–2005, 3.2 over 2005–2010, 2.9 over 2010–2015 and 2.1 over 2015–2019. The steps of the upper boundary over the same intervals are 1.7, 1.1, 0.9 and 0.7 years. The last interval is four years long and the others five; even so, every step of the lower boundary is larger than the matching step of the upper boundary.

Steps also show that the lower boundary's gain was fastest over 2005–2010. After that the steps get smaller. At the upper boundary the shrinking starts earlier and is steadier.

## Why did the middle band narrow less?

Narrowing tails does not mean the whole distribution narrowed. To test this we computed one more measure: the middle band between the quarter and three-quarter boundaries. In 2000 the band was 13.0 years wide. In 2023 it was 11.1 years. The decrease is 1.9 years. The decrease between the ends is 7.6 years. So the distance between the ends shrank far more than the middle band did.

Read together, the small decrease of the middle band and the large decrease between the ends tell the story; one does not stand in for the other.

This shows why the tail approach is needed. Had we looked only at the middle band, we might have concluded that the gap between countries barely changed. Looking at the ends reveals a larger movement.

Over 2000–2023 both lines run downward overall, and the space between the two series closes. The gap between the ends was 26.0 years in 2005, 23.9 years in 2010, 21.9 years in 2015, and 20.5 years in 2019. One point stands out: in 2021 the gap rose to 21.1 years.

## Where does Türkiye stand in this distribution?

For readers in Türkiye, one more question needs an answer: where does Türkiye's value sit in this distribution? We answer it as a measure only. Türkiye's life expectancy was 71.9 years in 2000, and 77.2 years in 2023. The gain is 5.3 years.

Over the same years the median rose from 70.5 to 74.6 years. Türkiye's value was 1.4 years above the median in 2000, and 2.6 years above it in 2023. In 2023 the three-quarter boundary is 79.6 years, and Türkiye's value is 2.4 years below that boundary.

Against the world series: in 2023, Türkiye's value is 77.2 years and the world value is 73.3 years. Position and speed are separate things: Türkiye's gain is larger than the median's gain but smaller than the gain of the lower-tenth boundary. The world series is population weighted, while the country distribution gives every country equal weight. The two series therefore cannot stand in for each other.

## 2019–2021: what happened to the distribution?

A steadily falling line would have been the natural expectation. But the series moves backward over 2019–2021. Across these two years, which cover the pandemic period, the median fell by 1.9 years, from 74.2 to 72.3 years. The lower-tenth boundary fell by 1.0 year, from 62.2 to 61.2 years. The upper-tenth boundary fell by 0.4 years, from 82.7 to 82.3 years.

In the world series the fall was 1.7 years over the same two years, from 72.9 to 71.2 years. Türkiye's value fell by 2.0 years, from 77.7 to 75.7 years.

These figures show one thing: the fall is present across the whole distribution, but it is not equal. It is larger in the middle and smaller at the upper end. Because the lower boundary fell by 1.0 year and the upper boundary by 0.4 years, the gap between the ends opened by 0.6 years over the two years. In 2023 it fell to 19.3 years, below the 20.5 years seen in 2019. In the same years the lower-tenth boundary rose to 1.5 years above its value in 2019. For the median the difference is 0.4 years and for the upper-tenth boundary 0.3 years. So the lower boundary recovered the most after the fall.

Why the fall happened is not something the series tells us. It shows level and change only. We make no causal claim.

## A counter-reading: is this narrowing good news by itself?

Labelling the narrowing as good or bad news is not our aim; we read the measure. But the other ways of reading the data should be stated.

First, the percentile boundaries come from a ranking of countries, and countries can change places in the ranking. The country that sets the lower-tenth boundary in 2000, and the one that sets it in 2023, need not be the same. So these data cannot support the same countries moved up; they support only the value at this point of the distribution rose.

Second, countries are counted with equal weight. A small economy and a very populous country take the same place in the distribution. A population-weighted reading could give a different picture. The 5.6-year gain of the world series is larger than the 4.1-year gain of the median, but because the two series are built by different methods we do not attach the difference to a single cause.

Third, whether we read the gain in years or in per cent. In years the lower boundary's gain is 12.3 and the upper boundary's 4.7; in per cent, 23.9% and 6.0%. Both measures point the same way, but because the starting value at the lower end is low, the percentage difference should be read with a different weight than the difference in years.

Fourth, life expectancy is a one-dimensional measure. It tells you the expected length at birth; it does not tell you how a life is lived or under what conditions at each age.

## What does the data measure, and what can it not?

On sources: the World Bank series draws on the World Population Prospects work of the United Nations (UN) Population Division and on releases from national statistical offices and from Eurostat, the statistical office of the European Union. By the World Bank's own note, the annual values taken from the UN series are interpolated from five-year period data, and so may not reflect real events as well as observed data do.

Our results depend on that limit. Small movements between years, for example the difference over 2019–2021, should be read with caution. The long-run trend, the narrowing since 2000, is less affected by this limit. But a single decimal in a single year should not be treated as a firm finding.

Another limit is the volatility of single-country values. Individual values at the very bottom of the distribution can change a great deal from year to year; in some years one country's value separates sharply from the neighbouring years. That is why we did not use the single lowest and highest country and used boundary values instead. We did not single out any country for sitting at an end, and we did not name countries at the ends. The percentile values are tied to a position in the ranking, not to one country.

Boundaries are recomputed each year from that year's values; they are not tied to a fixed list of countries. A third limit is the country list. The 217 countries and economies include small economies. The list's coverage follows the World Bank's country definitions; we excluded aggregated regions.

## How we calculated

- We pulled the series life expectancy at birth, total (years) from the World Bank's World Development Indicators database. The series draws on the UN Population Division's World Population Prospects.
- We excluded aggregated regions (income groups and geographic regions, for example). That left 217 countries and economies.
- For each year we ranked the countries from lowest to highest. We took the value that ends the bottom tenth, the bottom quarter, the middle, the bottom three quarters and the bottom nine tenths using the nearest rank method: the boundary is the value of the first country in the ranking that covers the stated share. So every boundary is a country's published value, not an interpolated number. The method invents no value between two neighbouring countries; this lets the boundaries be found again exactly in the source data.
- We computed the gap between the ends by subtracting the lower-tenth boundary from the upper-tenth boundary, and the middle band by subtracting the quarter boundary from the three-quarter boundary. We found the gains by subtracting the value of the first year from the value of the last year.
- We took the values for Türkiye and for the world series from the same database, year by year.
- We rounded values to one decimal and computed differences from the rounded values.
- We chose observations in 2000, in 2005, in 2010, in 2015, in 2019, in 2021 and in 2023. The span 2019–2021 lets us see the pandemic period; the year 2023 is our end point.
