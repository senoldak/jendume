The widespread adoption of artificial intelligence tools is creating unexpected bureaucratic bottlenecks across public services and judicial systems. While governments aim to transform state machinery and save billions of pounds through automation, the public's concurrent use of AI is generating an overwhelming surge of machine-generated correspondence, complaints, and official requests.

Judicial bodies are facing similar pressures. Employment tribunal cases across England, Scotland, and Wales reportedly rose by 39 percent in a single year, driven in part by claimants using AI to draft their cases. Magistrates and judges now encounter numerous filings built with AI tools that often cite non-existent legal precedents, requiring human officials to carefully untangle genuine grievances from fabricated text. Parliamentary offices and government departments similarly report being swamped by AI-generated Freedom of Information requests and constituent letters.

The phenomenon extends to the labor market as well. Employers who automated their initial job application screening are now met with an avalanche of automated applications sent by candidates across the board. Consequently, neither employers nor applicants can easily separate genuine intent from the flood of machine-generated text. Analysts note that much like traffic congestion in cities, shortcuts lose their efficiency when everyone uses them at the same time.

Historically, humans have always learned to develop ways around problems of our own making, such as spam filters for emails or congestion charges. As society adapts to these new communication dynamics, policymakers may need to reconsider how digital interactions are managed to prevent institutional paralysis.
