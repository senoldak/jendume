Researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) have utilized artificial intelligence models to uncover how the human brain processes the vast array of linguistic structures found across the globe. While languages take on thousands of forms, ranging from tonal languages like Vietnamese to those relying on precise word orders like English, the study suggests that the human brain processes them through a universal mechanism. By employing multilingual AI models, the team analyzed brain responses from individuals speaking 21 different languages. The research team recorded brain activity while participants listened to stories from "Alice in Wonderland" in their native languages. The languages included in the study were Afrikaans, Dutch, Farsi, French, Lithuanian, Marathi, Norwegian, Romanian, Spanish, Tamil, Turkish, Vietnamese, Italian, Portuguese, German, Russian, Polish, Hindi, Arabic, Mandarin, and Korean. By coupling this brain imaging data with outputs from multilingual models, the researchers created "encoding models." These models take the numerical representation of a sentence from an AI and predict how the human brain might respond to the same input. The findings revealed consistency: the encoding models accurately predicted brain responses even in languages they had never been trained on. For instance, a model trained to predict brain responses in French could successfully predict responses in Vietnamese. This discovery suggests that all human brains represent language through a shared neural thread. The researchers concluded that this shared component is linked to the concept of meaning. This research has implications for the future of AI-driven language tools. Currently, AI tools designed to detect cognitive decline based on brain activity patterns are largely limited to well-studied languages like English. By demonstrating that AI and the human brain overlap across diverse languages, this research supports the development of more inclusive clinical tools. Furthermore, it offers pathways for preserving endangered languages. If a language is on the brink of extinction, it might be possible to use brain imaging from a single speaker to decode and translate that language, ensuring it does not vanish.
