The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence technologies has heightened concerns that automation could lead to widespread unemployment, affecting white-collar professions as well. Warnings from prominent figures such as Bill Gates and Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei have prompted policymakers and economists to seek alternative safeguards. In this context, the idea of guaranteeing meaningful and decently paid employment to anyone who wants to work has returned to the political forefront.

Rooted historically in public works initiatives from the Great Depression era, job guarantee models aim to mitigate the financial and psychological damages of joblessness. In the United States, Representative Ayanna Pressley reintroduced a congressional resolution calling for a federal job guarantee that would pay participants at least 25 dollars an hour. The proposed jobs span sectors such as emergency preparedness, caregiving, and public art projects.

Another recent legislative proposal suggests taxing large artificial intelligence companies to fund a federal job guarantee. Representatives Greg Casar, Valerie Foushee, and Sara Jacobs proposed taxing major AI firms based on either their token values or AI product revenues, with tax rates scaling up if unemployment rises. Meanwhile, organizations like Universal Basic Employment in Cleveland are launching pilot programs to subsidize existing private-sector wages.

Economists note that the costs of job guarantees can be offset by savings on unemployment benefits and increased tax revenues from employed participants. Experts such as Max Kasy from Oxford and Pavlina Tcherneva from Bard College emphasize that when factoring in broader societal benefits and health improvements, such programs should be viewed as strategic investments.
