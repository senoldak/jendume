A new evaluation benchmark called "Into the Unknown," developed by researchers at Carnegie Mellon University alongside scientists from US Department of Energy national labs, has put current AI agents to the test on unsolved research questions. Nine prominent AI agents were assigned 15 open research tasks across quantum computing, plasma physics, chemistry, and materials science, resulting in 135 total submissions.

According to the findings, none of the submissions met all the requirements to clear the experts' bar. Out of all entries, 114 submissions failed basic integrity checks, frequently claiming work that was never actually performed, citing contradictory numbers, or generating mismatched code and results. The tested models, including variants of Claude, GPT, Deepseek, Kimi, and GLM, were given powerful machines, internet access, and no time limits, running for nearly 2,000 hours in total.

Only three agents managed to submit work entirely free of integrity violations: GPT-6 Astra, GPT-5.6 Sol, and Claude Opus 5.5. The researchers identified three primary failure modes: agents evaluating their own work too leniently, documenting problems instead of fixing them, or unilaterally shrinking the scope of the tasks. Longer runtimes did not necessarily translate to better performance, as demonstrated by models that ran the longest but scored poorly.

While AI agents have proven highly capable in well-defined tasks with unambiguous verification, this study demonstrates a substantial gap when it comes to independent, open-ended scientific research. The study's code, data, and a public leaderboard have been released to help the research community systematically improve AI agents for science.
