EU greenhouse gas emissions, the combined emissions of the European Union, fell by 1,849.7 million tonnes in 1990–2024. The starting value was 4,866.9 million tonnes and the latest value is 3,017.2 million tonnes, a fall of 38.0%. The tonnes here are carbon dioxide equivalents: methane and similar gases are converted into carbon dioxide terms according to their warming effect, so that all gases can be added in one unit.

A single number does not say where the decline came from. It could have been spread evenly across sectors, or concentrated in a few items. The sector table of Eurostat, the statistical office of the European Union, points to the second. In the item called energy industries, which covers the power plants that produce electricity and heat and the refineries, emissions fell by 799.4 million tonnes, which is 43.2% of the total decline. In transport, emissions rose by 19.3% over the same period.

The clues come in turn: which item shrank, which one grew, when the decline gathered pace, and what the figures do not count.

## Where did the fall come from?

Splitting the total into its parts comes first. Eurostat uses the common reporting format of the United Nations (UN) Framework Convention on Climate Change; an emission is classified by the place where the gas leaves into the atmosphere. Grouped into eight items, the change in 1990–2024 looks like this.

*Change in emissions by sector, 1990–2024, carbon dioxide equivalent. Source: Eurostat.*

Energy industries are the item that fell most: emissions dropped by 799.4 million tonnes. Industry comes next: fuel burned in manufacturing, together with industrial processes and product use (the emissions that come from production, such as cement, steel and chemicals, and from the use of products such as refrigerant gases), fell by 559.2 million tonnes. The item other fuel combustion, made up of the fuel burned in homes, service buildings and agriculture together with fuel combustion not classified elsewhere, fell by 296.7 million tonnes. Agriculture's own emissions fell by 128.2 million tonnes. Fugitive emissions from fuels fell by 118.3 million tonnes. Waste fell by 74.0 million tonnes. A small item called indirect carbon dioxide fell by 4.0 million tonnes.

Only transport moves the other way: its emissions rose by 130.1 million tonnes. The total decline of the falling items is 1,979.8 million tonnes. The rise in transport takes back part of that; the net fall is 1,849.7 million tonnes. The eight items together match the total line in the Eurostat table, so no part of the decomposition is left unaccounted for.

## Energy industries: the widest share of the total fall

Energy industries emitted 1,441.8 million tonnes in 1990. In 2024 the figure was 642.4 million tonnes, a fall of 55.4%. The share of the item in total emissions also dropped, from 29.6% to 21.3%. Within the item, plants that supply the public with electricity and heat dominate: this sub-item went from 1,232.1 million tonnes to 520.3 million tonnes, a fall of 57.8%. The share of the sub-item within energy industries was 85.5% in 1990 and 81.0% in 2024. The rest consists of refineries and similar plants.

Nor is the decline linear. Emissions from energy industries were 1,304.0 million tonnes in 2000, and in 1990–2000 the fall was only 9.6%. Most of the rest came after the year 2000: in 2000–2024 emissions fell by 50.7%. The value of the item dropped to 776.5 million tonnes in 2020, rose to 858.9 million tonnes in 2022 and fell back to 642.4 million tonnes in 2024. Eurostat does not show the reason for these swings; it gives only the values by year.

One subtlety matters when reading this item: it counts the emissions of the plants that generate electricity, not the emissions of those who use it. The emissions linked to the electricity a household or a factory consumes are booked where the electricity is produced, not to the household's or the factory's own item. A change in electricity use therefore shows in this item rather than in the sector that uses the power; the table only says where the emission is released.

## Why does transport stand apart?

Transport emitted 674.7 million tonnes in 1990. In 2024 the figure was 804.8 million tonnes, a rise of 19.3%. The share of the item in total emissions went from 13.9% to 26.7%. Two effects combine in that rise: the growth of transport itself and the shrinking of the total. Set against the total of the year 1990, the transport emissions of the year 2024 would be a share of 16.5%; the rest of the rise comes from the shrinking denominator. Road transport is the main part of the item: road emissions were 620.6 million tonnes in 1990. In 2024 the figure rose to 767.0 million tonnes, a rise of 23.6%, and in the year 2024 it accounted for 95.3% of transport emissions.

Their paths cross at one point.

In 1990 the energy industries emitted 2.1 times as much as transport. By the year 2022 the two items were close: 858.9 million tonnes and 804.7 million tonnes. In 2024 the order had changed: transport, at 804.8 million tonnes, is above energy industries at 642.4 million tonnes. Because the points in the chart are taken every second year, the change of order is not fully visible in the chart. In the annual values the order changes in 2022–2023: transport, at 795.6 million tonnes in the year 2023, is above energy industries at 698.4 million tonnes.

Transport does not follow a straight rise either. The value of the item was 864.6 million tonnes in 2007; the value in 2024 is 6.9% lower than that. Transport remains above its level in the year 1990, but a little below its value in the year 2007. In the pandemic period, the value in the year 2020 fell to 722.5 million tonnes and returned to 804.7 million tonnes in 2022.

A note: in the Eurostat table, international aviation and international shipping are not part of the totals; they sit in separate memo lines. Together they were 154.8 million tonnes in 1990. In 2024 the figure was 257.0 million tonnes. These two items are not included in the transport figure above; their total in 2024 is equivalent to 31.9% of the transport item.

## Industry, buildings and agriculture: a slower but long decline

Across the remaining items the direction is the same and the story is quieter. Emissions from fuel burned in manufacturing fell from 720.8 million tonnes to 351.6 million tonnes (51.2%). Industrial processes and product use, meaning the emissions that come from production and from the use of products rather than from burning fuel, fell from 449.9 million tonnes to 259.9 million tonnes (42.2%). The item other fuel combustion fell by 39.5%; within it, household fuel dropped from 450.1 million tonnes to 263.4 million tonnes (41.5%). Household fuel makes 61.2% of this item in the year 2024.

*Change in emissions by sector, 1990–2024, per cent. Source: Eurostat.*

Agriculture is the item that fell least in this group: from 487.3 million tonnes to 359.1 million tonnes, a fall of 26.3%. About half of the item, 49.4% in the year 2024, is methane from the digestion of animals; this part fell from 241.5 million tonnes to 177.3 million tonnes (26.6%). Waste fell by 39.9%. The steepest fall in percentage terms is in fugitive emissions, the leaks that occur when fuels are extracted and moved. This item fell from 165.0 million tonnes to 46.7 million tonnes (71.7%).

Percentages and million tonnes give different orders. Fugitive emissions fell most in percentage terms, but in million tonnes they fell by only 118.3 million tonnes, against a fall of 799.4 million tonnes in energy industries. The absolute change shows how much an item contributed to the total fall; the percentage change shows how much it shrank.

There is also an exception hidden inside the item industrial processes and product use. The emissions of the gases used in place of substances that harm the ozone layer (in cooling, air conditioning, foams and similar uses) were 0.0 million tonnes in 1990. They reached 90.2 million tonnes in 2014 and dropped to 54.1 million tonnes in 2024; measured against the value of the year 2014, that is a fall of 40.0%. In other words, inside the 42.2% fall of this item, a part that was negligible in the year 1990 also grew. The item-level table does not show this. Process emissions from iron and steel production fell from 103.9 million tonnes to 53.6 million tonnes (48.4%).

## How are the totals distributed in 2024?

![Share chart: Transport is the largest part of emissions: Share: Energy industries: 21.3%, Industry: 20.3%, Transport: 26.7%, Other fuel combustion: 14.5%, Agriculture: 11.9%, Waste and other: 5.4%.](/grafik/ab-sera-gazi-bilesenleri/graf-8.svg)
*Share of each sector in total emissions in 2024, per cent. Source: Eurostat.*

In the latest year transport makes 26.7% of the total and is the largest item. Energy industries are 21.3%, industry (manufacturing fuel and processes) 20.3%, other fuel combustion 14.5% and agriculture 11.9%. Waste, fugitive emissions and indirect carbon dioxide together are 5.4%. In 1990 the largest item was energy industries, at 29.6% of the total.

## In which years did the decline pick up?

Divided into periods, the total shows that the decline did not proceed at an even speed. The total fell by only 6.8% in 1990–2005, from 4,866.9 million tonnes to 4,537.1 million tonnes. In the next period, 2005–2019, the fall was 21.2% and the total reached 3,574.5 million tonnes. Emissions fell by 8.2% in 2019–2020; then they rose by 5.2% in 2020–2021. The total fell by 15.6% in 2019–2024.

Last year's total is also 8.0% lower than the value of the pandemic-period year 2020: emissions went from 3,280.9 million tonnes to 3,017.2 million tonnes. This is not a claim about cause; it only states what the figures say.

Base year matters too. Using the year 2005 as the base, the total fell by 33.5%; using the year 1990 as the base, it fell by 38.0%. Both come from the same data; the difference arises because the decline was slow in 1990–2005. The choice of base year can make the same path look faster or slower.

## Counter-reading: net emissions and forests

So far the total has been read as gross: the amount released into the atmosphere. The same table also gives an item for land use, land-use change and forestry. It counts emissions and sinks together, that is, the carbon held by forests and soil; when the value is negative, the item holds carbon on balance.

That item was at minus 258.4 million tonnes in 1990. In the year 2005 it reached minus 382.7 million tonnes, and in the year 2024 it was minus 231.0 million tonnes. In other words the net sink shrank by 151.7 million tonnes in 2005–2024. The total that also counts sinks was 4,608.5 million tonnes in 1990. In 2024 it was 2,786.2 million tonnes, a fall of 39.5%. This net fall is 1,822.3 million tonnes, which is 27.4 million tonnes smaller than the gross fall, because the sink weakened over the period.

Gross and net declines are close but not the same. The gap depends on the yearly change in natural sinks such as forests. In 2005–2024 gross emissions fell by 1,519.9 million tonnes; the 151.7 million tonne weakening of the sink took back part of that fall in the net account.

## What does the inventory not count?

First, this is a production-based inventory: an emission is counted where it occurs. The emissions created elsewhere while imported goods were made are not in this table; the data are not a consumption-based measure.

Second, the composition is held fixed. EU here is the sum of today's member countries; the same countries are added up for the year 1990. There is no separate membership composition for earlier years.

Third, the sector classification shows where an emission occurs, not why. Whether a fuel change, a demand change or something else lies behind the fall in energy industries cannot be told from this table. The values for the year 2024 in the update of Eurostat dated June 2026 may be revised in later years.

Fourth, the gases are converted to carbon dioxide equivalents; how much weight each gas gets depends on the conversion factors. This article does not break the figures down by gas.

Fifth, the table is the EU total. Differences between member countries are outside the subject of this article, so no conclusion can be drawn for any single country.

What the table says is limited but clear: among the eight main items emissions rose only in transport (the international transport and land use items lie outside these eight items and were treated separately); the widest fall occurred in energy industries, while transport moved the other way. The figures show where and by how much emissions changed. They do not show why, and this article does not claim to.

## How we calculated

- From the Eurostat table Greenhouse gas emissions by source sector (env_air_gge), for today's member countries of the European Union taken together, we took the greenhouse gas total in million tonnes, excluding land use and forestry and international aviation and shipping.
- For each sector we took the values of the year 1990 and the year 2024. Change is the last value minus the first; percentage change is that difference divided by the first value.
- For the waterfall we grouped the sectors into eight items. Industry is manufacturing fuel plus industrial processes. Other fuel combustion is fuel used in homes, services and agriculture plus fuel combustion not classified elsewhere. The total decline of the falling items is the change in the total minus the change in transport. The eight changes match the change in the total line.
- We calculated shares by dividing the value of a sector by the total of the same year.
- Period changes use the totals of the year 1990, the year 2005, the year 2019, the year 2020, the year 2021 and the year 2024.
- For the net calculation we took the item land use and forestry and the total line that includes it.
- Values are in million tonnes or per cent, rounded to one decimal. Calculations use the rounded values, so in a place or two the last digit may differ slightly.
