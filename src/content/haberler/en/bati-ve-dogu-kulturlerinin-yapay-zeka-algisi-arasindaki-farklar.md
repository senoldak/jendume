The rapid development and spread of artificial intelligence worldwide are interpreted through vastly different cultural and theological narratives across regions. In the West, artificial intelligence is frequently discussed through Judeo-Christian traditions of apocalyptic scenarios, godlike powers, and angelic or demonic figures destined to destroy or save humanity. Recent rhetoric in the United States highlights deep-seated cultural anxieties that autonomous technology might spiral out of control and subjugate humanity. Philosopher Nick Bostrom's concept of superintelligence popularized the notion of a future where superior entities rule over ordinary people within Western imagination. Conversely, in regions with distinct philosophical roots like China, AI is perceived not as a theological threat or a machine god, but as an opportunity to lead a new industrial revolution and a practical instrument for societal progress. As author Chen Qiufan points out, the deepest fear in China is less about machines enslaving humanity and more about missing out on the industrial revolution race of the nineteenth century. Chinese science fiction and media favor system-like narratives where technology guides individuals rather than staging robot uprisings. These cultural divides, stretching from science fiction literature to national policies, directly dictate regulatory frameworks and public acceptance. While Western institutions grapple with existential risks and puritanical fears of artificial intelligence, Asian cultural frameworks position systems as collaborative partners and efficiency managers. This divergence demonstrates that artificial intelligence is not merely a technical tool, but a mirror reflecting humanity's millennia-old cultural codes.
