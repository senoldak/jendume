In 2000, 28.6% of the peer-reviewed journal and conference articles in science and engineering tracked by the NSF were credited to the United States and 5.0% to China. In 2023 the figures were 13.2% and 28.5%. Over those twenty-three years the total of these articles rose from 1,070,967 to 3,271,296, an increase of 205.5%. So it was not only the shares that moved; the pie being divided also grew.

This article looks at one question: how did the shares of countries in that total shift, and how did the rankings change? We first explain what the measure is, then look at the shares of thirteen countries, then at where the growth came from and how else the data can be read. A ranking here tells you one thing only, the article count. It carries no judgment about a country's scientific strength, contribution or quality.

The data come from the World Bank's World Development Indicators, from the series on scientific and technical journal articles. The World Bank does not produce the series itself. It takes it from the Science and Engineering Indicators of the National Science Foundation (NSF) in the United States. The World Bank publishes the series under a Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY) licence.

## How are articles counted?

The NSF counts articles from the records of a bibliographic database that it tracks. The count covers articles in scientific journals and some conference proceedings. The fields follow the NSF's own taxonomy: agricultural sciences, biological and biomedical sciences, chemistry, computer sciences, engineering, geosciences, health sciences, materials science, mathematics and statistics, physics, psychology and social sciences are among them.

One feature of the count is worth knowing: fractional counting. When authors at institutions in different countries write an article together, the credit for the article is shared among those countries. In the NSF's definition, a country's share of the credit equals the proportion of authors with an institutional address in that country. An article written entirely by authors in one country counts as one full article for that country. Fractional counting makes the country counts add up to the total number of articles, which is why shares can be calculated meaningfully. In the World Bank table the values appear rounded from raw numbers that are not whole.

Two cautions come first. The NSF says that the database is updated continuously, while each edition of its report uses a fixed copy; it therefore does not recommend comparing exact counts across editions, and it says that trends are comparable. The NSF also writes that the 2000–2002 data come from earlier editions and may not be strictly comparable with the 2003–2022 data. This article covers 2000–2023, so the second caution applies to us directly. In the section on other readings we recalculated the result, starting in 2003.

We compiled a list of thirteen countries for this article. The list has the United States, China, India, Germany, the United Kingdom, Japan, France, Italy, South Korea, Brazil, Türkiye, Canada and Australia. Three things stand out. First, China's share rose by 23.5 points. Second, the US share fell by 15.4 points. Third, India's share rose by 5.0 points, while Japan's fell by 6.1 points.

Ranks give a similar picture. In 2000, among the thirteen countries, the United States was first, Japan second, the United Kingdom third, Germany fourth, China fifth, France sixth and India tenth. In 2023, China is first, the United States second, India third, Germany fourth, the United Kingdom fifth and Japan sixth. China's rank was fifth in 2000 and first in 2023; India's was tenth and third; Japan's was second and sixth. Germany's rank was fourth in both years.

## How did the article counts of China and the US develop over the years?

Yearly series for these two countries show when the change happened. China's count was above Japan's in 2005: 165,262 against 111,936. In 2010, China's count was 307,848 and the US count was 407,406. China's count was 401,136 in 2015 and 671,731 in 2020; the US count was 429,258 in 2015 and 453,890 in 2020. In 2000 the US article count was 306,473 and China's was 53,285. China's count as a ratio to the US count was 0.17. In 2023, China's count was 932,712 and the US count was 430,843, and the ratio was 2.16.

Around 2016–2017 the order of the two series changed. In 2016 the US count was 1,458 higher than China's. In 2017, China's count was 28,777 higher than the US count. In every year over 2017–2023, China's count was higher than the US count.

There is one more detail in the series. The US article count reached its highest value over 2000–2023, 471,378, in 2021; in 2023 it was 430,843, a fall of 8.6%. In the same two years China's count rose from 756,203 to 932,712. We say that these two movements occurred in the same period; the data themselves do not show that one depends on the other or on anything else.

## Where did the growth in the world total come from?

Over 2000–2023 the world total rose by 2,200,329 articles. Of this rise, 879,427 are credited to China, 206,765 to India and 124,370 to the United States. In other words, 40.0% of the rise comes from China, 9.4% from India and 5.7% from the United States. In 2023, China and India together had 1,160,886 articles, which is 35.5% of the world total.

Worldwide, the total rose in every period: by 78.3% over 2000–2010, by 50.2% over 2010–2020 and by 14.1% over 2020–2023. The periods differ in length; the last one covers only three years. That concentration of the rise also explains the shift in shares. The total grew to more than three times its size; the share of every country that grew more slowly than that fell. A share is a ratio, so both the numerator and the denominator move: the US count rose by 40.6%, but because the world total rose by 205.5%, its share became smaller.

Elsewhere, the story of the eleven countries other than China and the United States is more scattered.

Germany's count rose from 69,739 to 108,782, an increase of 56.0%. But its share fell from 6.5% to 3.3%. The United Kingdom's count rose from 77,969 to 97,536, an increase of 25.1%. Its share fell from 7.3% to 3.0%. In both countries the share fell and the count rose. A share has to be read together with the growth in the total.

Japan's case is different. Its article count was 96,607 in 2000 and 96,543 in 2023, a difference of only 64 articles. So Japan's count fell by 0.1% over these twenty-three years, while its share fell from 9.0% to 3.0%.

India and Germany changed places in 2015–2020. In 2015, Germany's count was 106,776 and India's was 94,794. In 2020, India's count was 145,711 and Germany's was 109,198.

South Korea's count rose from 15,875 to 71,724. Its share rose from 1.5% to 2.2%. Its rank was eleventh in 2000 and eighth in 2023.

Türkiye's count rose from 6,815 to 50,180. That is 7.4 times its value in 2000. Its share rose from 0.6% to 1.5%. Its rank among the thirteen countries was thirteenth in both years; the counts of the eleven countries other than Japan also rose in the same period. As a measure for Türkiye: 50,180 articles in 2023, 1.5% of the world total.

Canada, France, Italy, Brazil and Australia have shares between 1.7% and 2.5% in 2023. In 2000, the shares of these five countries lay between 1.2% and 4.7%. Brazil's share rose; the shares of Canada, France, Italy and Australia fell.

## How else can the same data be read?

One other reading comes from the difference between share and count. In most countries whose share fell, the count did not fall. The article count rose over 2000–2023 in Germany, the United Kingdom and the United States. A table of shares can be read as if something were shrinking; here the count did not shrink, only the total grew faster. That is why we give counts and shares side by side.

A second concerns the starting year. The 2000–2002 data come from earlier editions and, according to the NSF, may not be strictly comparable with later years. So we recalculated the shares, starting in 2003. China's share was 7.2% in 2003; in 2023, it was 21.3 points higher. The US share fell from 27.4% to 13.2%, a fall of 14.2 points. India's share rose from 2.2% to 7.0%, a rise of 4.8 points. The sizes are somewhat smaller than the differences with the start in 2000; the direction is the same for these three countries. This test uses only a start in 2003 and covers only these three countries; we did not measure the effect of the starting year for the other countries or for the ranks.

A third concerns fractional counting. Because the credit for jointly written articles is divided, the count of a country with many international collaborations can come out smaller than it would under whole counting. The whole-counting series is not in this table, so we cannot measure the difference between the two methods. We report only the results of fractional counting.

## What do these figures not say?

An article count measures the volume of output. It does not measure the quality, influence or originality of an article. Two countries may publish the same number of articles that are used in very different ways. These data do not show that.

Only articles with an English title and abstract enter the count. The NSF states that this condition carries a bias that cannot be measured: not all science and engineering articles meet it. For that reason, small differences in shares should not be treated as meaningful on their own, especially between countries that are close to each other. In 2023, Germany has 108,782, the United Kingdom 97,536 and Japan 96,543 articles, side by side; the order among these three values could change with a small revision.

Nor do the data explain causes. We do not say why a country's share rose or fell; this series does not show the reason. Nor does the series contain scientific capacity, funding or student numbers. It counts only the articles that are recorded as published.

Finally, the World Bank reports that the aggregation method for this series is a gap-filled total. We used only the values published in the table and did not do our own gap-filling.

## How we calculated

We set out each step. All values were retrieved from the World Bank's World Development Indicators API with a single-country, single-year query.

- Series: IP.JRN.ARTC.SC, scientific and technical journal articles. Unit: articles (fractional count), rounded to a whole number.
- Countries: the United States, China, India, Germany, the United Kingdom, Japan, France, Italy, South Korea, Brazil, Türkiye, Canada and Australia, plus the world total. The list was compiled to suit the question of the article; it does not cover all countries.
- Years: in 2000, in 2003, in 2005, in 2010, in 2015, in 2020 and in 2023. For China and the United States, every year in 2000–2023.
- Share: the country's article count divided by the world article count, times one hundred. Each share was computed from the country's and the world's values for the same year.
- Point difference: the difference between the share in two years. We computed the difference from unrounded shares, so it can differ slightly from the difference of the two rounded shares in the table.
- Percentage change: the last-year value minus the first-year value, divided by the first-year value, times one hundred.
- Ranks: the article counts of the thirteen countries were sorted from largest to smallest.
- Distribution of the rise: each country's difference over 2000–2023 was divided by the difference in the world total over the same period.

Every number we calculated is in the evidence file together with its inputs.
